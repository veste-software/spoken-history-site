The Bayeux Tapestry: What It Really Shows

Look closely at this man. Above him, stitched in wool, is one name: Harold. And an arrow seems to strike him in the eye. For many of us, this is how 1066 ends. But around 1729, an artist copied the tapestry for a French scholar, with orders to alter nothing. In his drawing, says the historian Michael Livingston, this man holds a spear. Or at best, a long shaft without feathers. The arrow in the eye first appears in a copy made almost ninety years later. And the British Museum's curators now say the arrow is likely a nineteenth-century addition.

So what does the Bayeux Tapestry really show? As of November 2026, it is on loan from France to the British Museum in London, until July 2027. In the curators' words, it is the only remaining pictorial account of these events. So let's check it against the written sources.

An embroidery, not a tapestry

First, it is not a tapestry at all, but an embroidery: dyed wool, stitched onto linen. The museum in Bayeux gives its length as 68.3 metres. The embroidered band is about 50 centimetres high. 9 pieces of linen. 10 colours of wool, says the Bayeux museum, from three dye plants: woad, madder and weld. Over 600 figures. And only three of them, in the main band, are women.

Did you know? Over 600 figures appear in the main body of the Bayeux Tapestry, but only three of them are women (British Museum).

Who made it?

Who made it, and how many hands? No source tells us. No maker and no patron is named on it. The old name, Queen Matilda's tapestry, rests on a local tradition recorded in the 1700s. The museum in Bayeux says she probably did not make it. Most scholars today think it was made in England, probably in or around Canterbury, because its design draws on Canterbury manuscripts. The British Museum dates it to about 1072 to 1077. And the patron? Most historians think Duke William's half-brother was involved: Odo, Bishop of Bayeux, so prominent in its pictures. Two minor men named in the captions, Wadard and Vital, share their names with men who held land from Odo in Domesday Book. The historian Hugh Thomas still calls Odo the most likely patron, but no longer proven. Others have argued for Queen Edith, King Edward's widow, or a French abbey.

Why did Harold cross the sea?

Now the story. It opens with King Edward of England. Then Earl Harold rides to Bosham, and sails. He lands in the territory of Count Guy of Ponthieu, who seizes him. Why did Harold cross the sea? The tapestry does not say. William of Poitiers, Duke William's chaplain, writing in the 1070s, says King Edward sent him, to confirm by oath that William would be the next king. Eadmer, a monk of Canterbury, says Harold went to free his brother and nephew, hostages in Normandy, and that Edward warned him it would end in harm. And Wace, a canon of Bayeux writing about a century later, gives up. I find it written both the one way and the other, he says.

Did you know? William of Malmesbury, writing in the 1100s, knew the story that King Edward sent Harold, but preferred another: Harold went out from Bosham on a fishing boat for recreation, and a sudden storm drove him onto the coast of Ponthieu (Gesta Regum, Book II, tr. J. A. Giles 1847).

The oath at Bayeux

Harold rides with Duke William to war in Brittany, and, says the caption, drags men out of the sand. Then two captions. Here William came to Bayeux. And: where Harold made an oath to Duke William. What he swore, the tapestry does not say. William of Poitiers does: to work for William's succession, and to hand over Dover castle. But Poitiers puts the oath at Bonneville, before the war in Brittany. A later writer, Orderic Vitalis, says Rouen. The tapestry puts it at Bayeux, Odo's own city. For Hugh Thomas, that is one sign that Odo was behind it.

A funeral, a crown, a comet

Harold comes home to King Edward. And then something odd: the funeral comes first. Edward's body is carried to the church. Only then do we see him on his deathbed, speaking to his faithful men, and then dead. The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle says Edward died on 5 January 1066. Harold succeeded as the king had granted it to him, and was crowned the next day. Beside Harold's throne stands Archbishop Stigand, named in the caption. Norman writers said Stigand consecrated him, a man the pope had condemned. Then: these men marvel at the star. Halley's comet. The Chronicle says it appeared in April. More than three months after the coronation. The tapestry sets it right after. And in the border below, the curators see a ghostly fleet, perhaps a sign of the invasion to come.

Pevensey and a castle dug at Hastings

Here, says the caption, Duke William crossed the sea in a great ship, and came to Pevensey. One version of the Chronicle agrees: Pevensey, on the eve of Saint Michael's mass. 28 September. Then my favourite caption. In our rendering: he ordered that a castle should be dug at Hastings. And below it, men with spades heap up a mound of earth. The Chronicle says it too: they built a castle at the port of Hastings. Later, it says, Bishop Odo and Earl William fitz Osbern built castles widely through this country, and harassed the miserable people.

The battle, and Bishop Odo

The battle. 14 October 1066. Harold's brothers, Leofwine and Gyrth, fall. The Chronicle names them too. Then Bishop Odo, says the caption, holding a staff, gives strength to the boys. Poitiers has Odo among the clergy who were there to fight with prayers.

How did Harold die?

And then the scene we began with. Here King Harold was killed. Under the name Harold stands the man with the arrow. A little further on, a horseman cuts down a falling man. Which one is Harold? The British Museum's curators say historians are unsure: the first, the second, or both. So check the texts, oldest first. The earliest Norman accounts, William of Jumièges and William of Poitiers, have no arrow. Poitiers says only that the king had fallen, and that his body was known by certain marks, not by his face. The first arrow in the eye may come from Amatus, a monk of Monte Cassino, around 1080. But his text survives only in a French translation of the 1300s. In the 1100s, the arrow spreads. William of Malmesbury: it pierced his brain. Henry of Huntingdon: he was pierced in the eye, and then cut down by horsemen. Now the cloth. It was restored in the 1800s, with new wool. And the arrow first appears in the copy drawn by Charles Stothard, between 1816 and 1818. In the late 1970s, the historians Brooks and Walker wrote that only the head and shoulders of the arrow man are original. The arrow is restoration. Yet they still trusted Stothard, and read the tapestry as the earliest account of Harold's death by an arrow. Martin Foys disagrees. Harold, he argues, is the man cut down by the horseman, and restorers turned a spear into the arrow. Near the falling man there is a line of holes, where stitches were taken out. David Bernstein took them for an original arrow. Livingston thinks a restorer made them. So did an arrow kill Harold? We do not know. And the cloth cannot settle it, because the cloth itself was changed.

The lost end, and a hanging that nearly vanished

And the end? The last caption reads: and the English turned in flight. After that, the cloth is damaged, and almost certainly incomplete. Its first sure record is an inventory of Bayeux Cathedral, in 1476: a very long and narrow hanging, showing the conquest of England. In 1792, says a local tradition, it was nearly cut up to cover soldiers' carts. A local lawyer saved it. Charles Stothard, the man who drew the arrow, took two small pieces home. For years his wife was blamed. Wrongly, says the V&A. And in 1886, 35 women in England finished a full-size copy. In it, one naked man in the border wears underpants. Not their idea, says Reading Museum. They copied photographs already cleaned up by the men at the South Kensington Museum.

Did you know? The story went that Mrs Stothard had snatched the piece from Bayeux, and the tapestry's curator was apparently in the habit of telling visitors that she had confessed on her deathbed. Eliza Stothard was still alive, aged 91, when she learned of it in 1881. The fragment, bought by the South Kensington Museum in 1864, was returned to Bayeux on 14 August 1872 (V&A blog, N. Smith 2013; Fowke 1898).

Did you know? Twice before, the hanging was set to be lent to London, to the V&A: for the coronation of 1953 and for the 900th anniversary of the battle in 1966. Both loans fell through (V&A blog, N. Smith 2013).

What we really have

So, what do we really have? A picture story made within about ten years of the conquest, the British Museum thinks. Probably by English hands, probably for a Norman patron. Where we can check it, it often agrees with the Chronicle. Where it differs, it is telling: the oath at Bayeux, the comet straight after the crown. And at its most famous moment, much of what we see was stitched in the 1800s.

Verdict

So that is what the Bayeux Tapestry really shows. Not a photograph of 1066, but a story stitched in wool, with choices of its own. And a repaired one.

Quick answers

Is the Bayeux Tapestry really a tapestry?

No. It is not a tapestry at all, but an embroidery: dyed wool, stitched onto linen. The museum in Bayeux gives its length as 68.3 metres, and the embroidered band is about 50 centimetres high. It is made of nine pieces of linen, with ten colours of wool, says the Bayeux museum, from three dye plants: woad, madder and weld. Over 600 figures appear in it, and only three of them, in the main band, are women.

Who made the Bayeux Tapestry?

No source tells us, and no maker and no patron is named on it. The old name, Queen Matilda's tapestry, rests on a local tradition recorded in the 1700s, and the museum in Bayeux says she probably did not make it. Most scholars today think it was made in England, probably in or around Canterbury, because its design draws on Canterbury manuscripts; the British Museum dates it to about 1072 to 1077. Most historians think Duke William's half-brother Odo, Bishop of Bayeux, was involved. The historian Hugh Thomas still calls Odo the most likely patron, but no longer proven; others have argued for Queen Edith, King Edward's widow, or a French abbey.

Was King Harold killed by an arrow in the eye?

We do not know. The British Museum's curators say historians are unsure whether Harold is the man with the arrow, the man cut down by a horseman a little further on, or both. The earliest Norman accounts, William of Jumièges and William of Poitiers, have no arrow. The first arrow in the eye may come from Amatus, a monk of Monte Cassino, around 1080, but his text survives only in a French translation of the 1300s. In the 1100s the arrow spreads: William of Malmesbury says it pierced his brain, and Henry of Huntingdon that he was pierced in the eye and then cut down by horsemen. The cloth cannot settle it, because the cloth itself was changed.

Was the arrow in Harold's eye added to the tapestry later?

The British Museum's curators now say the arrow is likely a nineteenth-century addition. Around 1729 an artist copied the tapestry for a French scholar, with orders to alter nothing, and in his drawing, says the historian Michael Livingston, this man holds a spear, or at best a long shaft without feathers. The arrow first appears in the copy drawn by Charles Stothard between 1816 and 1818. The cloth was restored in the 1800s with new wool, and in the late 1970s the historians Brooks and Walker wrote that only the head and shoulders of the arrow man are original. Scholars still disagree about what was there before.

Why did Harold cross the sea before 1066?

The tapestry does not say. William of Poitiers, Duke William's chaplain, writing in the 1070s, says King Edward sent him, to confirm by oath that William would be the next king. Eadmer, a monk of Canterbury, says Harold went to free his brother and nephew, hostages in Normandy, and that Edward warned him it would end in harm. And Wace, a canon of Bayeux writing about a century later, gives up: "I find it written both the one way and the other", he says.

Where it happened

  • Bayeux : Odo's city, where the tapestry puts Harold's oath; its first sure record is an inventory of Bayeux Cathedral in 1476, and the museum in Bayeux keeps it.
  • Bosham : Earl Harold rides to Bosham and sails (captions HIC HAROLD MARE NAVIGAVIT).
  • Ponthieu : Count Guy's territory, where Harold landed and was seized. A region, not a town: the point only marks it near the Somme estuary.
  • Bonneville-sur-Touques : Where William of Poitiers puts Harold's oath, before the war in Brittany. The point is the modern village.
  • Rouen : Where Orderic Vitalis, a later writer, puts Harold's oath.
  • Pevensey (A.D. 1066): Duke William came to Pevensey (the caption); one version of the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle says on the eve of Saint Michael's mass, 28 September 1066. The point is the modern village.
  • Hastings (A.D. 1066): The castle dug at Hastings (caption and Chronicle). The point is the modern town; the site of the battle of 14 October 1066 is not discussed in the video.
  • Canterbury : Most scholars think the hanging was made in England, probably in or around Canterbury, because its design draws on Canterbury manuscripts.
  • British Museum, London (A.D. 2026): As of November 2026, the hanging is on loan from France to the British Museum, until July 2027.
  • Reading Museum (A.D. 1886): Home of the full-size Victorian copy finished in 1886 by thirty-five women (a replica, not the original).

    Transcript

    Read the full narration

    The Bayeux Tapestry. What it really shows, and what was added. Look closely at this man. Above him, stitched in wool, is one name: Harold. And an arrow seems to strike him in the eye. For many of us, this is how 1066 ends. But around 1729, an artist copied the tapestry for a French scholar, with orders to alter nothing. In his drawing, says the historian Michael Livingston, this man holds a spear. Or at best, a long shaft without feathers. The arrow in the eye first appears in a copy made almost ninety years later. And the British Museum's curators now say the arrow is likely a nineteenth-century addition. So what does the Bayeux Tapestry really show? As of November 2026, it is on loan from France to the British Museum in London, until July 2027. In the curators' words, it is the only remaining pictorial account of these events. So let's check it against the written sources. First, it is not a tapestry at all, but an embroidery: dyed wool, stitched onto linen. The museum in Bayeux gives its length as 68.3 metres. The embroidered band is about 50 centimetres high. 9 pieces of linen. 10 colours of wool, says the Bayeux museum, from three dye plants: woad, madder and weld. Over 600 figures. And only three of them, in the main band, are women. Who made it, and how many hands? No source tells us. No maker and no patron is named on it. The old name, Queen Matilda's tapestry, rests on a local tradition recorded in the 1700s. The museum in Bayeux says she probably did not make it. Most scholars today think it was made in England, probably in or around Canterbury, because its design draws on Canterbury manuscripts. The British Museum dates it to about 1072 to 1077. And the patron? Most historians think Duke William's half-brother was involved: Odo, Bishop of Bayeux, so prominent in its pictures. Two minor men named in the captions, Wadard and Vital, share their names with men who held land from Odo in Domesday Book. The historian Hugh Thomas still calls Odo the most likely patron, but no longer proven. Others have argued for Queen Edith, King Edward's widow, or a French abbey. Now the story. It opens with King Edward of England. Then Earl Harold rides to Bosham, and sails. He lands in the territory of Count Guy of Ponthieu, who seizes him. Why did Harold cross the sea? The tapestry does not say. William of Poitiers, Duke William's chaplain, writing in the 1070s, says King Edward sent him, to confirm by oath that William would be the next king. Eadmer, a monk of Canterbury, says Harold went to free his brother and nephew, hostages in Normandy, and that Edward warned him it would end in harm. And Wace, a canon of Bayeux writing about a century later, gives up. I find it written both the one way and the other, he says. Harold rides with Duke William to war in Brittany, and, says the caption, drags men out of the sand. Then two captions. Here William came to Bayeux. And: where Harold made an oath to Duke William. What he swore, the tapestry does not say. William of Poitiers does: to work for William's succession, and to hand over Dover castle. But Poitiers puts the oath at Bonneville, before the war in Brittany. A later writer, Orderic Vitalis, says Rouen. The tapestry puts it at Bayeux, Odo's own city. For Hugh Thomas, that is one sign that Odo was behind it. Harold comes home to King Edward. And then something odd: the funeral comes first. Edward's body is carried to the church. Only then do we see him on his deathbed, speaking to his faithful men, and then dead. The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle says Edward died on 5 January 1066. Harold succeeded as the king had granted it to him, and was crowned the next day. Beside Harold's throne stands Archbishop Stigand, named in the caption. Norman writers said Stigand consecrated him, a man the pope had condemned. Then: these men marvel at the star. Halley's comet. The Chronicle says it appeared in April. More than three months after the coronation. The tapestry sets it right after. And in the border below, the curators see a ghostly fleet, perhaps a sign of the invasion to come. If you are enjoying this type of content, please don't forget to subscribe. Here, says the caption, Duke William crossed the sea in a great ship, and came to Pevensey. One version of the Chronicle agrees: Pevensey, on the eve of Saint Michael's mass. 28 September. Then my favourite caption. In our rendering: he ordered that a castle should be dug at Hastings. And below it, men with spades heap up a mound of earth. The Chronicle says it too: they built a castle at the port of Hastings. Later, it says, Bishop Odo and Earl William fitz Osbern built castles widely through this country, and harassed the miserable people. The battle. 14 October 1066. Harold's brothers, Leofwine and Gyrth, fall. The Chronicle names them too. Then Bishop Odo, says the caption, holding a staff, gives strength to the boys. Poitiers has Odo among the clergy who were there to fight with prayers. And then the scene we began with. Here King Harold was killed. Under the name Harold stands the man with the arrow. A little further on, a horseman cuts down a falling man. Which one is Harold? The British Museum's curators say historians are unsure: the first, the second, or both. So check the texts, oldest first. The earliest Norman accounts, William of Jumièges and William of Poitiers, have no arrow. Poitiers says only that the king had fallen, and that his body was known by certain marks, not by his face. The first arrow in the eye may come from Amatus, a monk of Monte Cassino, around 1080. But his text survives only in a French translation of the 1300s. In the 1100s, the arrow spreads. William of Malmesbury: it pierced his brain. Henry of Huntingdon: he was pierced in the eye, and then cut down by horsemen. Now the cloth. It was restored in the 1800s, with new wool. And the arrow first appears in the copy drawn by Charles Stothard, between 1816 and 1818. In the late 1970s, the historians Brooks and Walker wrote that only the head and shoulders of the arrow man are original. The arrow is restoration. Yet they still trusted Stothard, and read the tapestry as the earliest account of Harold's death by an arrow. Martin Foys disagrees. Harold, he argues, is the man cut down by the horseman, and restorers turned a spear into the arrow. Near the falling man there is a line of holes, where stitches were taken out. David Bernstein took them for an original arrow. Livingston thinks a restorer made them. So did an arrow kill Harold? We do not know. And the cloth cannot settle it, because the cloth itself was changed. And the end? The last caption reads: and the English turned in flight. After that, the cloth is damaged, and almost certainly incomplete. Its first sure record is an inventory of Bayeux Cathedral, in 1476: a very long and narrow hanging, showing the conquest of England. In 1792, says a local tradition, it was nearly cut up to cover soldiers' carts. A local lawyer saved it. Charles Stothard, the man who drew the arrow, took two small pieces home. For years his wife was blamed. Wrongly, says the V&A. And in 1886, 35 women in England finished a full-size copy. In it, one naked man in the border wears underpants. Not their idea, says Reading Museum. They copied photographs already cleaned up by the men at the South Kensington Museum. So, what do we really have? A picture story made within about ten years of the conquest, the British Museum thinks. Probably by English hands, probably for a Norman patron. Where we can check it, it often agrees with the Chronicle. Where it differs, it is telling: the oath at Bayeux, the comet straight after the crown. And at its most famous moment, much of what we see was stitched in the 1800s. So that is what the Bayeux Tapestry really shows. Not a photograph of 1066, but a story stitched in wool, with choices of its own. And a repaired one. Two questions for you. Is Harold the man with the arrow, the falling man, or both?
