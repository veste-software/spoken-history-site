Roman Helmets: From Montefortino to Imperial Gallic

57 B.C. Northern Gaul, beside a river the Romans called the Sabis. Six of Caesar's legions have marched in, and the men are digging the defences of their camp. Then the Nervii burst out of the woods. So fast, Caesar writes, that they seemed to be at the forest edge, in the river, and at close quarters, almost at the same moment. In the Loeb translation: the time was so short, the temper of the enemy so ready for conflict, that there was no space not only to fit badges in their places, but even to put on helmets. Men fought where they stood, under the first standard they saw.

So while they dug, their helmets were not on their heads. And when the attack came, there was no time to put them on. The Roman helmet. Last time I asked: the helmets, or the scutum? Here are the helmets. Today: what it was, what has been dug up, and what the texts really say. And the first surprise: not one of the type names on the museum labels is Roman.

Names Rome never used

In Latin, a helmet was a galea, or a cassis. A writer of the seventh century A.D., Isidore of Seville, says the cassis was made of metal plate, and the galea of leather. But Roman writers call bronze helmets galea too. The names in museums, Montefortino, Coolus, Hagenau, Weisenau, are modern. Most of them are places where helmets were found. Montefortino is a Celtic cemetery near Ancona, in Italy, dug in the 1890s. Coolus is on the river Marne, in France, where helmets of this kind were found, one of them dredged from the river. Hagenau is a town in Alsace whose museum holds a helmet from the Rhine. Weisenau is a district of Mainz. The scholar Couissin sorted most of them in 1926. In 1975 the British armour expert Russell Robinson made a scheme of his own, with lettered sub-types. He split the iron helmets into Imperial Gallic and Imperial Italic, after where he thought they were made. German books mostly call the same helmets Weisenau. The archaeologist Mike Bishop says Robinson's system has never been wholly satisfactory. So: useful labels. But they are ours, not Rome's.

Montefortino: the oldest family

The oldest family is the one we call Montefortino. A tall bronze bowl, a knob on top, a short lip at the back over the neck, and hinged plates for the cheeks. Where did it come from? Here scholars disagree. John Paddock, who wrote his doctoral thesis on these helmets, says there can be no doubt it came from the Celts, whose iron helmets reached Italy around 400 B.C. Janka Istenič, who published the finds from Slovenia, traces it to bronze helmets made in Etruria in the fourth century B.C. Either way, by the third century B.C., Roman soldiers were wearing it. And here we have a witness. Polybius, a Greek who lived in Rome in the second century B.C., says the heavy infantry wear a bronze helmet. On top, a circle of feathers, with three upright purple or black feathers, about a cubit tall. That is about 45 centimetres. Why? In Paton's translation: to make every man look twice his real height.

Did you know? Polybius says the hastati wore three upright feathers about a cubit tall, to make every man look twice his real height (Polybius 6.23, translated by W. R. Paton).

Mass production, and a helmet from Sisak

About 150 of these helmets survive, Paddock counted. A study from 2021 says about 200. And the late ones look rushed. Paddock describes hammer marks left rough, one rivet where there used to be two, and knobs made separately and soldered on, some of them off-centre. He links that to the time of the army reforms credited to Marius, and to arming men in bulk. Ivan Radman-Livaja thinks the state may have started equipping soldiers earlier. One of these late helmets was dredged from the river Kupa, near Sisak in Croatia. Its bronze is hardly ever more than a millimetre thick, and it weighs 450 grams. Other helmets of the period weigh up to about 1.2 kilos. It seems to have taken a hard blow on the top. The damage was patched with lead, and the broken knob replaced by a clumsy new one. Perhaps in the field, says Radman-Livaja, conceivably by the owner himself. Who he was, we don't know.

Did you know? A Republican helmet dredged from the Kupa at Sisak weighs only 450 grams. It seems to have taken a heavy blow to the top and was patched with lead and given a clumsy new knob, perhaps in the field, perhaps by its owner (Radman-Livaja 2022).

Coolus: the Gallic cap

Next, the Coolus. A low, round bronze cap with a short neck guard, and no knob at all. It is Celtic in origin, says Paddock. It appears in the third century B.C., and turns up with the Roman army only in the first century B.C. Was it ever made in Roman workshops? Still debated. Radman-Livaja thinks it safer to assume the Gauls made it, and that Caesar's troops used it to some extent. A ship that sank off the south coast of France around 70 B.C. carried both kinds: a Coolus and a Montefortino, in use side by side. So which helmets lay beside the trenches at the Sabis? We don't know. Many scholars think Caesar's men wore some like these in Gaul. And one Coolus helmet from England, from the middle of the first century B.C., had a very different end. It was set upside down in a pit, and the burnt bones of a human being were placed inside it. A helmet, used as an urn.

Did you know? A Coolus helmet of the mid-first century B.C. found in England was set upside down in a pit with the burnt bones of a human being placed inside: a helmet used as an urn (British Museum report, Portable Antiquities Scheme 526999).

Imperial Gallic: iron, eyebrows, neck guards

Then, under Augustus, the Romans begin to wear mostly iron helmets, alongside the bronze ones. A deep guard over the neck. A bar across the brow. Guards over the ears. Broad cheek pieces. And on the front, two curved ridges beaten into the metal, like eyebrows. Robinson pointed to Celtic iron helmets, of the kind found at Port, in Switzerland. Peter Connolly agreed that at least one early Roman helmet is clearly derived from them. Astrid Bongartz, in a doctoral thesis from Cologne, goes further: the Romans took the Celtic helmet over after what they saw in the Gallic War, and by the time of Augustus it had become the Weisenau type. Why the bigger neck guard, and the brow bar? Robinson saw an answer to the slashing swords of Rome's enemies. Paddock disagreed. Rome had been facing those swords for three hundred years. He wondered whether it had more to do with a smaller shield. The evidence does not settle it.

Names punched into a neck guard

Now look closely at this neck guard. A bronze helmet of the Hagenau kind, dredged from the Rhine at Hönnepel, near Kalkar, in Germany. Punched into it, dot by dot, are names. The century of Amilisius: Tiberius Calventius. The century of Satrius: Publius Messius. A man of the seventh cohort. Eight inscriptions in all. From the way the names are written, it is dated before A.D. 30. Another, from the Thames or the Walbrook in London, now in the British Museum, carries the names of four owners, in at least two centuries, says the standard edition of Roman inscriptions from Britain. Why mark a helmet? Perhaps to get your own, well-fitting one back after weapons were handed in. Bongartz thinks it was not compulsory: each man decided for himself. Several names need not always mean several owners, she warns. But on the London helmet, the editors count four.

Did you know? Roman soldiers punched their names into their helmets. The Hoennepel helmet has eight inscriptions; the Thames helmet in the British Museum names four owners in at least two centuries.

What was inside

What was inside? A helmet only works with a padded lining, says Bishop. At Hod Hill, in Dorset, the hill fort from our scorpion episode, part of a helmet from the first century A.D. carries traces of coarse woven fabric, probably linen. At Newstead, in Scotland, James Curle found thick padding inside an iron cavalry helmet with a face mask. It seemed to be wool, fastened to the metal with a resinous substance that went sticky in hot water. Leather too? Paddock rejects it. Bongartz will not rule it out. What shape the linings took, nobody knows.

Bars across the top

Early in the 2nd century A.D., something new appears on some helmets: iron bars fixed across the top of the bowl. This one was found in a legion's fortress at Berzovia, in Romania. So it was lost between A.D. 101 and about 119. The usual answer is the Dacian falx, a curved, scythe-like blade. Bishop says the bars were probably added at first to counter it, because it could cut through the mild steel of a Roman helmet. Probably. The main evidence is the timing. No ancient text we know of says why. And there was a side effect. With bars across the top, Bongartz notes, there was no way to fix a crest.

Crests: films and sources

Which brings us to crests. In many films, every legionary wears one. The texts say less. Polybius has his feathers. Varro, in the 1st century B.C., says feathers on helmets marked out distinguished soldiers. Vegetius says centurions wore crests set crosswise, and silvered, so their men could find them. But he wrote at the end of the fourth century A.D. or later, about an army long gone. What the ground gives us is the fittings. Little bronze holders like these, recorded in Suffolk, once soldered to the top of a helmet. The crests themselves are lost.

What we really have

So, what do we really have? Hundreds of real helmets, from rivers, graves, shipwrecks and forts. A few lines in Polybius, Caesar, Varro, and later writers. Names, punched into bronze. A patch of lead on a dented bowl. What we don't have: a Roman name for any of these types, or a sure answer to why they changed.

Verdict, and what comes next

So that's the Roman helmet. Almost every type, says Bishop, was taken from helmets of Rome's neighbours in the north-west. And every type is named by us, not by them.

Questions for you: Why do you think a Roman soldier punched his name into his helmet: to get his own back, or something more? The bars across the top: an answer to the Dacian falx, or simply a better helmet? And what next: the scutum, or the Roman fort?

Quick answers

What was a Roman helmet called in Latin?

In Latin a helmet was a galea or a cassis. Isidore of Seville, writing in the seventh century A.D., says the cassis was made of metal plate and the galea of leather, but Roman writers also call bronze helmets galea.

Are Montefortino, Coolus and Imperial Gallic Roman names?

No. They are modern labels, mostly taken from places where helmets were found. The scholar Couissin sorted most of them in 1926; in 1975 Russell Robinson made his own scheme with lettered sub-types and named Imperial Gallic and Imperial Italic after where he thought they were made. German books mostly call the same iron helmets Weisenau.

Where did the Montefortino helmet come from?

Scholars disagree. John Paddock says there can be no doubt it came from the Celts, whose iron helmets reached Italy around 400 B.C.; Janka Istenič traces it to bronze helmets made in Etruria in the fourth century B.C. By the third century B.C. Roman soldiers were wearing it.

Did Roman soldiers put their names on their helmets?

Yes. A bronze helmet dredged from the Rhine at Hoennepel, near Kalkar, carries eight inscriptions naming centuries and soldiers, and is dated before A.D. 30 from the way the names are written. A helmet from the Thames or the Walbrook in London, now in the British Museum, names four owners in at least two centuries.

Why do some Roman helmets have bars across the top?

Iron cross-bars appear on some helmets early in the second century A.D. Mike Bishop says they were probably added at first to counter the Dacian falx; the main evidence is the timing, and no ancient text we know of says why.

Where it happened

  • The river Sabis (Sambre) (57 B.C.): Caesar's battle against the Nervii (Gallic War 2.19 to 2.21); the exact site is not known.
  • Montefortino, near Ancona, Italy (350 B.C.): The Celtic cemetery that gave the Montefortino type its name.
  • Coolus, Marne, France (100 B.C.): Helmets found here, one of them dredged from the Marne, gave the Coolus type its name.
  • Sisak (Siscia), Croatia (100 B.C.): A late Republican helmet dredged from the Kupa, apparently dented and patched with lead.
  • Kalkar-Hoennepel, Germany (A.D. 20): A bronze helmet from the Rhine with eight owners' inscriptions, dated before A.D. 30.
  • London (Thames or Walbrook) (A.D. 50): The Thames Coolus helmet in the British Museum, with four owners' names (RIB 2425.2).
  • Hod Hill, Dorset (A.D. 45): Part of a helmet with traces of a coarse woven lining, probably linen.
  • Newstead (Trimontium), Scotland (A.D. 90): James Curle found wool padding glued inside an iron cavalry helmet with a face mask.
  • Berzovia (Berzobis), Romania (A.D. 110): An iron helmet with cross-bars, lost between A.D. 101 and about 119.
  • Madrague de Giens, France (70 B.C.): A shipwreck of about 70 B.C. with a Coolus and a Montefortino helmet.

    Roman Helmets. From Montefortino to Imperial Gallic. 57 B.C. Northern Gaul, beside a river the Romans called the Sabis. Six of Caesar's legions have marched in, and the men are digging the defences of their camp. Then the Nervii burst out of the woods. So fast, Caesar writes, that they seemed to be at the forest edge, in the river, and at close quarters, almost at the same moment. In the Loeb translation: the time was so short, the temper of the enemy so ready for conflict, that there was no space not only to fit badges in their places, but even to put on helmets. Men fought where they stood, under the first standard they saw. So while they dug, their helmets were not on their heads. And when the attack came, there was no time to put them on. The Roman helmet. Last time I asked: the helmets, or the scutum? Here are the helmets. Today: what it was, what has been dug up, and what the texts really say. And the first surprise: not one of the type names on the museum labels is Roman. In Latin, a helmet was a galea, or a cassis. A writer of the seventh century A.D., Isidore of Seville, says the cassis was made of metal plate, and the galea of leather. But Roman writers call bronze helmets galea too. The names in museums, Montefortino, Coolus, Hagenau, Weisenau, are modern. Most of them are places where helmets were found. Montefortino is a Celtic cemetery near Ancona, in Italy, dug in the 1890s. Coolus is on the river Marne, in France, where helmets of this kind were found, one of them dredged from the river. Hagenau is a town in Alsace whose museum holds a helmet from the Rhine. Weisenau is a district of Mainz. The scholar Couissin sorted most of them in 1926. In 1975 the British armour expert Russell Robinson made a scheme of his own, with lettered sub-types. He split the iron helmets into Imperial Gallic and Imperial Italic, after where he thought they were made. German books mostly call the same helmets Weisenau. The archaeologist Mike Bishop says Robinson's system has never been wholly satisfactory. So: useful labels. But they are ours, not Rome's. The oldest family is the one we call Montefortino. A tall bronze bowl, a knob on top, a short lip at the back over the neck, and hinged plates for the cheeks. Where did it come from? Here scholars disagree. John Paddock, who wrote his doctoral thesis on these helmets, says there can be no doubt it came from the Celts, whose iron helmets reached Italy around 400 B.C. Janka Istenič, who published the finds from Slovenia, traces it to bronze helmets made in Etruria in the fourth century B.C. Either way, by the third century B.C., Roman soldiers were wearing it. And here we have a witness. Polybius, a Greek who lived in Rome in the second century B.C., says the heavy infantry wear a bronze helmet. On top, a circle of feathers, with three upright purple or black feathers, about a cubit tall. That is about 45 centimetres. Why? In Paton's translation: to make every man look twice his real height. About 150 of these helmets survive, Paddock counted. A study from 2021 says about 200. And the late ones look rushed. Paddock describes hammer marks left rough, one rivet where there used to be two, and knobs made separately and soldered on, some of them off-centre. He links that to the time of the army reforms credited to Marius, and to arming men in bulk. Ivan Radman-Livaja thinks the state may have started equipping soldiers earlier. One of these late helmets was dredged from the river Kupa, near Sisak in Croatia. Its bronze is hardly ever more than a millimetre thick, and it weighs 450 grams. Other helmets of the period weigh up to about 1.2 kilos. It seems to have taken a hard blow on the top. The damage was patched with lead, and the broken knob replaced by a clumsy new one. Perhaps in the field, says Radman-Livaja, conceivably by the owner himself. Who he was, we don't know. Next, the Coolus. A low, round bronze cap with a short neck guard, and no knob at all. It is Celtic in origin, says Paddock. It appears in the third century B.C., and turns up with the Roman army only in the first century B.C. Was it ever made in Roman workshops? Still debated. Radman-Livaja thinks it safer to assume the Gauls made it, and that Caesar's troops used it to some extent. A ship that sank off the south coast of France around 70 B.C. carried both kinds: a Coolus and a Montefortino, in use side by side. So which helmets lay beside the trenches at the Sabis? We don't know. Many scholars think Caesar's men wore some like these in Gaul. And one Coolus helmet from England, from the middle of the first century B.C., had a very different end. It was set upside down in a pit, and the burnt bones of a human being were placed inside it. A helmet, used as an urn. Then, under Augustus, the Romans begin to wear mostly iron helmets, alongside the bronze ones. A deep guard over the neck. A bar across the brow. Guards over the ears. Broad cheek pieces. And on the front, two curved ridges beaten into the metal, like eyebrows. Robinson pointed to Celtic iron helmets, of the kind found at Port, in Switzerland. Peter Connolly agreed that at least one early Roman helmet is clearly derived from them. Astrid Bongartz, in a doctoral thesis from Cologne, goes further: the Romans took the Celtic helmet over after what they saw in the Gallic War, and by the time of Augustus it had become the Weisenau type. Why the bigger neck guard, and the brow bar? Robinson saw an answer to the slashing swords of Rome's enemies. Paddock disagreed. Rome had been facing those swords for three hundred years. He wondered whether it had more to do with a smaller shield. The evidence does not settle it. Now look closely at this neck guard. A bronze helmet of the Hagenau kind, dredged from the Rhine at Hönnepel, near Kalkar, in Germany. Punched into it, dot by dot, are names. The century of Amilisius: Tiberius Calventius. The century of Satrius: Publius Messius. A man of the seventh cohort. Eight inscriptions in all. From the way the names are written, it is dated before A.D. 30. Another, from the Thames or the Walbrook in London, now in the British Museum, carries the names of four owners, in at least two centuries, says the standard edition of Roman inscriptions from Britain. Why mark a helmet? Perhaps to get your own, well-fitting one back after weapons were handed in. Bongartz thinks it was not compulsory: each man decided for himself. Several names need not always mean several owners, she warns. But on the London helmet, the editors count four. If you are enjoying this type of content, please don't forget to subscribe. What was inside? A helmet only works with a padded lining, says Bishop. At Hod Hill, in Dorset, the hill fort from our scorpion episode, part of a helmet from the first century A.D. carries traces of coarse woven fabric, probably linen. At Newstead, in Scotland, James Curle found thick padding inside an iron cavalry helmet with a face mask. It seemed to be wool, fastened to the metal with a resinous substance that went sticky in hot water. Leather too? Paddock rejects it. Bongartz will not rule it out. What shape the linings took, nobody knows. Early in the 2nd century A.D., something new appears on some helmets: iron bars fixed across the top of the bowl. This one was found in a legion's fortress at Berzovia, in Romania. So it was lost between A.D. 101 and about 119. The usual answer is the Dacian falx, a curved, scythe-like blade. Bishop says the bars were probably added at first to counter it, because it could cut through the mild steel of a Roman helmet. Probably. The main evidence is the timing. No ancient text we know of says why. And there was a side effect. With bars across the top, Bongartz notes, there was no way to fix a crest. Which brings us to crests. In many films, every legionary wears one. The texts say less. Polybius has his feathers. Varro, in the 1st century B.C., says feathers on helmets marked out distinguished soldiers. Vegetius says centurions wore crests set crosswise, and silvered, so their men could find them. But he wrote at the end of the fourth century A.D. or later, about an army long gone. What the ground gives us is the fittings. Little bronze holders like these, recorded in Suffolk, once soldered to the top of a helmet. The crests themselves are lost. So, what do we really have? Hundreds of real helmets, from rivers, graves, shipwrecks and forts. A few lines in Polybius, Caesar, Varro, and later writers. Names, punched into bronze. A patch of lead on a dented bowl. What we don't have: a Roman name for any of these types, or a sure answer to why they changed. So that's the Roman helmet. Almost every type, says Bishop, was taken from helmets of Rome's neighbours in the north-west. And every type is named by us, not by them. Why do you think a soldier punched his name into his helmet? To get his own back, or something more? Those bars across the top: an answer to the Dacian falx, or simply a better helmet? And what should I take apart next? The scutum, or the Roman fort?
