One of the first pictures of a gun in Europe was painted in England, around 1326. And it is shooting an arrow. A man in armour holds a rod to a fat, vase-shaped pot on a trestle. Out of its mouth comes a big bolt, aimed at a door. It sits in a book made for the young Edward III. The text around it never mentions guns. The Royal Armouries built a copy and fired it. Its bolt of oak, with a steel tip, flew several hundred metres.

How this timeline works

Why would a gun shoot an arrow? Because new weapons rarely wiped out the old ones. They borrowed from them, and piled up. Today: how the power behind a missile changed. Muscle. A machine that holds the pull. Twisted sinew. Gravity. And fire. The rule for this timeline: each weapon sits at its earliest dated evidence that survives. A find, a text, or a picture. Not the day it was invented. Nobody knows that day. And wood and cord rot, so what survives is often stone or bronze.

Muscle: the oldest bows and slings

Step one: muscle. Bow and sling. In Sibudu Cave, in South Africa, the archaeologists Marlize Lombard and Laurel Phillipson argue that small stone points tipped arrows, about 64,000 years ago. A clue, not a bow. Some of Europe's oldest real bows came out of a bog at Holmegaard, in Denmark. Five bows, from about 7000 B.C. They are made of elm. Yew, says the National Museum of Denmark, did not grow there yet. The Iceman, who died in the Alps over 5,300 years ago, carried a yew bow he had not finished. His quiver held 14 arrows. Only two were ready to shoot. The sling is harder to date. Two slings of plaited linen were packed into the tomb of Tutankhamun. And Roman soldiers cast sling bullets of lead, some with words. One, from the siege of Perugia, says: You're starving, and you're hiding it from me.

Did you know? The Iceman's quiver held fourteen arrows, but only two were finished and ready to shoot, and his 1.82 metre yew bow was not finished either (South Tyrol Museum of Archaeology).

Syracuse, 399 BC: the first catapult

Step two: a machine that holds the pull for you. Syracuse, in Sicily. By the dating of the historian Diodorus, 399 B.C. Dionysius, its ruler, is arming for war with Carthage. He brings in craftsmen from Italy, Greece, even Carthaginian land, with high wages. Temple porches, gymnasiums, the colonnades of the market: all, Diodorus says, were full of workers. There were prizes for the best. Dionysius went round every day, and took the keenest to dinner. Then comes the line: the catapult was invented at that time, in Syracuse. Soon after, at the siege of Motya, his bolt-shooters fired from the shore. The weapon caused great terror, Diodorus writes, because it was new. And beside them, on the ships, stood archers and slingers. The new had joined the old. What was that first catapult? Diodorus does not describe it, and he wrote more than three centuries later. Many scholars, Tracey Rihll among them, think it was a large bow, mounted with a release: the belly bow. Duncan Campbell warns that the usual story rests on working hypotheses.

Did you know? By Diodorus's account the first catapult came out of a paid competition: Dionysius of Syracuse hired craftsmen at high wages, set prizes for those judged best, went round the workers every day and took the keenest to dine with him (Diodorus 14.41 to 14.42; our translation).

The crossbow in China

China took its own road. A team studying the Terracotta Army writes that trigger-fired crossbows came in the Warring States period, and needed less skill and strength than a bow. From one pit alone, they studied 216 bronze triggers, made in batches. The wood has rotted. The triggers are all that remain.

Twisted sinew: torsion artillery

Step three: twisted sinew. Scholars following Eric Marsden put the next change around the middle of the 4th century B.C. The bow gave way to springs: thick bundles of twisted cord. The Roman engineer Vitruvius says the cords were of sinew, or of hair. Above all, women's hair. And the whole machine was sized from one number. The hole the spring passed through. For a bolt-shooter, Vitruvius makes that hole one ninth of the bolt's length, and the other parts follow from the hole. Rome's scorpion came from this line. So did the onager, named, the late Roman writer Ammianus says, after a wild ass that kicks stones at hunters.

Did you know? Ancient artillery ran on hair. Vitruvius says the springs were ropes of hair, above all women's hair, or of sinew (On Architecture 10.11.2; our translation).

Gravity: the trebuchet

Step four: gravity. It starts with people pulling. The trebuchet came from China. A long beam on an axle, a sling at one end, ropes at the other. A Chinese military manual of the 11th century lists the crews. For the largest machine, 250 men pulling. At Thessalonica, in 586 or 597, scholars still argue which, a bishop described the machines of the Avars. Lengths of wood, like the beams of a large house, he wrote, and these had slings. Then the crew was replaced by a falling weight. The earliest known drawing of a counterweight machine is in a treatise written for Saladin. The historian Michael Fulton dates it to about 1180. In 1304, Edward I had his Warwolf at Stirling, almost certainly a trebuchet. He paid for a window, so the queen and her ladies could watch the assault.

Fire: the earliest gunpowder formula

Step five: fire. China, again. That same manual, of 1044, holds what the historian Tonio Andrade calls the earliest known written formula for gunpowder. Saltpetre, sulphur and charcoal, with pitch, oil and wax. For fire balls, not guns. And the manual says how to deliver one: shoot it from a trebuchet. The newest power rode on the old one. Then came the tube: the fire lance, gunpowder in a tube on a pole. Perhaps its earliest certain record, Andrade writes, is from a battle in 1132.

Did you know? The earliest known written formula for gunpowder was not for a gun. It was for fire balls, and the manual of 1044 says to shoot them from a trebuchet (Wujing Zongyao, dated by Tonio Andrade; our reading of the Chinese).

The first guns, China and Europe

Metal guns followed. A small bronze gun found at Xanadu, the Mongol emperors' summer capital, carries a date that matches 1298. And a serial number. Andrade calls it perhaps the oldest surviving gun whose date is beyond doubt. Guns that may be older are dated only by where they lay. In Europe, the written record begins in Florence. On 11 February 1326, the city ordered iron balls, and cannons of metal. The Italian scholar Sandro Tiberini finds no European record of guns that holds up from before that year. That year or the next, in England, came our gun with the arrow. And a second book that Walter of Milemete prepared for the king has another gun, in borders left mostly unfinished. Was the arrow the artist's fancy? The written record has guns shooting arrows too. In July 1338, a French letter records an iron pot gun sent from Rouen, with 48 arrows, a pound of saltpetre and half a pound of sulphur.

Did you know? The Xanadu gun is small, 35 centimetres long and 6.2 kilograms, and besides its date it carries a serial number (Andrade 2022; China Maritime Museum).

Did guns replace bows?

So did guns replace the old weapons? Not for a long time. In 1521, at the siege of Tenochtitlan, Cortés ran short of ammunition. He agreed to build a trebuchet. At its first shot, write the historian Paul Chevedden and his co-authors, the stone went straight up, came back down, and smashed it.

Did you know? When Cortés had a trebuchet built at Tenochtitlan in 1521, its first stone went straight up, came back down and smashed the machine (Chevedden, Eigenbrod, Foley and Soedel, Scientific American, 1995).

The Mary Rose, 1545

The Mary Rose, Henry VIII's warship. She sank in 1545. Her lists give 91 guns, 76 of them iron. Most of her bronze guns carry the maker's name and a date. Four add two letters: HI. Henricus Invictissimus. Henry most invincible. And her lists give 250 longbows. 172 came up from the wreck. Among her weapons were fire darts, two metres long. The Mary Rose Trust says they were launched from the fighting tops, or shot from a gun. The historian Dan Spencer finds the first guns in England's accounts in 1345. Two hundred years on, archers still went to war beside the gunners.

Did you know? Henry VIII's bronze guns were signed. Most of those on the Mary Rose carry the maker's name and a date, and four add two letters, HI, for Henricus Invictissimus, Henry most invincible (Mary Rose Trust).

What the timeline really shows

So what does this timeline show? Dates of the earliest evidence that survived, not dates of invention. Some are finds, some texts, some pictures, and several are argued over. And the new did not wipe out the old at once. Slingers fought beside the first catapults. Trebuchets threw gunpowder fire balls. Early guns shot arrows.

Verdict, and two questions

So that's the road from sling to cannon. My reading: weapons did not climb a ladder, each step kicking away the last. They piled up, for centuries. Why do you think one of the first pictures of a gun in Europe shows it shooting an arrow, and not a ball?

Quick answers

When was the catapult invented?

Nobody knows the day any of these weapons was invented; what survives is the earliest dated evidence. For the catapult that is a text: the historian Diodorus says that at Syracuse, by his dating in 399 B.C., Dionysius brought in craftsmen at high wages while arming for war with Carthage, and that the catapult was invented at that time, in Syracuse. Diodorus does not describe it, and he wrote more than three centuries later. Many scholars, Tracey Rihll among them, think it was a large bow mounted with a release, the belly bow; Duncan Campbell warns that the usual story rests on working hypotheses.

What is the earliest known gunpowder formula?

A Chinese military manual of 1044 holds what the historian Tonio Andrade calls the earliest known written formula for gunpowder: saltpetre, sulphur and charcoal, with pitch, oil and wax. It was for fire balls, not guns, and the manual says to shoot them from a trebuchet. Then came the fire lance, gunpowder in a tube on a pole; perhaps its earliest certain record, Andrade writes, is from a battle in 1132.

What is the oldest surviving gun with a date?

A small bronze gun found at Xanadu, the Mongol emperors' summer capital, carries a date that matches 1298, and a serial number. Andrade calls it perhaps the oldest surviving gun whose date is beyond doubt; guns that may be older are dated only by where they lay. In Europe the written record begins in Florence: on 11 February 1326 the city ordered iron balls and cannons of metal, and the Italian scholar Sandro Tiberini finds no European record of guns that holds up from before that year.

Did early guns shoot arrows?

Yes, some did. One of the first pictures of a gun in Europe, painted in England around 1326 in a book made for the young Edward III, shows a fat, vase-shaped pot on a trestle with a big bolt coming out of its mouth, aimed at a door. The Royal Armouries built a copy and fired it; its bolt of oak, with a steel tip, flew several hundred metres. The written record has guns shooting arrows too: in July 1338 a French letter records an iron pot gun sent from Rouen with 48 arrows, a pound of saltpetre and half a pound of sulphur.

Did guns replace bows?

Not for a long time. The historian Dan Spencer finds the first guns in England's accounts in 1345. The Mary Rose, Henry VIII's warship, sank in 1545: her lists give 91 guns, 76 of them iron, and 250 longbows, and 172 longbows came up from the wreck. Two hundred years on, archers still went to war beside the gunners. Even in 1521, at the siege of Tenochtitlan, Cortés ran short of ammunition and agreed to build a trebuchet.

Where it happened

  • Sibudu Cave (62000 B.C.): Small stone points that Marlize Lombard and Laurel Phillipson argue tipped arrows, about 64,000 years ago (Antiquity 84, 2010); the year shown is that figure converted, and it is argued, not certain.
  • Holmegaard bog (7000 B.C.): Remains of five elm bows from about 7000 B.C., some of Europe's oldest bows (National Museum of Denmark).
  • Tisenjoch, Ötztal Alps (3300 B.C.): The Iceman died here over 5,300 years ago with an unfinished yew bow and fourteen arrows, two of them finished (South Tyrol Museum of Archaeology); the year shown is that figure converted.
  • Perugia (41 B.C.): Lead sling bullets from the siege of 41 to 40 B.C., one inscribed "You're starving, and you're hiding it from me" (Zangemeister 1885; our sling-bullet episode).
  • Syracuse (399 B.C.): Where, Diodorus writes, Dionysius gathered craftsmen and the catapult was invented; 399 B.C. is the standard conversion of Diodorus's year.
  • Motya (397 B.C.): Bolt-shooters fired from the shore at the siege, archers and slingers on the ships (Diodorus 14.50.4); the siege is conventionally dated 397 or 396 B.C.
  • Terracotta Army, near Xi'an (221 B.C.): 216 bronze crossbow triggers from Pit 1, made in batches (Li et al., Antiquity 88, 2014). Year shown: the end of the Warring States period (476 to 221 B.C.), when trigger crossbows came in; the pit's own date is not discussed in this episode.
  • Thessaloniki (A.D. 586): Bishop John described the Avars' machines with slings; the siege is dated 586 or 597, scholars disagree.
  • Stirling Castle (A.D. 1304): Edward I's Warwolf, almost certainly a trebuchet, and the oriel he paid for so the queen and her ladies could watch the assault (Bain, Calendar of Documents relating to Scotland).
  • Xanadu (Shangdu) (A.D. 1298): A small bronze gun found here carries a date matching 1298 and a serial number; perhaps the oldest surviving gun whose date is beyond doubt (Andrade).
  • Florence (A.D. 1326): On 11 February 1326 the city ordered iron balls and cannons of metal (Tiberini 2022).
  • Rouen (A.D. 1338): A French letter of July 1338 records an iron pot gun sent from Rouen with 48 arrows, a pound of saltpetre and half a pound of sulphur (Morton 2014).
  • Tenochtitlan (Mexico City) (A.D. 1521): Cortés, short of ammunition, had a trebuchet built; its first stone went straight up, came back down and smashed it (Chevedden et al. 1995).
  • The Solent, off Portsmouth (A.D. 1545): The Mary Rose sank here in 1545 with 91 guns and 250 longbows on her lists (Mary Rose Trust); position approximate.

    Transcript

    Read the full narration

    From Sling to Cannon. Guns and bows fought side by side. One of the first pictures of a gun in Europe was painted in England, around 1326. And it is shooting an arrow. A man in armour holds a rod to a fat, vase-shaped pot on a trestle. Out of its mouth comes a big bolt, aimed at a door. It sits in a book made for the young Edward III. The text around it never mentions guns. The Royal Armouries built a copy and fired it. Its bolt of oak, with a steel tip, flew several hundred metres. Why would a gun shoot an arrow? Because new weapons rarely wiped out the old ones. They borrowed from them, and piled up. Today: how the power behind a missile changed. Muscle. A machine that holds the pull. Twisted sinew. Gravity. And fire. The rule for this timeline: each weapon sits at its earliest dated evidence that survives. A find, a text, or a picture. Not the day it was invented. Nobody knows that day. And wood and cord rot, so what survives is often stone or bronze. Step one: muscle. Bow and sling. In Sibudu Cave, in South Africa, the archaeologists Marlize Lombard and Laurel Phillipson argue that small stone points tipped arrows, about 64,000 years ago. A clue, not a bow. Some of Europe's oldest real bows came out of a bog at Holmegaard, in Denmark. Five bows, from about 7000 B.C. They are made of elm. Yew, says the National Museum of Denmark, did not grow there yet. The Iceman, who died in the Alps over 5,300 years ago, carried a yew bow he had not finished. His quiver held 14 arrows. Only two were ready to shoot. The sling is harder to date. Two slings of plaited linen were packed into the tomb of Tutankhamun. And Roman soldiers cast sling bullets of lead, some with words. One, from the siege of Perugia, says: You're starving, and you're hiding it from me. Step two: a machine that holds the pull for you. Syracuse, in Sicily. By the dating of the historian Diodorus, 399 B.C. Dionysius, its ruler, is arming for war with Carthage. He brings in craftsmen from Italy, Greece, even Carthaginian land, with high wages. Temple porches, gymnasiums, the colonnades of the market: all, Diodorus says, were full of workers. There were prizes for the best. Dionysius went round every day, and took the keenest to dinner. Then comes the line: the catapult was invented at that time, in Syracuse. Soon after, at the siege of Motya, his bolt-shooters fired from the shore. The weapon caused great terror, Diodorus writes, because it was new. And beside them, on the ships, stood archers and slingers. The new had joined the old. What was that first catapult? Diodorus does not describe it, and he wrote more than three centuries later. Many scholars, Tracey Rihll among them, think it was a large bow, mounted with a release: the belly bow. Duncan Campbell warns that the usual story rests on working hypotheses. China took its own road. A team studying the Terracotta Army writes that trigger-fired crossbows came in the Warring States period, and needed less skill and strength than a bow. From one pit alone, they studied 216 bronze triggers, made in batches. The wood has rotted. The triggers are all that remain. Step three: twisted sinew. Scholars following Eric Marsden put the next change around the middle of the 4th century B.C. The bow gave way to springs: thick bundles of twisted cord. The Roman engineer Vitruvius says the cords were of sinew, or of hair. Above all, women's hair. And the whole machine was sized from one number. The hole the spring passed through. For a bolt-shooter, Vitruvius makes that hole one ninth of the bolt's length, and the other parts follow from the hole. Rome's scorpion came from this line. So did the onager, named, the late Roman writer Ammianus says, after a wild ass that kicks stones at hunters. If you are enjoying this type of content, please don't forget to subscribe. Step four: gravity. It starts with people pulling. The trebuchet came from China. A long beam on an axle, a sling at one end, ropes at the other. A Chinese military manual of the 11th century lists the crews. For the largest machine, 250 men pulling. At Thessalonica, in 586 or 597, scholars still argue which, a bishop described the machines of the Avars. Lengths of wood, like the beams of a large house, he wrote, and these had slings. Then the crew was replaced by a falling weight. The earliest known drawing of a counterweight machine is in a treatise written for Saladin. The historian Michael Fulton dates it to about 1180. In 1304, Edward I had his Warwolf at Stirling, almost certainly a trebuchet. He paid for a window, so the queen and her ladies could watch the assault. Step five: fire. China, again. That same manual, of 1044, holds what the historian Tonio Andrade calls the earliest known written formula for gunpowder. Saltpetre, sulphur and charcoal, with pitch, oil and wax. For fire balls, not guns. And the manual says how to deliver one: shoot it from a trebuchet. The newest power rode on the old one. Then came the tube: the fire lance, gunpowder in a tube on a pole. Perhaps its earliest certain record, Andrade writes, is from a battle in 1132. Metal guns followed. A small bronze gun found at Xanadu, the Mongol emperors' summer capital, carries a date that matches 1298. And a serial number. Andrade calls it perhaps the oldest surviving gun whose date is beyond doubt. Guns that may be older are dated only by where they lay. In Europe, the written record begins in Florence. On 11 February 1326, the city ordered iron balls, and cannons of metal. The Italian scholar Sandro Tiberini finds no European record of guns that holds up from before that year. That year or the next, in England, came our gun with the arrow. And a second book that Walter of Milemete prepared for the king has another gun, in borders left mostly unfinished. Was the arrow the artist's fancy? The written record has guns shooting arrows too. In July 1338, a French letter records an iron pot gun sent from Rouen, with 48 arrows, a pound of saltpetre and half a pound of sulphur. So did guns replace the old weapons? Not for a long time. In 1521, at the siege of Tenochtitlan, Cortés ran short of ammunition. He agreed to build a trebuchet. At its first shot, write the historian Paul Chevedden and his co-authors, the stone went straight up, came back down, and smashed it. The Mary Rose, Henry VIII's warship. She sank in 1545. Her lists give 91 guns, 76 of them iron. Most of her bronze guns carry the maker's name and a date. Four add two letters: HI. Henricus Invictissimus. Henry most invincible. And her lists give 250 longbows. 172 came up from the wreck. Among her weapons were fire darts, two metres long. The Mary Rose Trust says they were launched from the fighting tops, or shot from a gun. The historian Dan Spencer finds the first guns in England's accounts in 1345. Two hundred years on, archers still went to war beside the gunners. So what does this timeline show? Dates of the earliest evidence that survived, not dates of invention. Some are finds, some texts, some pictures, and several are argued over. And the new did not wipe out the old at once. Slingers fought beside the first catapults. Trebuchets threw gunpowder fire balls. Early guns shot arrows. So that's the road from sling to cannon. My reading: weapons did not climb a ladder, each step kicking away the last. They piled up, for centuries. Why do you think one of the first pictures of a gun in Europe shows it shooting an arrow, and not a ball? And muscle, a machine that holds the pull, twisted sinew, gravity or fire: which step changed war the most?
