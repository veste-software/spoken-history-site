In July 1518, a woman in Strasbourg began to dance. Within four weeks, a chronicle of the time says, more than four hundred people were dancing. Not for joy: they danced day and night, a later chronicle says, until they fell down, and the city called it a sickness. And one cure it tried was more dancing: stages in the markets, the later chronicle adds, and people paid to dance with them. It also banned dancing in the whole city, and sent the dancers on wagons to a saint in a cave. Why would hundreds of people dance until they dropped, and did some really dance to death, as the famous story says? The city's own papers, a handful of chronicles and one famous doctor will take us there.

The bad years

To understand that summer, we need the years before it. Strasbourg was a busy trading city on the Rhine, and according to the historian John Waller, its poor had just lived through terrible years. Hot summers and hard frosts ruined the harvests, and bread had rarely cost so much. Famine came in 1517, and so many died that one chronicler simply called it the bad year.

The first dancer

Then, in the summer of 1518, it began with one woman. A Strasbourg family chronicle, written, the scholar Alfred Martin judged, by someone who lived through it, puts the start in the middle of July. A woman began to dance, it says, and after some six days, “my lords had her taken to Saint Vitus at Saverne. There she was quiet.” While she was still on the road, more began to dance by the council's stable. In 4 days there were about 34, women and men. The council forbade the drums and pipes, and put the dancers in two guild halls, the tanners' and the carpenters'. And in 4 weeks, the chronicle ends, there were more than 400 dancers.

The cure was more dancing

So the city had a crisis on its streets, and its first answer came from the doctors. The physicians told the council that this was a natural sickness, which came from hot blood. The dancers themselves wanted masses said for them. So the council asked the bishop's deputy, and his answer is a surprise. “Since the doctors say it is a natural sickness,” he replied, “one should try natural remedies on them.” And so that something was done, he would have the preachers call the people to prayer. What the city tried, Martin and Waller both conclude, was dancing. A fuller account comes from Daniel Specklin, the city's architect, who was born in 1536 and wrote it down decades later. The dancers were given guild halls, he says, and on the horse market and the grain market, stages were built. People were hired, for pay, to keep dancing with them, to drums and pipes. And then he writes: “None of it helped. Many danced themselves to death.” Hold on to that last line, because we will come back to it.

Did you know? When the dancers asked for masses, the bishop's deputy answered that since the doctors called it a natural sickness, one should try natural remedies on them; the preachers would call the people to prayer.

The families in the council minutes

Who were the dancers? For once, the council's own minutes give us names. On a Friday in late July, three councillors called in the families of dancers, to settle who would pay. Balthasar Burgawer, a schoolmaster, said his son Bernhart had watched the dancers. That very morning, when the boy should have been helping him teach the children, he began to dance himself. His father kept him quiet until the afternoon. After two o'clock, the boy wanted to go to the dance, and his father had to take him there. He had no wife and no servants, he said, and he could not leave his pupils, but he would pay for his son. A wheelwright, Antheng Silinger, came home from the harvest and found his wife Apolonia standing in the room, hopping. He asked her three times what was wrong, thought her old fits had come back, and held her down. Then the dancers came past with a bagpipe, and she jumped up and ran after them. Hans Eckart, who lived on alms, had a daughter in the dance, another Apolonia. He was a poor, old, broken man, he said, but he would gladly take her to the saint. The council split the dancers into three groups, and ordered three wagons, each pulled by three horses. At the shrine, every dancer was to be led round the altar and give a penny. If a dancer could not, the man leading them was to pay it.

Did you know? At the shrine, every dancer was to be led round the altar and give a penny. If a dancer could not, the man leading them was to pay it (the council papers of 1518).

Saint Vitus and the red shoes

Why a saint? Because the dance already had a name, Saint Vitus' dance. Vitus was an early martyr, said to have come out of a cauldron of boiling lead unharmed, and along the Rhine, Waller writes, people believed he could send the dance as well as heal it. “God give you Saint Vitus” was a curse. “Whoever curses another,” says the town law of Rottweil in 1485, “with the fever, Saint Vitus' dance, or such words, shall pay three shillings.” Saint Vitus' shrine lay at a cave in the hills behind Saverne, more than twenty miles from Strasbourg, at a place called the Hollow Stone. There, Specklin says, the dancers were given little crosses and red shoes. Crosses were drawn on the shoes, on top and underneath, with holy oil, and sprinkled with holy water, in Saint Vitus' name. That, he says, helped them all greatly. Back in Strasbourg, a later extract from the council's minutes says, nobody was to dance anywhere in the city until Michaelmas, at the end of September, on pain of thirty shillings, and no drums were to be beaten. There was one exception. At weddings and first masses, people could dance to stringed instruments, as each one's conscience allowed.

Did you know? Saint Vitus' dance was also a curse. The town law of Rottweil, in 1485, fined anyone who cursed another with "Saint Vitus' dance" three shillings.

The dancing that would not end

But the dancing did not stop, and the council's orders grow more desperate. Families had to keep their sick at home, guilds had to keep theirs off the streets, with no music, no jewellery and no fine clothes. One order begins: “Since the sickness of the dancing, alas, will not end.” In the same order, someone crossed out the words “to Saint Vitus”, and wrote instead: “to the saints, wherever they please”. The council also decided to send the saint a candle made from a hundredweight of wax, with a sung mass, on behalf of the whole council and city. Martin, who printed these papers, thought the dancing went on until late September at least.

Did you know? In one of the council's orders, the words "to Saint Vitus" are crossed out, and "to the saints, wherever they please" is written instead.

Did they dance to death?

Now the hardest question: how many of them died? The famous answer is fifteen a day. In the council papers that Martin printed, no dancer dies at all. There is a fifteen in a chronicle of the time, kept by Wilhelm Rem, a merchant of Augsburg. In the summer of 1518, he wrote, Saint Vitus' dance came upon so many people in Strasbourg that about 15 were seized in a day. It says seized, not killed. We could not trace who first turned it into fifteen deaths a day. The deaths are in later chronicles. Specklin says many danced themselves to death, and a rhymed chronicle printed in 1625 says that they danced until they fainted, and many died of it. The historian John Waller follows them: dozens died, he writes. Even the numbers of dancers swing wildly: about 50, says the extract from the minutes, over 100 at once, says Specklin, more than 400 in 4 weeks, says the family chronicle. Even the year went wrong: for decades, the great medical histories put the outbreak a century too early, in 1418. And some of the old manuscripts burned in 1870, so we know them only from copies and quotations.

Did you know? The fifteen in Wilhelm Rem's chronicle of that summer are people seized by the dance in a day, not deaths. We could not trace who first turned them into fifteen deaths a day.

Did you know? For decades, the great medical histories dated the outbreak to 1418, a century too early (Alfred Martin, 1914).

Who was Frau Troffea?

And the first dancer? Modern books often call her Frau Troffea. The name comes from Paracelsus, the restless physician, who came to Strasbourg in 1526. In one of his books he tells how Saint Vitus' dance began, with a woman called Troffea, the first to suffer it. When her husband asked something of her that she disliked, he wrote, she pretended she could not help dancing, because nothing was more unpleasant to her husband than dance. Other women copied her, he says, and people took it for a real disease. But in the passage we read, he names no city and no year. Making Troffea the woman of Strasbourg in 1518 is how modern historians, Waller among them, read it. His real point was about belief. “An idea that someone puts about,” he wrote, “becomes a truth.”

Did you know? Paracelsus's Troffea, he wrote, pretended she could not help dancing, because nothing was more unpleasant to her husband than dance. In the passage we read, he names no city and no year.

Why did they dance?

So what made them dance? There are three main answers. The first is poison. In a book of the 1950s, Eugene Backman looked to ergot, a mould on damp rye that can cause convulsions and delusions. John Waller rejects it. Ergot, he writes, can trigger violent convulsions, but not coordinated movements that last for days. Waller's own answer is the mind: a mass psychogenic illness, what older books call mass hysteria. Hunger and disease left people in deep distress, he argues, and they believed an angry saint could curse them to dance, so one slipped into a trance, and others followed. And by putting the dancers in the most public places in the city, he says, the council helped the dance to spread. The historian Gregor Rohmann points to religion. Along the Upper Rhine, people went on dancing at saints' shrines for more than a century after 1518, as a vow and as a cure. And Specklin adds a sour note. “A lot of roguery,” he wrote, “got mixed in with it.”

What we really know

So what do we really have? The council's own papers show that the dancing was real, that it lasted for weeks, and that the city tried music, bans and a saint. The weak points are the death toll, and the first dancer's name. What would settle it? A burial or hospital record from that summer naming a dancer who died. In 2018, a Strasbourg museum exhibition gathered the documents again, and more may still lie in the archive.

Verdict

My verdict: the dancing plague was real, and fear and misery are, to me, the best explanation, though no one can test a mind five hundred years later. The fifteen deaths a day are in no record of that summer that we could find.

Over to you

If you had lived in Strasbourg in fifteen eighteen, would you have trusted the doctors' cure, or the saint's red shoes? And the woman Paracelsus called Troffea: the real first dancer, or a story told to blame a wife?

Where it happened

  • Strasbourg, France (A.D. 1518): Where the dancing began in July 1518. A family chronicle of the time places the first dancers by the council's stable and in the guild halls of the tanners and the carpenters, and, in Specklin's later account, stages on the horse market and the grain market (their exact sites are not marked here). The marker is the cathedral.
  • Saint Vitus cave (the Hollow Stone), above Saverne (A.D. 1518): Saint Vitus' shrine in a cave in the hills behind Saverne, where the council sent the dancers on wagons; Specklin says they were given little crosses and red shoes there. The marker is the town of Saverne, not the cave itself.
  • Rottweil, Germany (A.D. 1485): Its town law of 1485 fined anyone who cursed another with the fever or Saint Vitus' dance three shillings (Martin 1914, p. 121).
  • Augsburg, Germany (A.D. 1518): Home of the merchant Wilhelm Rem, whose chronicle of the time says that about fifteen people were seized by the dance in a day in Strasbourg.

    Read the full narration

    The Dancing Plague. The cure was more dancing. In July 1518, a woman in Strasbourg began to dance. Within four weeks, a chronicle of the time says, more than four hundred people were dancing. Not for joy: they danced day and night, a later chronicle says, until they fell down, and the city called it a sickness. And one cure it tried was more dancing: stages in the markets, the later chronicle adds, and people paid to dance with them. It also banned dancing in the whole city, and sent the dancers on wagons to a saint in a cave. Why would hundreds of people dance until they dropped, and did some really dance to death, as the famous story says? The city's own papers, a handful of chronicles and one famous doctor will take us there. To understand that summer, we need the years before it. Strasbourg was a busy trading city on the Rhine, and according to the historian John Waller, its poor had just lived through terrible years. Hot summers and hard frosts ruined the harvests, and bread had rarely cost so much. Famine came in 1517, and so many died that one chronicler simply called it the bad year. Then, in the summer of 1518, it began with one woman. A Strasbourg family chronicle, written, the scholar Alfred Martin judged, by someone who lived through it, puts the start in the middle of July. A woman began to dance, it says, and after some six days, my lords had her taken to Saint Vitus at Saverne. There she was quiet. While she was still on the road, more began to dance by the council's stable. In 4 days there were about 34, women and men. The council forbade the drums and pipes, and put the dancers in two guild halls, the tanners' and the carpenters'. And in 4 weeks, the chronicle ends, there were more than 400 dancers. So the city had a crisis on its streets, and its first answer came from the doctors. The physicians told the council that this was a natural sickness, which came from hot blood. The dancers themselves wanted masses said for them. So the council asked the bishop's deputy, and his answer is a surprise. Since the doctors say it is a natural sickness, he replied, one should try natural remedies on them. And so that something was done, he would have the preachers call the people to prayer. What the city tried, Martin and Waller both conclude, was dancing. A fuller account comes from Daniel Specklin, the city's architect, who was born in 1536 and wrote it down decades later. The dancers were given guild halls, he says, and on the horse market and the grain market, stages were built. People were hired, for pay, to keep dancing with them, to drums and pipes. And then he writes: None of it helped. Many danced themselves to death. Hold on to that last line, because we will come back to it. Who were the dancers? For once, the council's own minutes give us names. On a Friday in late July, three councillors called in the families of dancers, to settle who would pay. Balthasar Burgawer, a schoolmaster, said his son Bernhart had watched the dancers. That very morning, when the boy should have been helping him teach the children, he began to dance himself. His father kept him quiet until the afternoon. After two o'clock, the boy wanted to go to the dance, and his father had to take him there. He had no wife and no servants, he said, and he could not leave his pupils, but he would pay for his son. A wheelwright, Antheng Silinger, came home from the harvest and found his wife Apolonia standing in the room, hopping. He asked her three times what was wrong, thought her old fits had come back, and held her down. Then the dancers came past with a bagpipe, and she jumped up and ran after them. Hans Eckart, who lived on alms, had a daughter in the dance, another Apolonia. He was a poor, old, broken man, he said, but he would gladly take her to the saint. The council split the dancers into three groups, and ordered three wagons, each pulled by three horses. At the shrine, every dancer was to be led round the altar and give a penny. If a dancer could not, the man leading them was to pay it. If you are enjoying this type of content, please don't forget to subscribe. Why a saint? Because the dance already had a name, Saint Vitus' dance. Vitus was an early martyr, said to have come out of a cauldron of boiling lead unharmed, and along the Rhine, Waller writes, people believed he could send the dance as well as heal it. God give you Saint Vitus was a curse. Whoever curses another, says the town law of Rottweil in 1485, with the fever, Saint Vitus' dance, or such words, shall pay three shillings. Saint Vitus' shrine lay at a cave in the hills behind Saverne, more than twenty miles from Strasbourg, at a place called the Hollow Stone. There, Specklin says, the dancers were given little crosses and red shoes. Crosses were drawn on the shoes, on top and underneath, with holy oil, and sprinkled with holy water, in Saint Vitus' name. That, he says, helped them all greatly. Back in Strasbourg, a later extract from the council's minutes says, nobody was to dance anywhere in the city until Michaelmas, at the end of September, on pain of thirty shillings, and no drums were to be beaten. There was one exception. At weddings and first masses, people could dance to stringed instruments, as each one's conscience allowed. But the dancing did not stop, and the council's orders grow more desperate. Families had to keep their sick at home, guilds had to keep theirs off the streets, with no music, no jewellery and no fine clothes. One order begins: Since the sickness of the dancing, alas, will not end. In the same order, someone crossed out the words to Saint Vitus, and wrote instead: to the saints, wherever they please. The council also decided to send the saint a candle made from a hundredweight of wax, with a sung mass, on behalf of the whole council and city. Martin, who printed these papers, thought the dancing went on until late September at least. Now the hardest question: how many of them died? The famous answer is fifteen a day. In the council papers that Martin printed, no dancer dies at all. There is a fifteen in a chronicle of the time, kept by Wilhelm Rem, a merchant of Augsburg. In the summer of 1518, he wrote, Saint Vitus' dance came upon so many people in Strasbourg that about 15 were seized in a day. It says seized, not killed. We could not trace who first turned it into fifteen deaths a day. The deaths are in later chronicles. Specklin says many danced themselves to death, and a rhymed chronicle printed in 1625 says that they danced until they fainted, and many died of it. The historian John Waller follows them: dozens died, he writes. Even the numbers of dancers swing wildly: about 50, says the extract from the minutes, over 100 at once, says Specklin, more than 400 in 4 weeks, says the family chronicle. Even the year went wrong: for decades, the great medical histories put the outbreak a century too early, in 1418. And some of the old manuscripts burned in 1870, so we know them only from copies and quotations. And the first dancer? Modern books often call her Frau Troffea. The name comes from Paracelsus, the restless physician, who came to Strasbourg in 1526. In one of his books he tells how Saint Vitus' dance began, with a woman called Troffea, the first to suffer it. When her husband asked something of her that she disliked, he wrote, she pretended she could not help dancing, because nothing was more unpleasant to her husband than dance. Other women copied her, he says, and people took it for a real disease. But in the passage we read, he names no city and no year. Making Troffea the woman of Strasbourg in 1518 is how modern historians, Waller among them, read it. His real point was about belief. An idea that someone puts about, he wrote, becomes a truth. So what made them dance? There are three main answers. The first is poison. In a book of the nineteen fifties, Eugene Backman looked to ergot, a mould on damp rye that can cause convulsions and delusions. John Waller rejects it. Ergot, he writes, can trigger violent convulsions, but not coordinated movements that last for days. Waller's own answer is the mind: a mass psychogenic illness, what older books call mass hysteria. Hunger and disease left people in deep distress, he argues, and they believed an angry saint could curse them to dance, so one slipped into a trance, and others followed. And by putting the dancers in the most public places in the city, he says, the council helped the dance to spread. The historian Gregor Rohmann points to religion. Along the Upper Rhine, people went on dancing at saints' shrines for more than a century after 1518, as a vow and as a cure. And Specklin adds a sour note. A lot of roguery, he wrote, got mixed in with it. So what do we really have? The council's own papers show that the dancing was real, that it lasted for weeks, and that the city tried music, bans and a saint. The weak points are the death toll, and the first dancer's name. What would settle it? A burial or hospital record from that summer naming a dancer who died. In 2018, a Strasbourg museum exhibition gathered the documents again, and more may still lie in the archive. My verdict: the dancing plague was real, and fear and misery are, to me, the best explanation, though no one can test a mind five hundred years later. The fifteen deaths a day are in no record of that summer that we could find. If you had lived in Strasbourg in 1518, would you have trusted the doctors' cure, or the saint's red shoes?
