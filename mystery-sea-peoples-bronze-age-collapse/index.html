Who Were the Sea Peoples?

“Now the ships of the enemy have come. They have been setting fire to my cities.” A king of Ugarit wrote that, on the coast of Syria, as the Bronze Age was ending. Ugarit was destroyed, and archaeologists found arrowheads in its walls, amid burned houses. The capital of the Hittites burned. So did palaces in Greece. In Egypt, a pharaoh carved who he said had come: peoples from islands, from across the sea. In the nineteenth century, French scholars gave them one name: the Sea Peoples. So who were they? And did they really break the Bronze Age? Or did something else break it first? To find out, we need the walls they were carved on, the letters of the cities they hit, and the bones of the people they became.

Northerners from all lands

Start with the Egyptians, because they wrote the most about them. The first great wave reached Egypt under the pharaoh Merneptah. Eric Cline dates it to 1207 B.C. A Libyan chief, Meryey, invaded the western Delta. He brought his wife and children, and fighters from the north. Merneptah's inscription at Karnak names them: "Ekwesh, Teresh, Luka, Sherden, Shekelesh, Northerners coming from all lands." And it says why they came: for food. "To seek the necessities of their mouths", as the text puts it. His archers, the text says, cut them down for six hours ("six hours of destruction among them"). Then the scribes counted the dead. From the Libyans, they took the phalli. From the men of the countries of the sea, who had no foreskins, they took hands. For the Teresh, the list gives 742 men, and 790 hands. The Egyptologist James Breasted, who translated the list in 1906, wrote that why there were more hands than men "does not appear".

Did you know? Egypt's scribes counted the dead by trophies, and for the Teresh they listed 742 men but 790 hands. James Breasted, who translated the list in 1906, wrote that the reason "does not appear".

Soldiers for both sides

One of those names was not new in Egypt. About seventy years earlier, at the battle of Kadesh, the subject of our episode on Kadesh, Ramesses II had Sherden in his army. His poem calls them “the Sherden of the captivity of his majesty from the victories of his sword”. Captured men, it seems, now serving as the pharaoh's soldiers. So these were not strangers from nowhere. Some fought against Egypt. Some fought for it.

Did you know? The Sherden fought on both sides: captured men in Ramesses II's army at Kadesh, against Egypt in Merneptah's war, and at Medinet Habu both in the enemy ships and in the thick of the fight on Egypt's side.

Ugarit's last letters

Now go north, to the cities that were not as lucky as Egypt. Ugarit was an important trading port on the Syrian coast, a vassal of the Hittite king. Its clay letters show the end coming. Hittite letters sent to Ugarit ask for grain. One letter to Ugarit ends: “It is a matter of life or death!” Another, from further inland: “If you do not quickly arrive here, we ourselves will die of hunger. You will not see a living soul from your land!” Then came the ships. In a letter to the king of Alashiya, probably Cyprus, the king of Ugarit, Ammurapi, reports that seven enemy ships have come and pillaged his land. He explains why he could not stop them. His troops were away in Hittite country. His ships were in Lukka, in what is now southwest Turkey. The scholar Hara Georgiou suggested that the attack itself may have stopped the letter from being sent. Or it was a copy kept on file. We do not know.

Did you know? The king of Ugarit's letter about seven enemy ships may never have been sent. The scholar Hara Georgiou suggested that the attack itself may have stopped it; or it was a copy kept on file.

The walls of Medinet Habu

Then they reached Egypt again. This time, we have pictures. Ramesses III had the war carved on his temple at Medinet Habu, near Luxor. It was his eighth year. In Eric Cline's dating, 1177 B.C. His inscription of that year says: “As for the foreign countries, they made a conspiracy in their isles. … No land could stand before their arms.” The list of the fallen names Hatti, the Hittite empire, and Carchemish. "Their confederation was the Peleset, Theker, Shekelesh, Denye(n), and Weshesh, lands united." Ramesses says he was ready. He had the Nile mouth prepared “like a strong wall with warships”. The relief shows five enemy ships against four Egyptian ones. One enemy ship has capsized. The enemy wear feathered headdresses, or horned helmets, or skullcaps. In the land battle, the Egyptians reach heavy ox carts. In them are the enemy's wives, children and supplies. And in the thick of the fight, on Egypt's side, are Sherden. A papyrus written after his death says he put the prisoners in forts, and taxed them. In its words: "I settled them in strongholds, bound in my name."

Did they break the Bronze Age?

That is Egypt's story. Now the hard question: did these people break the Bronze Age? For a long time, many thought so. In the eighteen sixties and seventies, Gaston Maspero and other French scholars blamed the Sea Peoples, on Egypt's texts alone. After that, Cline writes, every burned layer a dig turned up was put down to them. But take the Hittite capital, Hattusa. Ramesses lists Hatti among the fallen. The excavations led by Jürgen Seeher found the city burned early in the twelfth century B.C. Yet the historian Trevor Bryce writes that by then it was “probably little more than a ghost town”. Many of its people had left, carrying off their valuables and the city's official records. The final blow, he suggests, may have come from the Kaska, a people from the north. Not from the sea. At Hazor, in Israel, the palace and the temples were burned. The rest of the city was essentially untouched. Who did that, we do not know. And Cline points to earthquakes: not one, but a series, over about fifty years, from about 1225 to 1175 B.C.

Did you know? When Hattusa, the Hittite capital, burned, it was probably little more than a ghost town: many of its people had left with their valuables and the city's official records (Trevor Bryce).

Drought in the tree rings

Then there is the weather, written in wood. At Gordion, in central Anatolia, about 230 kilometres west of Hattusa, juniper timbers preserve a tree-ring record. In a study of 2023, Sturt Manning's team looked at the driest years. Between about 1270 and 1135 B.C., only once did they come back to back. Three years in a row, from about 1198 to 1196 B.C. Give or take three years. Right when the Hittite kingdom was collapsing. In July 2026, a climate model from Stockholm University, run over 8,000 years, suggested why: the worst droughts came when several natural climate cycles lined up. Eric Cline calls the whole thing a perfect storm, a systems collapse. And he writes that the Sea Peoples may have been “as much victims as oppressors”.

Did you know? In the Gordion junipers, between about 1270 and 1135 B.C., the driest years came back to back only once: three years in a row, about 1198 to 1196 B.C., right when the Hittite kingdom was collapsing.

Who were they?

So if they did not do it alone, who were they? They left no inscriptions of their own. No ancient site has ever been identified as their home. So scholars match names: Sherden with Sardinia, Shekelesh with Sicily, Teresh with the Etruscans, Ekwesh with Homer's Achaeans. Lukka is generally accepted as a people of southwest Turkey. For Sardinia and Sicily, Cline writes, there is no real archaeological basis. And it is quite possible, he adds, that the Sherden only went to Sardinia after Egypt defeated them, and gave the island their name. One name holds up better than the rest. Champollion, the man who deciphered hieroglyphs, linked the Peleset to the Philistines of the Bible.

Did you know? Sardinia may be named after the Sherden, not the other way round: Eric Cline thinks it quite possible that they only went to the island after Egypt defeated them.

The Philistine graves

And the Philistines left something the others did not: their graves. In 2016, the team digging at Ashkelon announced the first Philistine cemetery ever found. More than 210 people have been excavated there. Beside some lay small vials of perfume. Presumably, one report said, so the dead could smell perfume throughout eternity. In 2019, geneticists tried 108 skeletal samples from Ashkelon. Only ten gave enough DNA. Four infants, buried under houses of the early Iron Age, carried a new, European-related ancestry. Within no more than two centuries, it could no longer be detected. Where did it come from? The best fit was Bronze Age Crete. Modern Sardinians and Bronze Age Iberia also fitted. The real source, the authors warn, may be missing from the data. Even the pigs changed. Wild boars in Israel today carry European pig DNA, and a study of 2013 raised the possibility that European pigs came with the Sea Peoples.

Did you know? Beside some of the dead in the Philistine cemetery at Ashkelon lay small vials of perfume, "presumably so that the deceased could smell perfume throughout eternity", as one report put it (NPR, 2016, quoting the newspaper Haaretz).

Did you know? Wild boars in Israel today carry European pig DNA, and a study of 2013 raised the possibility that European pigs came with the Sea Peoples.

Some of the others stayed too. Generations after Ramesses III, an Egyptian envoy named Wenamun landed at Dor, a city of the Tjeker. Its king sent him much bread, a jar of wine, and a joint of beef. Later, at Byblos, he saw eleven Tjeker ships coming from the sea, sent to arrest him. “I sat down and began to weep”, his story says.

The verdict

So who were the Sea Peoples? They were not one nation, but raiders, soldiers for hire, and families in ox carts. Some of them, it seems, became the Philistines. Did they break the Bronze Age? They were probably part of its end, with drought, earthquakes, hunger and fire. How big a part, nobody can yet say. What would settle it? More ancient DNA from the places their names point to, and destruction layers dated closely enough to say who struck first.

Quick answers

Who were the Sea Peoples?

The name is modern: in the nineteenth century, French scholars gave one name, the Sea Peoples, to the peoples an Egyptian pharaoh said had come from islands, from across the sea. Merneptah's inscription at Karnak names Ekwesh, Teresh, Lukka, Sherden and Shekelesh; Ramesses III names the Peleset, Tjeker, Shekelesh, Denyen and Weshesh. They were not one nation, but raiders, soldiers for hire, and families in ox carts. They left no inscriptions of their own, and no ancient site has ever been identified as their home. Some of them, it seems, became the Philistines.

Did the Sea Peoples cause the Bronze Age collapse?

They were probably part of its end, with drought, earthquakes, hunger and fire; how big a part, nobody can yet say. In the 1860s and 1870s Gaston Maspero and other French scholars blamed the Sea Peoples, on Egypt's texts alone. But the historian Trevor Bryce writes that the Hittite capital, Hattusa, was probably little more than a ghost town when it burned, and that the final blow may have come from the Kaska, from the north. Eric Cline calls the whole thing a perfect storm, a systems collapse, and writes that the Sea Peoples may have been as much victims as oppressors.

When did the Sea Peoples attack Egypt?

The first great wave reached Egypt under the pharaoh Merneptah, in 1207 B.C. in Eric Cline's dating, when the Libyan chief Meryey invaded the western Delta with fighters from the north. Ramesses III fought them in his eighth year, 1177 B.C. in Cline's dating, and had the war carved on his temple at Medinet Habu, near Luxor: five enemy ships against four Egyptian ones, and in the land battle heavy ox carts with the enemy's wives, children and supplies.

Was there a drought at the end of the Bronze Age?

Yes, at least in central Anatolia. At Gordion, about 230 kilometres west of Hattusa, juniper timbers preserve a tree-ring record. Sturt Manning's team (2023) found that between about 1270 and 1135 B.C. the driest years came back to back only once: three years in a row, from about 1198 to 1196 B.C., give or take three years, right when the Hittite kingdom was collapsing. In July 2026 a climate model from Stockholm University, run over 8,000 years, suggested that the worst droughts came when several natural climate cycles lined up.

Were the Philistines descended from the Sea Peoples?

Some of them, it seems. Champollion linked the Peleset of Egypt's texts to the Philistines of the Bible, the one name match that holds up better than the rest. In 2019 geneticists tried 108 skeletal samples from Ashkelon; only ten gave enough DNA. Four infants, buried under houses of the early Iron Age, carried a new, European-related ancestry, which within no more than two centuries could no longer be detected. The best fit was Bronze Age Crete; modern Sardinians and Bronze Age Iberia also fitted, and the authors warn that the real source may be missing from the data.

Where it happened

  • Ugarit (Ras Shamra), Syria (1185 B.C.): A trading port on the Syrian coast, a vassal of the Hittite king. Its clay letters ask for grain and report seven enemy ships (Georgiou 1979, Cline 2015); the city was destroyed and arrowheads were found in its walls amid burned houses (Cline 2015). The year is only a marker for the last years of the city: the letters are not dated to a year.
  • Medinet Habu, temple of Ramesses III, near Luxor, Egypt (1177 B.C.): Ramesses III had the war of his eighth year carved on his temple here: the inscription of year eight and the sea battle on the north wall (Edgerton and Wilson 1936). 1177 B.C. is Eric Cline's dating.
  • Karnak, Thebes, Egypt (1207 B.C.): Merneptah's Great Karnak Inscription names Ekwesh, Teresh, Luka, Sherden and Shekelesh, "Northerners coming from all lands" (Breasted 1906). 1207 B.C. is Eric Cline's dating.
  • Hattusa (Boğazkale), Turkey (1190 B.C.): The Hittite capital, burned early in the twelfth century B.C. (excavations led by Jürgen Seeher); Trevor Bryce writes that it was probably little more than a ghost town by then and suggests the final blow may have come from the Kaska, from the north. The year is a marker for "early in the twelfth century".
  • Gordion, Turkey (1197 B.C.): About 230 km west of Hattusa. Its juniper timbers show the only run of back to back extreme dry years between about 1270 and 1135 B.C.: three in a row, about 1198 to 1196 B.C., give or take three years (Manning et al., Nature 2023).
  • Ashkelon, Israel (A.D. 2016): The team digging here announced the first Philistine cemetery ever found in 2016; more than 210 people have been excavated. DNA from ten of 108 samples (Feldman et al. 2019) shows a European-related ancestry in four infants buried under early Iron Age houses (not in the cemetery).
  • Hazor, Israel : The palace and the temples were burned; the rest of the city was essentially untouched. Who did that is not known (Cline 2015).
  • Dor, Israel : Called a city of the Tjeker (Breasted: Thekel) in the Report of Wenamun, whose king sent the envoy bread, wine and beef (Breasted 1906).

Transcript

Read the full narration

The Sea Peoples. Their enemies wrote their story. Now the ships of the enemy have come. They have been setting fire to my cities. A king of Ugarit wrote that, on the coast of Syria, as the Bronze Age was ending. Ugarit was destroyed, and archaeologists found arrowheads in its walls, amid burned houses. The capital of the Hittites burned. So did palaces in Greece. In Egypt, a pharaoh carved who he said had come: peoples from islands, from across the sea. In the nineteenth century, French scholars gave them one name: the Sea Peoples. So who were they? And did they really break the Bronze Age? Or did something else break it first? To find out, we need the walls they were carved on, the letters of the cities they hit, and the bones of the people they became. Start with the Egyptians, because they wrote the most about them. The first great wave reached Egypt under the pharaoh Merneptah. Eric Cline dates it to 1207 B.C. A Libyan chief, Meryey, invaded the western Delta. He brought his wife and children, and fighters from the north. Merneptah's inscription at Karnak names them: "Ekwesh, Teresh, Luka, Sherden, Shekelesh, Northerners coming from all lands." And it says why they came: for food. "To seek the necessities of their mouths", as the text puts it. His archers, the text says, cut them down for six hours ("six hours of destruction among them"). Then the scribes counted the dead. From the Libyans, they took the phalli. From the men of the countries of the sea, who had no foreskins, they took hands. For the Teresh, the list gives 742 men, and 790 hands. The Egyptologist James Breasted, who translated the list in 1906, wrote that why there were more hands than men "does not appear". One of those names was not new in Egypt. About seventy years earlier, at the battle of Kadesh, the subject of our episode on Kadesh, Ramesses the Second had Sherden in his army. His poem calls them the Sherden of the captivity of his majesty, from the victories of his sword. Captured men, it seems, now serving as the pharaoh's soldiers. So these were not strangers from nowhere. Some fought against Egypt. Some fought for it. Now go north, to the cities that were not as lucky as Egypt. Ugarit was an important trading port on the Syrian coast, a vassal of the Hittite king. Its clay letters show the end coming. Hittite letters sent to Ugarit ask for grain. One letter to Ugarit ends: It is a matter of life or death! Another, from further inland: If you do not quickly arrive here, we ourselves will die of hunger. You will not see a living soul from your land! Then came the ships. In a letter to the king of Alashiya, probably Cyprus, the king of Ugarit, Ammurapi, reports that seven enemy ships have come and pillaged his land. He explains why he could not stop them. His troops were away in Hittite country. His ships were in Lukka, in what is now southwest Turkey. The scholar Hara Georgiou suggested that the attack itself may have stopped the letter from being sent. Or it was a copy kept on file. We do not know. Then they reached Egypt again. This time, we have pictures. Ramesses III had the war carved on his temple at Medinet Habu, near Luxor. It was his eighth year. In Eric Cline's dating, 1177 B.C. As for the foreign countries, they made a conspiracy in their isles. No land could stand before their arms. The list of the fallen names Hatti, the Hittite empire, and Carchemish. "Their confederation was the Peleset, Theker, Shekelesh, Denye(n), and Weshesh, lands united." Ramesses says he was ready. He had the Nile mouth prepared like a strong wall, with warships. The relief shows five enemy ships against four Egyptian ones. One enemy ship has capsized. The enemy wear feathered headdresses, or horned helmets, or skullcaps. In the land battle, the Egyptians reach heavy ox carts. In them are the enemy's wives, children and supplies. And in the thick of the fight, on Egypt's side, are Sherden. A papyrus written after his death says he put the prisoners in forts, and taxed them. In its words: "I settled them in strongholds, bound in my name." If you are enjoying this type of content, please don't forget to subscribe. That is Egypt's story. Now the hard question: did these people break the Bronze Age? For a long time, many thought so. In the eighteen sixties and seventies, Gaston Maspero and other French scholars blamed the Sea Peoples, on Egypt's texts alone. After that, Cline writes, every burned layer a dig turned up was put down to them. But take the Hittite capital, Hattusa. Ramesses lists Hatti among the fallen. The excavations led by Jürgen Seeher found the city burned early in the twelfth century B.C. Yet the historian Trevor Bryce writes that by then it was probably little more than a ghost town. Many of its people had left, carrying off their valuables and the city's official records. The final blow, he suggests, may have come from the Kaska, a people from the north. Not from the sea. At Hazor, in Israel, the palace and the temples were burned. The rest of the city was essentially untouched. Who did that, we do not know. And Cline points to earthquakes: not one, but a series, over about fifty years, from about 1225 to 1175 B.C. Then there is the weather, written in wood. At Gordion, in central Anatolia, about 230 kilometres west of Hattusa, juniper timbers preserve a tree-ring record. In a study of 2023, Sturt Manning's team looked at the driest years. Between about 1270 and 1135 B.C., only once did they come back to back. Three years in a row, from about 1198 to 1196 B.C. Give or take three years. Right when the Hittite kingdom was collapsing. In July 2026, a climate model from Stockholm University, run over 8,000 years, suggested why: the worst droughts came when several natural climate cycles lined up. Eric Cline calls the whole thing a perfect storm, a systems collapse. And he writes that the Sea Peoples may have been as much victims as oppressors. So if they did not do it alone, who were they? They left no inscriptions of their own. No ancient site has ever been identified as their home. So scholars match names: Sherden with Sardinia, Shekelesh with Sicily, Teresh with the Etruscans, Ekwesh with Homer's Achaeans. Lukka is generally accepted as a people of southwest Turkey. For Sardinia and Sicily, Cline writes, there is no real archaeological basis. And it is quite possible, he adds, that the Sherden only went to Sardinia after Egypt defeated them, and gave the island their name. One name holds up better than the rest. Champollion, the man who deciphered hieroglyphs, linked the Peleset to the Philistines of the Bible. And the Philistines left something the others did not: their graves. In 2016, the team digging at Ashkelon announced the first Philistine cemetery ever found. More than 210 people have been excavated there. Beside some lay small vials of perfume. Presumably, one report said, so the dead could smell perfume throughout eternity. In 2019, geneticists tried 108 skeletal samples from Ashkelon. Only ten gave enough DNA. Four infants, buried under houses of the early Iron Age, carried a new, European-related ancestry. Within no more than two centuries, it could no longer be detected. Where did it come from? The best fit was Bronze Age Crete. Modern Sardinians and Bronze Age Iberia also fitted. The real source, the authors warn, may be missing from the data. Even the pigs changed. Wild boars in Israel today carry European pig DNA, and a study of 2013 raised the possibility that European pigs came with the Sea Peoples. Some of the others stayed too. Generations after Ramesses the Third, an Egyptian envoy named Wenamun landed at Dor, a city of the Tjeker. Its king sent him much bread, a jar of wine, and a joint of beef. Later, at Byblos, he saw eleven Tjeker ships coming from the sea, sent to arrest him. I sat down and began to weep, his story says. So who were the Sea Peoples? They were not one nation, but raiders, soldiers for hire, and families in ox carts. Some of them, it seems, became the Philistines. Did they break the Bronze Age? They were probably part of its end, with drought, earthquakes, hunger and fire. How big a part, nobody can yet say. What would settle it? More ancient DNA from the places their names point to, and destruction layers dated closely enough to say who struck first. The Sea Peoples: raiders who broke the Bronze Age, or refugees it broke first? And the Sherden, who fought on both sides at Medinet Habu: men from Sardinia, or men who gave Sardinia its name later?
