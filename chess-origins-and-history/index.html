How Chess Began, and Why the Queen Got Her Power

For about nine hundred years, the piece we call the queen moved just one square at a time, on the diagonal. It was not even a queen. In Persian and Arabic chess, it was the king's counsellor, standing at his side. Then, at the end of the 1400s, it became the strongest piece on the board, almost at once. Players in France called the new game the chess of the mad queen. So where did chess begin, how did it cross half the world, and who gave the queen her power? A royal pun, a Persian riddle, an emperor's insult and a love poem from Valencia will take us there.

India: the four limbs of an army

To find the beginning, we start with the oldest clear words in India that name the game. Early in the 600s, the court poet Bana wrote a book in praise of his king, Harsha, who ruled northern India. He praised the peace of the kingdom with puns. In the translation of Cowell and Thomas: “only chessboards teach the positions of the four members; there is no cutting off the four principal limbs of condemned criminals.” The joke turns on one word, chaturanga, four limbs. It meant the four arms of an Indian army, and it meant a game. Foot soldiers, horses, elephants and chariots. Their places on your board are the pawns, the knights, the bishops and the rooks. The board is older than the game. The Indian grammarian Patanjali, writing more than two thousand years ago, already knew a board of eight squares by eight. What was played on it, we do not know; Murray thought it was a race game with dice.

Did you know? The board of eight squares by eight is older than chess. The Indian grammarian Patanjali, writing more than two thousand years ago, already knew it; what was played on it, we do not know.

Persia: a riddle for the king

From India, the game went west, into the Persian empire of the Sasanian kings. A Persian romance about Ardashir, the first of those kings, was probably written around the year 600. It praises the young hero like this: “he became more skilled than them all in ball-play, in horsemanship, in chess.” Ardashir had lived more than three hundred years before. So the line proves only that, when it was written, chess was a skill a hero should have. A second Persian text tells how the game arrived, and it is a riddle story. An Indian king sends the Persian king of kings, Khusraw, a game with sixteen emerald pieces and sixteen ruby-red ones. With it comes a letter: “if now you cannot discover the interpretation of the chatrang, pay us tribute and revenue.” The king asks for three days, and no wise man in Iran can solve it. Then the wise Wuzurgmihr explains it: the game is fashioned after the likeness of a battle, with kings, rooks, a counsellor, elephants, horses and foot soldiers. He wins twelve games against the Indian envoy. Then he invents a game of his own, nard, played with dice and thirty pieces, and the Indians cannot explain it, even in forty days. The scholar Antonio Panaino calls its historicity very doubtful, and the Indian king probably fictitious. But it keeps something chess historians take seriously: in Persia, chess was remembered as a game that came from India. Persian also gave us two famous words. When the shah, the king, was attacked, players said shah. When he had no way out, they said shah mat. Mat, Murray explains, is Persian for “at a loss, helpless, defeated.” That is our check, and our checkmate.

Did you know? Checkmate is Persian: shah mat. Mat, Murray explains, means at a loss, helpless, defeated.

India or China?

But was India really the beginning? On that, scholars still disagree. In 1962, Joseph Needham, the great historian of Chinese science, argued that chess grew out of an old Chinese way of telling the future. Only in China, he argued, could a fortune-telling game have been devised that was ready to become a game of war. His key text: in 569, Emperor Wu of the Northern Zhou finished a book on an image game, and gathered his officials in a palace hall to lecture them on it. A Chinese writer of about 1085 already said that the emperor's game had the sun, the moon, the stars and the constellations as its pieces. And a game invented in 569, Eales argues, could hardly have turned into chess and been well established in Persia by 600. So Eales thought it probable that chess was devised in northern India, in the sixth century, and more likely by many hands than by one inventor. What has been dug up does not settle it either. In 1977, at Afrasiab, the ruins of old Samarkand, the archaeologist Yuri Buryakov found what the chess historian Jean-Louis Cazaux calls the earliest known chess pieces. Seven little ivory figures: a king, a chariot, an elephant, a horse, two foot soldiers, and probably a counsellor. Datings run from the sixth to the eighth century. A coin in the same layer, Cazaux reports, points to about the year 700.

The legend of the grain

The players had their own story of how it all began. The Muslim historian al-Yaqubi, writing in the late 800s, tells it like this. An Indian queen's son is killed by a rebel, and no one dares to tell her. A wise man named Qaflan has pieces carved from wood of two colours, marks 64 squares on leather, and says: “this is war without bloodshed.” He plays it before the queen, and the winner says shah mat. She understands, and says: “my son is dead.” For his reward, he asks for grain on the board: one on the first square, two on the second, and double again on every square after that. She orders the corn brought, until the corn of the whole country is gone. Doubled across 64 squares, the sum comes to more than 18 billion billion grains. Murray worked out that it would cover England more than 38 feet deep. Later tellers named the inventor Sissa, son of Dahir. It is a legend, and Murray thought the sum itself might be older than chess.

Did you know? Doubled across sixty-four squares, the legend's grain comes to 18,446,744,073,709,551,615 grains (two to the sixty-fourth power, minus one: our arithmetic). Murray worked out that it would cover England more than thirty-eight feet deep.

Baghdad: the game of the caliphs

Legends aside, the first place where chess players have names and records is the Muslim world of the 800s and 900s, above all the court at Baghdad. After the Arab conquest of Persia, chatrang became the Arabic shatranj. Its counsellor still moved one square on the diagonal, so it could only ever reach 32 squares of the board. The elephant jumped two squares on the diagonal. Each one could reach only eight squares in a whole game, and two elephants could never meet. And the board was all one colour. Light and dark squares came later, in Europe. Chess was even good for an insult. The historian al-Tabari, writing about a century later, quotes a letter from the new Byzantine emperor Nicephorus to the caliph Harun al-Rashid, from about 803. “The queen who was my predecessor,” it says, “set you up in the position of a rook, and herself as merely a pawn.” Send back what you received. Harun wrote his answer on the back of the letter: “I have read your letter, and the reply is what you will see, without you having to hear it.” And he marched. Baghdad had champions, too. The most famous was al-Suli, a courtier and writer who died in 946. Shown a lovely garden, the caliph al-Radi once stopped his courtiers' praise: “al-Suli's skill at chess charms me more than these flowers.” Three centuries later, the biographer Ibn Khallikan wrote that people still said of a fine player: “he plays at chess like al-Suli.” He had even met a great number of people who believed al-Suli had invented chess. “An erroneous opinion,” he added.

Did you know? In the old game the elephant jumped two squares on the diagonal, so each one could reach only eight squares in a whole game, and two elephants could never meet.

Charlemagne's chessmen?

Harun's name brings us to Europe's favourite chess legend. In Paris there are sixteen ivory chessmen long known as Charlemagne's chessmen, and a story often told today makes them a gift from Harun himself. The Frankish royal annals do list what Harun sent. In July 802, an elephant arrived at Aachen. The elephant's name was Abul Abaz. In 807 came a great tent, silks, perfumes and a brass water clock that dropped little bronze balls onto a cymbal to sound the hours. There are no chessmen on the list. Charlemagne and his court, Eales thinks, must have known nothing of the game. The French national library dates a queen of the set to between 1080 and 1100, made in southern Italy, more than two and a half centuries after Charlemagne died.

Did you know? The water clock Harun sent in 807 dropped little bronze balls onto a cymbal to sound the hours (the Frankish royal annals).

How chess reached Europe

So how did chess really reach Europe? The most likely doors, Eales argues, were Spain and southern Italy, where Christians lived beside the Muslim world. Around the year 1000, a Latin poem copied at the monastery of Einsiedeln, in Switzerland, describes the game. Its counsellor now has a new name, regina, the queen. And it says some players colour the squares, because it makes the moves easier to follow. Then comes a will. In a will dated 1008, Count Ermengol of Urgell, on the Christian frontier of Spain, left his chessmen to the monastery of Saint Giles, for its church. The Latin says “ipsos meos schacos”: those chessmen of mine. About fifty years later, in Italy, a cardinal gave chess a scolding. Around 1061, Cardinal Peter Damian wrote to the newly elected pope, and told of a journey with the bishop of Florence. One morning his groom told him that the bishop had spent the evening at chess. The bishop defended himself: “scachus is one thing, alea another.” Church law banned dice, and by its silence permitted chess. Damian would have none of it. The penance: run through the psalter three times, and wash the feet of twelve poor men, and give each a coin. In England, around 1180, a royal treasury official explained why his office was called the Exchequer: “it has a shape similar to that of a chess board.” And in our episode on the Lewis chessmen, carved around 1150 to 1200, every queen sits with her hand to her face. Her carving had changed, but her move had not.

The mad queen

Chess at the end of the Middle Ages, Eales writes, was essentially the same game Europe had taken over from the Islamic world five hundred years earlier. Players had tried small fixes, like letting the queen leap once at the start. But she stayed, Eales adds, a rather weak piece, weaker even than the king. A game could drag on until one side had lost everything but the king. A real checkmate was so rare that, by medieval custom, it won a double stake. Then, somewhere in Spain, Italy or France, probably between 1470 and 1490, two pieces were changed. The bishop could now run as far as it liked along a diagonal. And the queen took the moves of the rook and the bishop together, and became the strongest piece on the board. Now a careless player could be mated in four moves, and players began to study the openings. The names tell how it felt: in Spain and France, the queen's chess; in France also, the chess of the mad queen; in Italy, mad chess. The earliest known game with the new moves is a poem from Valencia, called Scachs d'amor, Chess of Love. Three poets wrote it. Francesc de Castellví plays Mars, Narcís Vinyoles plays Venus, and the priest Bernat Fenollar, as Mercury, explains the rules. It has 64 stanzas, one for each square of the board. Every piece stands for something. Mars's queen is Will. Venus's queen is Beauty, and her rooks are Shame. One rule in the poem reads: “if the queen is lost, let the game be lost.” After 21 moves, it is Mars's queen that gives checkmate. The chess historian Ricardo Calvo dated it to between 1470 and 1490. The manuscript was found in Barcelona in 1905, and seems to have vanished during the Spanish Civil War. A photograph of it survives. Valencia also printed a chess book in 1495, by Francesch Vicent, and not one copy survives. The last one known, Eales writes, probably burned at Montserrat in 1834. The oldest printed book on playing chess that does survive came from Salamanca, around 1497, by a young man studying there, Luis de Lucena. It teaches, in his words, the difference between “the game which we now play, which is called of the queen, and the game which used to be played.” He dedicated it to Prince Juan, the heir of Ferdinand and Isabella, who died in 1497, only a few months after his wedding.

Why the queen?

That leaves the question in our title: why did the queen, of all the pieces, get the power? In her book Birth of the Chess Queen, Marilyn Yalom tied the queen's rise to the reign of Isabella of Castile, in Spain, where most of the early evidence comes from. It is an attractive idea. But in the end, a review in Kirkus said, she is left with attractive correlations, and no smoking causative guns. Eales had no patience for the idea that the new queen reflected the emancipation of women. Which women, he asked, and what sort of emancipation? And the same Lucena, he pointed out, bound into his chess book a Discourse on Love that its modern editor called the most extensive anti-feminist document of early Spanish literature. He saw the new chess as the end of a long run of attempts to speed up a slow game, though the new queen herself had no medieval model. Where it began is argued too. Murray leaned to Italy, Calvo to Valencia, and Eales noted that almost all the reliable early evidence is linked with Spain or Portugal. What is certain is the speed. By 1510, Eales writes, the new chess was well entrenched in Spain, Italy and probably France.

Did you know? The oldest surviving printed book on playing chess, Lucena's, has a Discourse on Love bound into it that its modern editor called the most extensive anti-feminist document of early Spanish literature.

What we really have

So what do we really have, after a thousand years of chess? A game first recorded in India and Persia around the year 600, probably born in northern India, though China has its defenders. Around it grew a great many legends. And a queen who moved one square for some nine hundred years, until players at the end of the 1400s let her loose.

Verdict

Chess began as a picture of an Indian army, became a Persian riddle and a Baghdad art, and only a little over five hundred years ago became the game we play.

Quick answers

Where did chess come from?

Scholars still disagree. The game is first recorded in India and Persia around the year 600, and in Persia chess was remembered as a game that came from India. In 1962 Joseph Needham argued that chess grew out of an old Chinese way of telling the future; the chess historian Richard Eales was not convinced, and thought it probable that chess was devised in northern India, in the sixth century, and more likely by many hands than by one inventor. The inventor Sissa, son of Dahir, belongs to a legend.

Where does the word checkmate come from?

From Persian. When the shah, the king, was attacked, players said shah; when he had no way out, they said shah mat. Mat, Murray explains, is Persian for at a loss, helpless, defeated. That is our check, and our checkmate.

Did Harun al-Rashid give Charlemagne a chess set?

That is a legend. The Frankish royal annals list what Harun sent: an elephant named Abul Abaz, which arrived at Aachen in July 802, and in 807 a great tent, silks, perfumes and a brass water clock. There are no chessmen on the list. The French national library dates a queen of the so-called Charlemagne chessmen in Paris to between 1080 and 1100, made in southern Italy, more than two and a half centuries after Charlemagne died.

When did the chess queen become the strongest piece?

At the end of the 1400s: somewhere in Spain, Italy or France, probably between 1470 and 1490. The earliest known game with the new moves is a poem from Valencia, Scachs d'amor, and by 1510, Eales writes, the new chess was well entrenched in Spain, Italy and probably France.

It is still argued. Where it happened

  • Afrasiab (old Samarkand), Uzbekistan (A.D. 1977): Where Yuri Buryakov found, in 1977, the seven little ivory figures that Jean-Louis Cazaux calls the earliest known chess pieces; a coin in the same layer points to about the year 700. The marker is the site, not the trench.
  • Baghdad, Iraq (A.D. 900): The court of the caliphs, where chess players first have names and records (the 800s and 900s): al-Suli, who died in 946, and the caliph al-Radi.
  • Constantinople (Istanbul) (A.D. 803): The emperor Nicephorus wrote from here to Harun al-Rashid, about 803, in a letter that used chess for an insult (al-Tabari).
  • Aachen, Germany (A.D. 802): Charlemagne's palace: the elephant Abul Abaz arrived here in July 802 (the Frankish royal annals).
  • Einsiedeln, Switzerland (A.D. 1000): A Latin poem on chess copied at the monastery around the year 1000 calls the counsellor regina, the queen.
  • La Seu d'Urgell, Catalonia (A.D. 1008): Count Ermengol of Urgell, whose will dated 1008 leaves his chessmen to the monastery of Saint Giles.
  • Florence, Italy (A.D. 1061): Peter Damian's letter of about 1061 tells of the bishop of Florence who spent an evening at chess.
  • Westminster, London (A.D. 1180): The royal Exchequer, whose name, an official explained around 1180, came from its shape, similar to that of a chess board.
  • Valencia, Spain (A.D. 1470): Scachs d'amor, the earliest known game with the new moves, a poem by three Valencian poets, dated by Ricardo Calvo to between 1470 and 1490 (the year on the map is the start of that range).
  • Salamanca, Spain (A.D. 1497): Luis de Lucena's book, around 1497: the oldest printed book on playing chess that survives.

    Read the full narration

    How Chess Began. The queen went mad. For about nine hundred years, the piece we call the queen moved just one square at a time, on the diagonal. It was not even a queen. In Persian and Arabic chess, it was the king's counsellor, standing at his side. Then, at the end of the 1400s, it became the strongest piece on the board, almost at once. Players in France called the new game the chess of the mad queen. So where did chess begin, how did it cross half the world, and who gave the queen her power? A royal pun, a Persian riddle, an emperor's insult and a love poem from Valencia will take us there. To find the beginning, we start with the oldest clear words in India that name the game. Early in the 600s, the court poet Bana wrote a book in praise of his king, Harsha, who ruled northern India. He praised the peace of the kingdom with puns. In the translation of Cowell and Thomas: only chessboards teach the positions of the four members; there is no cutting off the four principal limbs of condemned criminals. The joke turns on one word, chaturanga, four limbs. It meant the four arms of an Indian army, and it meant a game. Foot soldiers, horses, elephants and chariots. Their places on your board are the pawns, the knights, the bishops and the rooks. The board is older than the game. The Indian grammarian Patanjali, writing more than two thousand years ago, already knew a board of eight squares by eight. What was played on it, we do not know; Murray thought it was a race game with dice. From India, the game went west, into the Persian empire of the Sasanian kings. A Persian romance about Ardashir, the first of those kings, was probably written around the year 600. It praises the young hero like this: he became more skilled than them all in ball-play, in horsemanship, in chess. Ardashir had lived more than three hundred years before. So the line proves only that, when it was written, chess was a skill a hero should have. A second Persian text tells how the game arrived, and it is a riddle story. An Indian king sends the Persian king of kings, Khusraw, a game with sixteen emerald pieces and sixteen ruby-red ones. With it comes a letter: if now you cannot discover the interpretation of the chatrang, pay us tribute and revenue. The king asks for three days, and no wise man in Iran can solve it. Then the wise Wuzurgmihr explains it: the game is fashioned after the likeness of a battle, with kings, rooks, a counsellor, elephants, horses and foot soldiers. He wins twelve games against the Indian envoy. Then he invents a game of his own, nard, played with dice and thirty pieces, and the Indians cannot explain it, even in forty days. The scholar Antonio Panaino calls its historicity very doubtful, and the Indian king probably fictitious. But it keeps something chess historians take seriously: in Persia, chess was remembered as a game that came from India. Persian also gave us two famous words. When the shah, the king, was attacked, players said shah. When he had no way out, they said shah mat. Mat, Murray explains, is Persian for at a loss, helpless, defeated. That is our check, and our checkmate. But was India really the beginning? On that, scholars still disagree. In 1962, Joseph Needham, the great historian of Chinese science, argued that chess grew out of an old Chinese way of telling the future. Only in China, he argued, could a fortune-telling game have been devised that was ready to become a game of war. His key text: in 569, Emperor Wu of the Northern Zhou finished a book on an image game, and gathered his officials in a palace hall to lecture them on it. A Chinese writer of about 1085 already said that the emperor's game had the sun, the moon, the stars and the constellations as its pieces. And a game invented in 569, Eales argues, could hardly have turned into chess and been well established in Persia by 600. So Eales thought it probable that chess was devised in northern India, in the sixth century, and more likely by many hands than by one inventor. What has been dug up does not settle it either. In 1977, at Afrasiab, the ruins of old Samarkand, the archaeologist Yuri Buryakov found what the chess historian Jean-Louis Cazaux calls the earliest known chess pieces. Seven little ivory figures: a king, a chariot, an elephant, a horse, two foot soldiers, and probably a counsellor. Datings run from the sixth to the eighth century. A coin in the same layer, Cazaux reports, points to about the year 700. The players had their own story of how it all began. The Muslim historian al-Yaqubi, writing in the late 800s, tells it like this. An Indian queen's son is killed by a rebel, and no one dares to tell her. A wise man named Qaflan has pieces carved from wood of two colours, marks 64 squares on leather, and says: this is war without bloodshed. He plays it before the queen, and the winner says shah mat. She understands, and says: my son is dead. For his reward, he asks for grain on the board: one on the first square, two on the second, and double again on every square after that. She orders the corn brought, until the corn of the whole country is gone. Doubled across 64 squares, the sum comes to more than 18 billion billion grains. Murray worked out that it would cover England more than 38 feet deep. Later tellers named the inventor Sissa, son of Dahir. It is a legend, and Murray thought the sum itself might be older than chess. Legends aside, the first place where chess players have names and records is the Muslim world of the 800s and 900s, above all the court at Baghdad. After the Arab conquest of Persia, chatrang became the Arabic shatranj. Its counsellor still moved one square on the diagonal, so it could only ever reach 32 squares of the board. The elephant jumped two squares on the diagonal. Each one could reach only eight squares in a whole game, and two elephants could never meet. And the board was all one colour. Light and dark squares came later, in Europe. Chess was even good for an insult. The historian al-Tabari, writing about a century later, quotes a letter from the new Byzantine emperor Nicephorus to the caliph Harun al-Rashid, from about 803. The queen who was my predecessor, it says, set you up in the position of a rook, and herself as merely a pawn. Send back what you received. Harun wrote his answer on the back of the letter: I have read your letter, and the reply is what you will see, without you having to hear it. And he marched. Baghdad had champions, too. The most famous was al-Suli, a courtier and writer who died in 946. Shown a lovely garden, the caliph al-Radi once stopped his courtiers' praise: al-Suli's skill at chess charms me more than these flowers. Three centuries later, the biographer Ibn Khallikan wrote that people still said of a fine player: he plays at chess like al-Suli. He had even met a great number of people who believed al-Suli had invented chess. An erroneous opinion, he added. Harun's name brings us to Europe's favourite chess legend. In Paris there are sixteen ivory chessmen long known as Charlemagne's chessmen, and a story often told today makes them a gift from Harun himself. The Frankish royal annals do list what Harun sent. In July 802, an elephant arrived at Aachen. The elephant's name was Abul Abaz. In 807 came a great tent, silks, perfumes and a brass water clock that dropped little bronze balls onto a cymbal to sound the hours. There are no chessmen on the list. Charlemagne and his court, Eales thinks, must have known nothing of the game. The French national library dates a queen of the set to between 1080 and 1100, made in southern Italy, more than two and a half centuries after Charlemagne died. So how did chess really reach Europe? The most likely doors, Eales argues, were Spain and southern Italy, where Christians lived beside the Muslim world. Around the year 1000, a Latin poem copied at the monastery of Einsiedeln, in Switzerland, describes the game. Its counsellor now has a new name, regina, the queen. And it says some players colour the squares, because it makes the moves easier to follow. Then comes a will. In a will dated 1008, Count Ermengol of Urgell, on the Christian frontier of Spain, left his chessmen to the monastery of Saint Giles, for its church. The Latin says ipsos meos schacos: those chessmen of mine. About fifty years later, in Italy, a cardinal gave chess a scolding. Around 1061, Cardinal Peter Damian wrote to the newly elected pope, and told of a journey with the bishop of Florence. One morning his groom told him that the bishop had spent the evening at chess. The bishop defended himself: scachus is one thing, alea another. Church law banned dice, and by its silence permitted chess. Damian would have none of it. The penance: run through the psalter three times, and wash the feet of twelve poor men, and give each a coin. In England, around 1180, a royal treasury official explained why his office was called the Exchequer: it has a shape similar to that of a chess board. And in our episode on the Lewis chessmen, carved around 1150 to 1200, every queen sits with her hand to her face. Her carving had changed, but her move had not. Chess at the end of the Middle Ages, Eales writes, was essentially the same game Europe had taken over from the Islamic world five hundred years earlier. Players had tried small fixes, like letting the queen leap once at the start. But she stayed, Eales adds, a rather weak piece, weaker even than the king. A game could drag on until one side had lost everything but the king. A real checkmate was so rare that, by medieval custom, it won a double stake. Then, somewhere in Spain, Italy or France, probably between 1470 and 1490, two pieces were changed. The bishop could now run as far as it liked along a diagonal. And the queen took the moves of the rook and the bishop together, and became the strongest piece on the board. Now a careless player could be mated in four moves, and players began to study the openings. The names tell how it felt: in Spain and France, the queen's chess; in France also, the chess of the mad queen; in Italy, mad chess. The earliest known game with the new moves is a poem from Valencia, called Scachs d'amor, Chess of Love. Three poets wrote it. Francesc de Castellví plays Mars, Narcís Vinyoles plays Venus, and the priest Bernat Fenollar, as Mercury, explains the rules. It has 64 stanzas, one for each square of the board. Every piece stands for something. Mars's queen is Will. Venus's queen is Beauty, and her rooks are Shame. One rule in the poem reads: if the queen is lost, let the game be lost. After 21 moves, it is Mars's queen that gives checkmate. The chess historian Ricardo Calvo dated it to between 1470 and 1490. The manuscript was found in Barcelona in 1905, and seems to have vanished during the Spanish Civil War. A photograph of it survives. Valencia also printed a chess book in 1495, by Francesch Vicent, and not one copy survives. The last one known, Eales writes, probably burned at Montserrat in 1834. The oldest printed book on playing chess that does survive came from Salamanca, around 1497, by a young man studying there, Luis de Lucena. It teaches, in his words, the difference between the game which we now play, which is called of the queen, and the game which used to be played. He dedicated it to Prince Juan, the heir of Ferdinand and Isabella, who died in 1497, only a few months after his wedding. That leaves the question in our title: why did the queen, of all the pieces, get the power? In her book Birth of the Chess Queen, Marilyn Yalom tied the queen's rise to the reign of Isabella of Castile, in Spain, where most of the early evidence comes from. It is an attractive idea. But in the end, a review in Kirkus said, she is left with attractive correlations, and no smoking causative guns. Eales had no patience for the idea that the new queen reflected the emancipation of women. Which women, he asked, and what sort of emancipation? And the same Lucena, he pointed out, bound into his chess book a Discourse on Love that its modern editor called the most extensive anti-feminist document of early Spanish literature. He saw the new chess as the end of a long run of attempts to speed up a slow game, though the new queen herself had no medieval model. Where it began is argued too. Murray leaned to Italy, Calvo to Valencia, and Eales noted that almost all the reliable early evidence is linked with Spain or Portugal. What is certain is the speed. By 1510, Eales writes, the new chess was well entrenched in Spain, Italy and probably France. So what do we really have, after a thousand years of chess? A game first recorded in India and Persia around the year 600, probably born in northern India, though China has its defenders. Around it grew a great many legends. And a queen who moved one square for some nine hundred years, until players at the end of the 1400s let her loose. Chess began as a picture of an Indian army, became a Persian riddle and a Baghdad art, and only a little over five hundred years ago became the game we play. If you could play one game of the old chess, with its one-step counsellor and its jumping elephants, would you try it, or is it too slow for you? And why do you think the queen, of all the pieces, got the power: a reigning queen in Spain, or just players who wanted a faster game?
