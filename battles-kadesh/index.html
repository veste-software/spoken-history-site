Ramesses II Said He Won the Battle of Kadesh Alone

I am all alone, and none other is with me. My soldiers have forsaken me. A pharaoh says that, in the poem he had carved on his temple walls. Ramesses II, at the battle of Kadesh, in Syria. In our calendar, about 1274 B.C. About, because ancient reigns are hard to pin to our years. A book of 1927 put it some twenty years earlier. He tells it as a victory he won almost single-handed, against the Hittites and all their allies. But on the same walls, his own sculptors carved the troops who came to save him. And years later, the brother of the king he fought wrote to him, and the two were still arguing about who had won. So did Ramesses win at Kadesh? Or was he saved?

Two men on the road

It began on a road in Syria, in the fifth year of his reign. His army marched in four divisions, named after gods: Amun, Re, Ptah and Sutekh. The king rode ahead with Amun. Near a town called Shabtuna, two Bedouin, Shasu in the Egyptian text, came to him. They said the Hittite king was far to the north, in the land of Aleppo, and that he feared to come south. Ramesses' own official record adds: they said it falsely. The Hittite king had sent them to spy out where the pharaoh was. Believing them, Ramesses pushed on, and made camp on the northwest side of Kadesh, with only the division of Amun.

The beaten scouts

He sat on a throne of gold. Behind his tent, the camp relief shows a lion. The Egyptologist James Breasted took it for the king's pet. In 1906, he found no basis for the story that it fought beside him. Then a scout brought in two prisoners: scouts of the Hittite king. The relief shows what happened next. Its caption reads: They are beating them, to make them tell where the wretched chief of Kheta is. They told. The Hittite army was standing, drawn up for battle, behind Kadesh the Deceitful. The king called his princes, and blamed his governors and officials. Every day, he said, they had told him the enemy was in Aleppo. The princes agreed. It is a great fault, they said.

The ambush

While they talked, the blow fell to the south. Hittite chariots came out from the south side of the city, and cut through the division of Re in its middle, while they were marching, without knowing. On the reliefs, a Hittite chariot carries three men. An Egyptian chariot carries two. The broken division fled north, into the king's half built camp, and the enemy came with them. The Egyptian texts say 2,500 chariots closed around him. No Hittite text we have gives a number. Across the river, the Hittite king held back a reserve of warriors. 8,000, says the caption at the Ramesseum. 9,000, says the one at Abu Simbel.

Did you know? The four ancient drawings of the battlefield do not agree. “In no way can any of the four ancient drawings of this battlefield be made to coincide with the data of the inscriptions,” James Breasted wrote in 1906. “This is not remarkable when we remember that they do not coincide with each other.” (Ancient Records of Egypt III, p. 153)

The poem's battle

This is the moment the poem was written for. He takes his coat of mail. His two horses are named Victory in Thebes and Mut is Content. And he prays. What is it then, my father Amun? Hath a father indeed forgotten his son? Amun answers, the poem says: Forward, forward! I am with thee. His charioteer, Menna, panics. "We stand alone in the midst of the foe ... save us, Ramesses." Steady, steady thine heart, my charioteer, says the king. And he charges, six times. Afterwards, he scolds his army for cowardice. And he vows that his two horses will eat their feed in front of him, every day, back in his palace. The poem was long called the Poem of Pentaur. Pentaur was a scribe who copied it onto papyrus. The poet is unknown.

Did you know? In the poem, the enemy explain the king to each other: “This is no man that is among us, he is Sutekh, great of strength; Baal is in his limbs.” (tr. A. M. Blackman, in A. Erman, The Literature of the Ancient Egyptians, 1927, p. 265)

What the walls also show

Now look at the walls again. One relief scene is captioned: "The arrival of the recruits of Pharaoh ... from the land of Amor." They catch the Hittites breaking into the camp, and cut them off. They left not a single survivor, the caption says. Ramesses had sent his vizier south to hurry up the rest of the army. On the Luxor relief, among the troops coming up, a fragment of text reads: Arrival of the vizier. So the king was not alone for long. Who were these recruits? The word in the Egyptian text is Nearin. Breasted could not match them to any of the four divisions. The historian Eduard Meyer suggested they had landed on the coast. We do not know. The same walls show the prince of Aleppo, hurled into the river, the caption says, and now held upside down by his own soldiers to empty him of water.

Did you know? The official record ends the king's account with an oath: “I charged all countries, while I was alone, my infantry and my chariotry having forsaken me. Not one among them stood to turn about. I swear, as Re loves me, as my father, Atum, favors me, that, as for every matter which his majesty has stated, I did it in truth, in the presence of my infantry and my chariotry.” (tr. Breasted, 1906, sec. 327)

Who kept Kadesh?

So how did it end? Kadesh was not taken. Even the poem never claims it, Breasted noted. The poem adds a second day of battle, and a Hittite letter begging for mercy: Give us breath! Breasted doubted that second day. The Hittite side kept its own version. Decades later, a Hittite treaty recalls how Muwattalli and the king of Egypt fought over the land of Amurru. Its verdict fits in three words: Muwattalli defeated him. The historian Trevor Bryce writes that the Egyptians were forced to retreat south, with the Hittite army in pursuit, as far as the region of Damascus. His verdict: on the day, battle honours were fairly even. In the long run, he writes, the Hittites were the victors, in the land they gained. And Ramesses? He had his version carved, Bryce counts, on no fewer than five temples.

The letter war

The Hittite king at Kadesh was Muwattalli. His younger brother, Hattusili, fought there too. After Muwattalli died, Hattusili took the throne from his own nephew. The nephew fled, and found refuge in Egypt. Hattusili sent Ramesses a hostile letter about the nephew. Ramesses' reply quotes a line of it back. Have you forgotten the days of the enemies from the Land of Hatti? Ramesses would not let it pass. His clay letter, found at the Hittite capital, Hattusa, answers: But I brought about his defeat quite single-handed, although my army was not with me, and my chariots were not with me. After that, Bryce notes, the surviving letters never mention Kadesh again.

The silver tablet

In the 21st year of Ramesses, two Hittite envoys reached him with a silver tablet. It was a treaty. Egypt carved its copy at Karnak and at the Ramesseum. Clay versions turned up in the Hittite archive at Hattusa. It never names Kadesh. The Hittite king mentions the war in one clause: "in the time of [Muwattalli] ... my brother, he fought w[ith Ramses II], the great ruler of Egypt." It promises good peace and good brotherhood forever, and calls on the thousand gods of each land to destroy whoever breaks it. And it says that a man sent back across the border must not be harmed: let no injury be done to his eyes, to his ears, to his mouth, nor to his feet. A copy of the clay version is mounted today at the entrance to the Security Council of the United Nations, in New York.

The queen and the bride

On the other side of the silver tablet, the Egyptian copy describes the seal of the Hittite queen, Puduhepa. Hattusili's own account says that when he came back from the land of Egypt, he married her. Ramesses' queen, Nefertari, called her my sister, and sent her a gold necklace of twelve strands. Puduhepa wrote to Ramesses herself. When he asked why her daughter, his promised bride, was so long in coming, she wrote back: the storehouse of Hatti has been burnt out. In his 34th year, the princess arrived. Ramesses had it carved as if Hatti were paying him tribute.

Did you know? When the Hittite princess finally reached Egypt, the pharaoh's recorded reaction was that she was “beautiful in the heart of His Majesty, and he loved her more than anything” (as quoted by Trevor Bryce, Letters of the Great Kings, 2003, p. 109).

Did he win?

So, did Ramesses win at Kadesh? On his walls, yes. In the evidence, two Bedouin led him into a trap, and he held on until help arrived. His stand saved his army from a rout, Bryce thinks. But the Hittites kept Kadesh.

Quick answers

What happened at the Battle of Kadesh?

In the fifth year of his reign, about 1274 B.C., Ramesses II marched into Syria in four divisions named after gods: Amun, Re, Ptah and Sutekh. Near Shabtuna two Bedouin told him the Hittite king was far to the north, in the land of Aleppo; his own official record adds that they said it falsely. He made camp on the northwest side of Kadesh with only the division of Amun. Two captured Hittite scouts, beaten to make them tell, revealed that the Hittite army stood drawn up behind the city. Hittite chariots then cut through the division of Re on the march and followed the fleeing men into the king's half built camp. The king held on until help arrived: his own reliefs show the recruits from the land of Amor catching the Hittites in the camp.

Did Ramesses II win the Battle of Kadesh?

On his walls, yes: his poem tells it as a victory he won almost single-handed. In the evidence, Kadesh was not taken; even the poem never claims it, Breasted noted. A Hittite treaty decades later says "Muwattalli defeated him". The historian Trevor Bryce writes that the Egyptians were forced to retreat south, as far as the region of Damascus; on the day, battle honours were fairly even, and in the long run the Hittites were the victors, in the land they gained. His stand saved his army from a rout, Bryce thinks. But the Hittites kept Kadesh.

When was the Battle of Kadesh?

In the fifth year of Ramesses II, which is about 1274 B.C. in our calendar. About, because ancient reigns are hard to pin to our years: a book of 1927 put it some twenty years earlier.

Who were the Nearin who saved Ramesses at Kadesh?

A relief caption on his own walls reads: "The arrival of the recruits of Pharaoh, from the land of Amor." They caught the Hittites breaking into the camp and cut them off. The word for these recruits in the Egyptian text is Nearin. Breasted could not match them to any of the four divisions, and the historian Eduard Meyer suggested they had landed on the coast. We do not know who they were.

What does the Egyptian Hittite peace treaty say about Kadesh?

Nothing by name. In the twenty-first year of Ramesses, two Hittite envoys brought him a silver tablet with the treaty. Egypt carved its copy at Karnak and at the Ramesseum, and clay versions turned up in the Hittite archive at Hattusa. The Hittite king mentions the war in one clause, about the time of Muwattalli, his brother. The treaty promises good peace and good brotherhood forever, and says that a man sent back across the border must not be harmed. A copy of the clay version is mounted today at the entrance to the Security Council of the United Nations, in New York.

Where it happened

  • Kadesh on the Orontes (Tell Nebi Mend), Syria (1274 B.C.): The city of the battle, about 1274 B.C.; today the mound of Tell Nebi Mend. Ramesses camped on its northwest side with the division of Amun; the Hittite army stood drawn up behind the city. Kadesh was not taken. Where exactly the camp and the fighting were is not known.
  • Aleppo (1274 B.C.): Where the two Bedouin said the Hittite king was, far to the north; Ramesses' own record says they said it falsely.
  • Damascus (1274 B.C.): Trevor Bryce writes that after the battle the Egyptians were forced to retreat south, with the Hittite army in pursuit, as far as the region of Damascus.
  • Hattusa, the Hittite capital (Bogazkale, Turkey) (1259 B.C.): The Hittite archive where clay versions of the treaty and Ramesses' letter about Kadesh were found.
  • Abu Simbel, the great temple of Ramesses II (1274 B.C.): The Kadesh relief on the north wall of the great hall: the camp, the council, the beaten scouts. Its caption gives the Hittite reserve as 9,000.
  • The Ramesseum, Thebes (1274 B.C.): Ramesses' temple at Thebes. Its caption gives the Hittite reserve as 8,000; Egypt carved its copy of the treaty here and at Karnak.
  • Luxor temple, Thebes (1274 B.C.): Kadesh reliefs on the first pylon; among the troops coming up, a fragment of text reads: Arrival of the vizier.
  • Karnak, Thebes (1259 B.C.): The Egyptian copy of the treaty of Ramesses' twenty-first year is carved on a wall here.
  • Abydos, temple of Ramesses II (1274 B.C.): One of the temples where Breasted (1906) records Kadesh texts or reliefs.

Egypt carved its copy at Karnak and at the Ramesseum. Clay versions turned up in the Hittite archive at Hattusa. It never names Kadesh. The Hittite king mentions the war in one clause: "in the time of [Muwattalli] ... my brother, he fought w[ith Ramses II], the great ruler of Egypt." It promises good peace and good brotherhood forever, and calls on the thousand gods of each land to destroy whoever breaks it. And it says that a man sent back across the border must not be harmed: let no injury be done to his eyes, to his ears, to his mouth, nor to his feet. A copy of the clay version is mounted today at the entrance to the Security Council of the United Nations, in New York. On the other side of the silver tablet, the Egyptian copy describes the seal of the Hittite queen, Puduhepa. Hattusili's own account says that when he came back from the land of Egypt, he married her. Ramesses' queen, Nefertari, called her my sister, and sent her a gold necklace of twelve strands. Puduhepa wrote to Ramesses herself. When he asked why her daughter, his promised bride, was so long in coming, she wrote back: the storehouse of Hatti has been burnt out. In his 34th year, the princess arrived. Ramesses had it carved as if Hatti were paying him tribute. So, did Ramesses win at Kadesh? On his walls, yes. In the evidence, two Bedouin led him into a trap, and he held on until help arrived. His stand saved his army from a rout, Bryce thinks. But the Hittites kept Kadesh. Ramesses at Kadesh: a brave king who saved his army, or a careless one who nearly lost it? And the Nearin who came into his camp: his own troops from the coast, or someone else?
