Knight's Armour: From Mail to Plate

In 1361, the dead of a battle were thrown into pits, many still wearing their armour. More than five hundred years later, archaeologists dug them up. Visby, on the island of Gotland. 27 July 1361. The army of the Danish king, Valdemar Atterdag, meets the farmers of Gotland outside the town wall. The graves show who was among them. Boys, old men, and men with diseases and old injuries. About 1,800 died, the museum in Stockholm says. It is an estimate.

The mass graves

Four mass graves were found between 1905 and 1930. In three, archaeologists found nearly 1,200 people. Armour was valuable, and the dead were usually stripped. Here, a Danish encyclopedia says, the burial was so hasty that it stayed on. The museum thinks valuables were probably taken. Yet one leather purse, with 385 coins, was left in a grave. Those graves hold this whole story at once. Mail hoods of a kind common in the late 1200s. And plates that were, for their time, the latest thing. Because armour did not jump from mail to plate. Plate was added piece by piece, for more than a century. Today: what was dug up, what it weighed, and what a treadmill showed.

Did you know? The museum in Stockholm thinks the dead of Visby were probably plundered of everything valuable. But in one of the graves lay a leather purse with 385 coins, and it stayed there (Swedish History Museum, "Mannen i montern"; our translation from the Swedish).

How mail was made

It starts with mail. Small iron rings, linked together, most of them closed with a tiny rivet. One mail shirt in London's Victoria and Albert Museum alternates rows of riveted rings with rows of solid ones. How many rings? The London Museum gives an average of 28,000, for a shirt like its own. A long one, says the Royal Armouries, could take up to a hundred days to make. Very few were signed. One shirt in New York carries two brass rings inscribed with a maker's name. Bechler. Mail stops a cut well. The Royal Armouries says it did less against arrows and maces. And it needs a quilted coat underneath. And Dirk Breiding, writing for the Met, calls chain mail a modern phrase that says the same thing twice.

Did you know? Very few mail garments were signed by their makers. A German mail shirt of the 1400s in the Met has two brass links inscribed with the name BECHLER (The Met, 14.25.1540). And "chain mail", writes Dirk Breiding, is a modern phrase that says the same thing twice.

Plates over mail: the 1300s

By 1200, a mounted warrior's legs were in mail too. Then came the plates. Small ones first: caps for the knees, of hardened leather or of steel. The Royal Armouries puts a revolution between 1325 and 1340. In a village church in Cambridgeshire, a brass shows Sir John de Creke, who died around 1328. His surcoat is cut short in front, so you can count the layers. A padded jacket, mail, a coat of plates. Plates on his limbs. Caps on his knees. That short surcoat was a brief fashion, it seems almost only in the British Isles. Scholars of armour love it for what it reveals. But the brass was probably ordered by his son, in the 1340s. So, writes Robert Kinsey, its armour cannot show anything Sir John actually wore. A monument dates the man. It does not date the armour.

Did you know? The brass of Sir John de Creke, who died around 1328, shows armour of the late 1330s and 1340s. In Robert Kinsey's words, it "cannot have been based on anything that Sir John would have actually worn" (Transactions of the Monumental Brass Society XXI, 2020).

The coat of plates

On the body, early plates went inside cloth. The Royal Armouries calls it a pair of plates: iron plates riveted inside fabric. One coat of plates from the Visby graves carries little symbols. The museum links them to the arms of a noble family of Friesland, the Roorda. It could have belonged to one of two brothers, Bavo or Schelto. What became of them, nobody knows. Why add plates at all? The Royal Armouries suggests the crossbow may have been one reason: mail could no longer stop it. The London Museum adds another. Better metalworking made plate cheaper.

The breastplate and the Black Prince

In the late 1300s, the solid breastplate appears. Soon after, a rest for the lance. Edward, the Black Prince, died in 1376. He asked to be shown “fully armed in plate of war”. His gilded effigy at Canterbury does exactly that. Above the tomb hang a helm, a shield and gauntlets. They are copies from the 1950s. The originals are in the crypt. The art historian Jessica Barker says the effigy copies his own armour down to the position of the rivets. Her study dates it about ten years after his death. Others think it was made soon after.

Did you know? The helm, shield and gauntlets hanging above the Black Prince's tomb are copies made in the 1950s. The originals are in the crypt (Canterbury Historical and Archaeological Society).

Full plate armour by about 1420

By about 1420, the work was done. Plate, from head to foot. The Met thinks it was probably first made in Milan, around 1400. Italian armour was usually lopsided on purpose. The left side, which met the enemy first, got the bigger plates. And from around 1400, no complete armour survives at all. The Met's armour in that style was put together in the 1920s, from pieces found in a ruined fortress in Greece. The earliest near-complete plate harness, says Ralph Moffat of Glasgow Museums, is the Avant armour in Glasgow. Even there, the helmet and the right gauntlet are later. The left one is a twentieth century copy.

How heavy was it, and what did it cost?

So how heavy was it? Breiding gives 20 to 25 kilos for a whole field armour. The helmet alone, 2 to 4. Less, he notes, than a firefighter's kit with oxygen gear. And spread over the whole body. The Royal Armouries says 60 to 70 pounds. And it was dear. In 1374, an English knight's whole armoury was valued at over £16. Five to eight years' rent for a London merchant's house. A single helmet was worth a cow.

Did you know? In 1374 an English knight's whole armoury was valued at over £16, about five to eight years' rent for a London merchant's house, and a single helmet, a bascinet, was worth a cow (Breiding, the Met, 2004; the 1374 record itself was not read by us).

The crane myth

Then there is the crane that winched knights onto their horses. Breiding thinks it began as a joke in the late 1800s. A film made it famous: Laurence Olivier's Henry V, in 1944. His historical advisers protested, the Master of the Armouries at the Tower of London among them. A knight in full armour, Breiding writes, was expected to mount and dismount unaided. And a marshal of France, Boucicaut, is said to have climbed the underside of a ladder in full armour, with only his hands.

Did you know? The knight winched onto his horse by a crane seems to have begun as a joke in the late 1800s. Laurence Olivier put it in his film Henry V in 1944, despite the protests of his historical advisers, among them Sir James Mann, Master of the Armouries at the Tower of London (Breiding, the Met, 2004).

Walking in armour: the treadmill study

But light is not the same as easy. A study published in 2011 put four fight interpreters from the Royal Armouries in Leeds on a treadmill. They wore made-to-measure copies of armour from the mid to late 1400s. About 35 kilos, with the padded jacket. That made them about 40% heavier. But walking, they used more than twice the energy, at the same speed. Running, almost twice. So it is not just the weight. Every step swings steel on the legs. And they breathed fast and shallow. The authors think the armour limited how deeply they could breathe in. Could that have decided battles? The authors say it may have contributed, at Agincourt for one. But Daniel Jaquet and his colleagues point out that these copies are later than the armour of Agincourt. And in their own test, with one man, the armour limited how his joints moved only a little.

Did you know? In the treadmill study, the armour made the four men only about 40% heavier, yet walking cost them 2.1 to 2.3 times the energy at the same speed. Most of the extra cost, the authors found, came from swinging the armoured limbs and from impaired breathing (Askew, Formenti and Minetti, Proceedings of the Royal Society B, 2012).

Armour against guns: proof marks

So by 1420 the knight was closed in steel. The same century made the great guns of our episode on the Dardanelles Gun. Armour had long been tested. An Italian merchant, Francesco Datini, listed helmets and breastplates of proof, and of half proof. And the sources, says Moffat, show armour proofed with crossbow bolts. Later the test was a bullet. An Italian armour at the Met, from around 1620, was shot at before it was finished. The dents were left in, as a guarantee. It weighs over 39 kilos, one of the heaviest field armours known. By then, Breiding writes, armour got heavier to stop bullets, and smaller. Only the head, the body and the hands stayed in steel.

What we really know

So what do we know? The order: mail, plates over it, coats of plates, the breastplate, full plate by about 1420. What we do not know is exactly why. The crossbow and cheaper plate are offered as reasons, with care. And we cannot weigh the armour of Agincourt. Not one complete armour of that time survives.

Verdict

So that is the knight's armour. Not a jump, but more than a century of adding plate to mail. And some of the best evidence comes from the dead of Visby, most of them farmers.

Over to you

Fighting on foot all day, would you pick mail and a padded coat, or plate that more than doubled the cost of walking in one study? Sir John de Creke's brass shows armour from after his death. Why would his son choose the newest?

Quick answers

How heavy was a medieval suit of armour?

Dirk Breiding, writing for the Met, gives 20 to 25 kilos for a whole field armour, and 2 to 4 for the helmet alone: less, he notes, than a firefighter's kit with oxygen gear, and spread over the whole body. The Royal Armouries says 60 to 70 pounds. Later, armour got heavier to stop bullets: an Italian armour at the Met from around 1620 weighs over 39 kilos, one of the heaviest field armours known.

When did plate armour replace mail?

Not in one jump. Plate was added to mail piece by piece, for more than a century. By 1200 a mounted warrior's legs were in mail too; then came caps for the knees, of hardened leather or of steel. The Royal Armouries puts a revolution between 1325 and 1340. Early body plates went inside cloth, as a coat of plates; the solid breastplate appears in the late 1300s; and by about 1420 plate covered a man from head to foot. Exactly why is not known: the crossbow and cheaper plate are offered as reasons, with care.

Why were the dead of Visby buried in their armour?

After the battle outside Visby on 27 July 1361, the dead were thrown into pits, many still wearing their armour. Armour was valuable, and the dead were usually stripped; a Danish encyclopedia says the burial was so hasty that it stayed on. The museum in Stockholm thinks valuables were probably taken. Yet one leather purse, with 385 coins, was left in a grave.

Did knights need a crane to get onto their horses?

Probably not. Dirk Breiding of the Met thinks the crane began as a joke in the late 1800s, and a film made it famous: Laurence Olivier's Henry V, in 1944, over the protests of his historical advisers, the Master of the Armouries at the Tower of London among them. A knight in full armour, Breiding writes, was expected to mount and dismount unaided.

How hard was it to walk in armour?

A study published in 2011 put four fight interpreters from the Royal Armouries in Leeds on a treadmill, in made to measure copies of armour from the mid to late 1400s: about 35 kilos, with the padded jacket. That made them about 40% heavier, but walking at the same speed they used more than twice the energy, and running almost twice. Every step swings steel on the legs, and they breathed fast and shallow. The authors say this may have contributed to the outcome of battles, at Agincourt for one. Daniel Jaquet and his colleagues point out that the copies are later than the armour of Agincourt, and in their own test, with one man, the armour limited how his joints moved only a little.

Where it happened

  • Visby, Gotland (A.D. 1361): On 27 July 1361 the army of the Danish king Valdemar Atterdag met the farmers of Gotland outside the town wall; four mass graves were found there between 1905 and 1930, nearly 1,200 people in three of them (Swedish History Museum). The exact positions of the graves are not shown.
  • Westley Waterless, Cambridgeshire (A.D. 1340): The brass of Sir John de Creke, who died around 1328; probably ordered by his son in the 1340s, so its armour is later than the man (Kinsey 2020).
  • Canterbury Cathedral (A.D. 1376): The gilded effigy of Edward, the Black Prince, who died in 1376; the helm, shield and gauntlets above the tomb are 1950s copies, the originals are in the crypt.
  • Milan (A.D. 1400): Where complete plate armour was probably first made, around 1400, the Met thinks.
  • Chalcis, Euboea (A.D. 1400): Pieces found in the ruins of the Venetian fortress here were put together in the 1920s into the Met's armour in the style of about 1400.
  • Glasgow : The Avant armour, the earliest near complete plate harness in the words of Ralph Moffat of Glasgow Museums; its helmet and right gauntlet are later, the left a twentieth century copy.
  • Royal Armouries, Leeds (A.D. 2011): Four fight interpreters from the Royal Armouries walked and ran on a treadmill in copies of armour from the mid to late 1400s (Askew, Formenti and Minetti, published 2011).
  • Agincourt (A.D. 1415): The treadmill study's authors say the cost of moving in armour may have contributed to the outcome there; Jaquet and colleagues point out the copies are later than the armour of Agincourt.
  • Prato : Home of the merchant Francesco Datini, who listed helmets and breastplates of proof and of half proof (Moffat 2024).

    Transcript

    Read the full narration

    Knight's Armour. Buried still wearing it. In 1361, the dead of a battle were thrown into pits, many still wearing their armour. More than five hundred years later, archaeologists dug them up. Visby, on the island of Gotland. 27 July 1361. The army of the Danish king, Valdemar Atterdag, meets the farmers of Gotland outside the town wall. The graves show who was among them. Boys, old men, and men with diseases and old injuries. About 1,800 died, the museum in Stockholm says. It is an estimate. Four mass graves were found between 1905 and 1930. In three, archaeologists found nearly 1,200 people. Armour was valuable, and the dead were usually stripped. Here, a Danish encyclopedia says, the burial was so hasty that it stayed on. The museum thinks valuables were probably taken. Yet one leather purse, with 385 coins, was left in a grave. Those graves hold this whole story at once. Mail hoods of a kind common in the late 1200s. And plates that were, for their time, the latest thing. Because armour did not jump from mail to plate. Plate was added piece by piece, for more than a century. Today: what was dug up, what it weighed, and what a treadmill showed. It starts with mail. Small iron rings, linked together, most of them closed with a tiny rivet. One mail shirt in London's Victoria and Albert Museum alternates rows of riveted rings with rows of solid ones. How many rings? The London Museum gives an average of 28,000, for a shirt like its own. A long one, says the Royal Armouries, could take up to a hundred days to make. Very few were signed. One shirt in New York carries two brass rings inscribed with a maker's name. Bechler. Mail stops a cut well. The Royal Armouries says it did less against arrows and maces. And it needs a quilted coat underneath. And Dirk Breiding, writing for the Met, calls chain mail a modern phrase that says the same thing twice. By 1200, a mounted warrior's legs were in mail too. Then came the plates. Small ones first: caps for the knees, of hardened leather or of steel. The Royal Armouries puts a revolution between 1325 and 1340. In a village church in Cambridgeshire, a brass shows Sir John de Creke, who died around 1328. His surcoat is cut short in front, so you can count the layers. A padded jacket, mail, a coat of plates. Plates on his limbs. Caps on his knees. That short surcoat was a brief fashion, it seems almost only in the British Isles. Scholars of armour love it for what it reveals. But the brass was probably ordered by his son, in the 1340s. So, writes Robert Kinsey, its armour cannot show anything Sir John actually wore. A monument dates the man. It does not date the armour. On the body, early plates went inside cloth. The Royal Armouries calls it a pair of plates: iron plates riveted inside fabric. One coat of plates from the Visby graves carries little symbols. The museum links them to the arms of a noble family of Friesland, the Roorda. It could have belonged to one of two brothers, Bavo or Schelto. What became of them, nobody knows. Why add plates at all? The Royal Armouries suggests the crossbow may have been one reason: mail could no longer stop it. The London Museum adds another. Better metalworking made plate cheaper. In the late 1300s, the solid breastplate appears. Soon after, a rest for the lance. Edward, the Black Prince, died in 1376. He asked to be shown “fully armed in plate of war”. His gilded effigy at Canterbury does exactly that. Above the tomb hang a helm, a shield and gauntlets. They are copies from the 1950s. The originals are in the crypt. The art historian Jessica Barker says the effigy copies his own armour down to the position of the rivets. Her study dates it about ten years after his death. Others think it was made soon after. By about 1420, the work was done. Plate, from head to foot. The Met thinks it was probably first made in Milan, around 1400. Italian armour was usually lopsided on purpose. The left side, which met the enemy first, got the bigger plates. And from around 1400, no complete armour survives at all. The Met's armour in that style was put together in the 1920s, from pieces found in a ruined fortress in Greece. The earliest near-complete plate harness, says Ralph Moffat of Glasgow Museums, is the Avant armour in Glasgow. Even there, the helmet and the right gauntlet are later. The left one is a twentieth century copy. If you are enjoying this type of content, please don't forget to subscribe. So how heavy was it? Breiding gives 20 to 25 kilos for a whole field armour. The helmet alone, 2 to 4. Less, he notes, than a firefighter's kit with oxygen gear. And spread over the whole body. The Royal Armouries says 60 to 70 pounds. And it was dear. In 1374, an English knight's whole armoury was valued at over £16. Five to eight years' rent for a London merchant's house. A single helmet was worth a cow. Then there is the crane that winched knights onto their horses. Breiding thinks it began as a joke in the late 1800s. A film made it famous: Laurence Olivier's Henry V, in 1944. His historical advisers protested, the Master of the Armouries at the Tower of London among them. A knight in full armour, Breiding writes, was expected to mount and dismount unaided. And a marshal of France, Boucicaut, is said to have climbed the underside of a ladder in full armour, with only his hands. But light is not the same as easy. A study published in 2011 put four fight interpreters from the Royal Armouries in Leeds on a treadmill. They wore made-to-measure copies of armour from the mid to late 1400s. About 35 kilos, with the padded jacket. That made them about 40% heavier. But walking, they used more than twice the energy, at the same speed. Running, almost twice. So it is not just the weight. Every step swings steel on the legs. And they breathed fast and shallow. The authors think the armour limited how deeply they could breathe in. Could that have decided battles? The authors say it may have contributed, at Agincourt for one. But Daniel Jaquet and his colleagues point out that these copies are later than the armour of Agincourt. And in their own test, with one man, the armour limited how his joints moved only a little. So by 1420 the knight was closed in steel. The same century made the great guns of our last episode. Armour had long been tested. An Italian merchant, Francesco Datini, listed helmets and breastplates of proof, and of half proof. And the sources, says Moffat, show armour proofed with crossbow bolts. Later the test was a bullet. An Italian armour at the Met, from around 1620, was shot at before it was finished. The dents were left in, as a guarantee. It weighs over 39 kilos, one of the heaviest field armours known. By then, Breiding writes, armour got heavier to stop bullets, and smaller. Only the head, the body and the hands stayed in steel. So what do we know? The order: mail, plates over it, coats of plates, the breastplate, full plate by about 1420. What we do not know is exactly why. The crossbow and cheaper plate are offered as reasons, with care. And we cannot weigh the armour of Agincourt. Not one complete armour of that time survives. So that is the knight's armour. Not a jump, but more than a century of adding plate to mail. And some of the best evidence comes from the dead of Visby, most of them farmers. Two questions for you. Fighting on foot all day, would you pick mail and a padded coat, or plate that more than doubled the cost of walking in one study? And Sir John de Creke's brass shows armour from after his death.
