The Lewis Chessmen: Why the Warders Bite Their Shields

Four of these little ivory warriors are biting their own shields. Someone carved them that way, about eight hundred years ago. They are chess pieces. The Lewis chessmen. The biters stand where your rook stands. But this rook is no castle. He is a soldier on foot, with a sword and a shield. So why carve a chess piece chewing its shield? The usual answer comes from an old book written in Iceland. But the pieces argue back.

They were found on the Isle of Lewis, in the Outer Hebrides of Scotland, in 1831. The British Museum has called them the largest single surviving group of objects from the period that were made purely for recreational purposes. So let's look at the evidence.

93 pieces, at least four sets

There are 93 pieces. 78 of them are chessmen. 8 kings, 8 queens, 16 bishops, 15 knights, 12 warders and 19 pawns. The rest are 14 flat discs for another board game, and one ivory buckle. So this is not one chess set. It is at least four. Against four full sets, one knight and four warders are missing. And 44 pawns. The researchers David Caldwell, Mark Hall and Caroline Wilkinson think those were probably too broken for the finders to pick up. The biggest is about 10 centimetres tall. So many sets made Frederic Madden, the first scholar to publish them, think of a merchant's stock, lost in a shipwreck. Caldwell and his colleagues call that probably the least plausible story. Perhaps, they suggest, it was the prized possession of a local prince, nobleman or senior churchman.

Did you know? Against four full sets, 44 pawns are missing. Caldwell, Hall and Wilkinson think they were probably too broken for the finders to pick up.

Walrus ivory, and a red board?

Almost all are carved from walrus ivory. Five are sperm whale tooth. One walrus tusk, says National Museums Scotland, could give up to four pieces. The ivory probably came from Greenland. Ancient DNA from walrus skulls in medieval towns suggests Greenland may have been Europe's only supplier from the 1120s on. The chessmen were not part of that study. Turn a king around, and the back of his throne is richer than the front. Each player sees the backs of their own pieces. So that is where the carver has really gone to town, says the curator Alice Blackwell. And some of them were once red. In 1832, Madden wrote that part of them had been stained a dark red, beetroot colour. In Edinburgh, scientists have since found traces of mercury, from cinnabar, a red pigment. So the board, the British Museum suggests, may have been red and white, not black and white.

Did you know? The throne backs are carved more richly than the fronts. "After all, each player sees the backs of their own pieces", says Alice Blackwell, senior curator at National Museums Scotland: "The carver has really gone to town on the throne backs" (press release, 2024).

Found in 1831: a story that grew

How they were found is a story that grew in the telling. The first sure record is 11 April 1831, when they were shown in Edinburgh, at the Society of Antiquaries of Scotland. In June, a newspaper said that a peasant of Uig had found them while digging a sand bank. His name, Malcolm MacLeod, first appears in print some thirty years later. Later accounts add a small stone chamber like an oven. Later still come a grazing cow, and a sailor murdered for his treasure. Even the place is argued. Most accounts say the sands of Uig Bay. Caldwell and his colleagues see a strong possibility of Mealista, on the same coast. Within months the hoard was split. According to Neil Stratford, a former keeper at the British Museum, an Edinburgh dealer paid £30 for them. Ten pieces went quietly to a collector. The British Museum bought 82, for 80 guineas. Madden had knocked the price down from 100. The other eleven reached the national museum in Edinburgh in 1888.

Did you know? In October 1831, with the hoard in London at the British Museum, Madden showed the chessmen to the visiting Sir Walter Scott, who had never seen them (Neil Stratford, in Sotheby's catalogue, 2019).

When and where were they made?

When were they made? Nobody wrote it down. The date comes from style. The bishops wear their mitres with the two points at front and back, a fashion that began around the middle of the 1100s. The British Museum dates the pieces to about 1150 to 1200. Caldwell and his colleagues would move at least some of them later, into the early 1200s. So call them Norse, rather than Viking. The Historical Association puts the end of the Viking Age at about 1150. And Lewis then was Norwegian. The island belonged to the kingdom of Norway until the Treaty of Perth, in 1266.

Norway or Iceland?

Where were they made? Caldwell and his colleagues name an important Scandinavian centre, especially Trondheim, the seat of Norway's archbishop. In the late 1800s, an excavator in the ruins of a Trondheim church found burnt fragments of a small ivory figure. He took it for a Madonna. Only his drawing survives. In it, two archaeologists saw a queen of the Lewis type: the right hand rests against the cheek. But Iceland has its champion. Gudmundur Thorarinsson points to the saga of Bishop Páll of Skálholt. It says the bishop sent an archbishop a staff carved of tusk by Margrét the Adroit, the most skilled carver in Iceland. The saga never mentions chess. Thorarinsson himself concedes that his case is circumstantial. The historian Alex Woolf answered: not impossible, but a Norwegian explanation sounds more likely.

Berserkers? The saga and the pieces

Now, the shield biters. Four of the twelve warders have their teeth on the top edge of their shields. Madden, of the British Museum, also gave the warders their name. And he saw berserkers. His evidence was a history of the kings of Norway, Heimskringla, written in Iceland around 1220 to 1230, and usually credited to Snorri Sturluson. In the translation of Alison Finlay and Anthony Faulkes, Odin's men went without mail and were as wild as dogs or wolves, biting their shields, being as strong as bears or bulls. Neither fire nor iron took effect on them. That is called berserk fury. A perfect match? Look closer. The saga's berserkers went without mail. Three of the four chess biters seem to wear long coats of mail. Their faces show no obvious fury. They look like the other pieces. And the saga was written down decades after the British Museum's date for the pieces. Nothing carved on them says berserker. So Caldwell and his colleagues call a straightforward berserker reading far from certain. Perhaps a joke about berserkers, they suggest. Or a sign of frustration with a slow game. The archaeologist Neil Price, on the other hand, has cited them as a rare depiction of the berserker.

Did you know? Another Icelandic saga has a shield biter too. In Grettis saga, chapter 40, the berserk Snækollr "began to roar aloud, and bit the rim of his shield, and thrust it up into his mouth" (translation by William Morris and Eiríkr Magnússon). It is a story told in a saga, not a historical record.

The queens, and the carvers

The queens have a puzzle of their own. Every one sits with her right hand to her face. Neil MacGregor of the British Museum read it as intense thought and wise counsel. But to us, he said, they look comically glum. And who carved them? The forensic anthropologist Caroline Wilkinson compared their faces. Fifty fell into five groups, possibly the work of five craftsmen. One of them, the authors write, can be reckoned a genius. And one bishop was never finished. His hair is only blocked out.

Did you know? One of the queens has one side of her throne attached as a separate piece, "neatly held in place with four ivory rivets". Caldwell, Hall and Wilkinson think it is probably the work of the original craftsman (Medieval Archaeology 53, 2009, p. 188).

The warder sold in 2019

The British Museum says the first Harry Potter film, in 2001, used a replica of the Lewis set for its wizard's chess. And in 2019, one more warder turned up. In 1964, an Edinburgh antiques dealer had bought it for £5. His family kept it, for decades in a drawer. Sotheby's catalogued it as attributed to the Lewis chessmen workshop, probably Norwegian. It has lost its left eye. On 2 July 2019, it sold in London for £735,000, with the buyer's premium. But Sotheby's own catalogue printed a twist. One radiocarbon report dated its ivory to between 1328 and 1434. Later than the date usually given to the hoard, the catalogue says. Perhaps, it suggests, the whole hoard is about eighty years younger. No piece in the two museums had been radiocarbon dated, it added. So is this warder one of them? We do not know.

Did you know? The Victorians copied the pieces in plaster casts called "fictile ivories"; the Victoria and Albert Museum had 16 of them in 1872. And the chessmen have appeared as a giant Lego sculpture of over 90,000 bricks, made at the National Museum of Scotland (British Museum blog, 2017).

What we really have

So what do we really have? 93 pieces of ivory from the Norse world, probably carved in Norway, perhaps in Trondheim, around 1200. Buried on Lewis by someone we cannot name, for a reason we do not know.

Verdict

And the warders really do bite their shields. Berserkers, or a joke about them, the pieces cannot tell us.

Quick answers

Why do some of the Lewis chessmen bite their shields?

We do not know. Four of the twelve warders have their teeth on the top edge of their shields. Frederic Madden saw berserkers, from Heimskringla, a history of the kings of Norway written in Iceland around 1220 to 1230. But the saga's berserkers went without mail, and three of the four chess biters seem to wear long coats of mail. Their faces show no obvious fury, and nothing carved on them says berserker. Caldwell and his colleagues call a straightforward berserker reading far from certain: perhaps a joke about berserkers, or a sign of frustration with a slow game. The archaeologist Neil Price, on the other hand, has cited them as a rare depiction of the berserker.

Where were the Lewis chessmen made?

Probably in Norway, perhaps in Trondheim. Caldwell and his colleagues name an important Scandinavian centre, especially Trondheim, the seat of Norway's archbishop; in the drawing of a burnt ivory figure found in a Trondheim church, two archaeologists saw a queen of the Lewis type. Gudmundur Thorarinsson argues for Iceland, from the saga of Bishop Páll of Skálholt, which says the bishop sent an archbishop a staff carved of tusk by Margrét the Adroit, the most skilled carver in Iceland. But the saga never mentions chess and he concedes that his case is circumstantial. The historian Alex Woolf answered: not impossible, but a Norwegian explanation sounds more likely.

How many Lewis chessmen are there?

There are 93 pieces, and 78 of them are chessmen: 8 kings, 8 queens, 16 bishops, 15 knights, 12 warders and 19 pawns. The rest are 14 flat discs for another board game, and one ivory buckle. So this is not one chess set but at least four. The British Museum bought 82; the other eleven reached the national museum in Edinburgh in 1888.

How old are the Lewis chessmen, and are they Viking?

The date comes from style. The bishops wear their mitres with the two points at front and back, a fashion that began around the middle of the 1100s. The British Museum dates the pieces to about 1150 to 1200; Caldwell and his colleagues would move at least some of them later, into the early 1200s. So call them Norse, rather than Viking: the Historical Association puts the end of the Viking Age at about 1150. Lewis then belonged to the kingdom of Norway, until the Treaty of Perth in 1266.

Was the warder sold in 2019 one of the Lewis chessmen?

We do not know. An Edinburgh antiques dealer bought it in 1964 for £5, and his family kept it, for decades in a drawer. Sotheby's catalogued it as attributed to the Lewis chessmen workshop, probably Norwegian, and on 2 July 2019 it sold in London for £735,000 with the buyer's premium. But the catalogue also printed one radiocarbon report that dates its ivory to between 1328 and 1434, later than the date usually given to the hoard, and it added that no piece in the two museums had been radiocarbon dated.

Where it happened

  • Uig Bay, Isle of Lewis (A.D. 1831): Most accounts place the find on the sands of Uig Bay (the dunes at Ardroil). The exact find spot is not known.
  • Mealista, Isle of Lewis (A.D. 1831): On the same coast. Caldwell, Hall and Wilkinson (2009) see a strong possibility that the hoard was found here.
  • Trondheim (A.D. 1175): Seat of Norway's archbishop. Caldwell and colleagues name an important Scandinavian centre, especially Trondheim, as the likely place of making: a proposal, not a finding.
  • Skálholt (A.D. 1200): Seat of Bishop Páll in Iceland. Gudmundur Thorarinsson's Icelandic case points to his saga and Margrét the Adroit; he concedes it is circumstantial.
  • British Museum, London (A.D. 1831): Bought 82 pieces for 80 guineas after Madden knocked the price down from 100.
  • National Museum of Scotland, Edinburgh (A.D. 1888): The other eleven pieces arrived in 1888. The hoard was first shown at the Society of Antiquaries of Scotland in Edinburgh on 11 April 1831.

    Transcript

    Read the full narration

    The Lewis Chessmen. Why they bite their shields. Four of these little ivory warriors are biting their own shields. Someone carved them that way, about eight hundred years ago. They are chess pieces. The Lewis chessmen. The biters stand where your rook stands. But this rook is no castle. He is a soldier on foot, with a sword and a shield. So why carve a chess piece chewing its shield? The usual answer comes from an old book written in Iceland. But the pieces argue back. They were found on the Isle of Lewis, in the Outer Hebrides of Scotland, in 1831. The British Museum has called them the largest single surviving group of objects from the period that were made purely for recreational purposes. So let's look at the evidence. There are 93 pieces. 78 of them are chessmen. 8 kings, 8 queens, 16 bishops, 15 knights, 12 warders and 19 pawns. The rest are 14 flat discs for another board game, and one ivory buckle. So this is not one chess set. It is at least four. Against four full sets, one knight and four warders are missing. And 44 pawns. The researchers David Caldwell, Mark Hall and Caroline Wilkinson think those were probably too broken for the finders to pick up. The biggest is about 10 centimetres tall. So many sets made Frederic Madden, the first scholar to publish them, think of a merchant's stock, lost in a shipwreck. Caldwell and his colleagues call that probably the least plausible story. Perhaps, they suggest, it was the prized possession of a local prince, nobleman or senior churchman. Almost all are carved from walrus ivory. Five are sperm whale tooth. One walrus tusk, says National Museums Scotland, could give up to four pieces. The ivory probably came from Greenland. Ancient DNA from walrus skulls in medieval towns suggests Greenland may have been Europe's only supplier from the 1120s on. The chessmen were not part of that study. Turn a king around, and the back of his throne is richer than the front. Each player sees the backs of their own pieces. So that is where the carver has really gone to town, says the curator Alice Blackwell. And some of them were once red. In 1832, Madden wrote that part of them had been stained a dark red, beetroot colour. In Edinburgh, scientists have since found traces of mercury, from cinnabar, a red pigment. So the board, the British Museum suggests, may have been red and white, not black and white. How they were found is a story that grew in the telling. The first sure record is 11 April 1831, when they were shown in Edinburgh, at the Society of Antiquaries of Scotland. In June, a newspaper said that a peasant of Uig had found them while digging a sand bank. His name, Malcolm MacLeod, first appears in print some thirty years later. Later accounts add a small stone chamber like an oven. Later still come a grazing cow, and a sailor murdered for his treasure. Even the place is argued. Most accounts say the sands of Uig Bay. Caldwell and his colleagues see a strong possibility of Mealista, on the same coast. Within months the hoard was split. According to Neil Stratford, a former keeper at the British Museum, an Edinburgh dealer paid £30 for them. Ten pieces went quietly to a collector. The British Museum bought 82, for 80 guineas. Madden had knocked the price down from 100. The other eleven reached the national museum in Edinburgh in 1888. When were they made? Nobody wrote it down. The date comes from style. The bishops wear their mitres with the two points at front and back, a fashion that began around the middle of the 1100s. The British Museum dates the pieces to about 1150 to 1200. Caldwell and his colleagues would move at least some of them later, into the early 1200s. So call them Norse, rather than Viking. The Historical Association puts the end of the Viking Age at about 1150. And Lewis then was Norwegian. The island belonged to the kingdom of Norway until the Treaty of Perth, in 1266. Where were they made? Caldwell and his colleagues name an important Scandinavian centre, especially Trondheim, the seat of Norway's archbishop. In the late 1800s, an excavator in the ruins of a Trondheim church found burnt fragments of a small ivory figure. He took it for a Madonna. Only his drawing survives. In it, two archaeologists saw a queen of the Lewis type: the right hand rests against the cheek. But Iceland has its champion. Gudmundur Thorarinsson points to the saga of Bishop Pall of Skalholt. It says the bishop sent an archbishop a staff carved of tusk by Margret the Adroit, the most skilled carver in Iceland. The saga never mentions chess. Thorarinsson himself concedes that his case is circumstantial. The historian Alex Woolf answered: not impossible, but a Norwegian explanation sounds more likely. If you are enjoying this type of content, please don't forget to subscribe. Now, the shield biters. Four of the twelve warders have their teeth on the top edge of their shields. Madden, of the British Museum, also gave the warders their name. And he saw berserkers. His evidence was a history of the kings of Norway, Heimskringla, written in Iceland around 1220 to 1230, and usually credited to Snorri Sturluson. In the translation of Alison Finlay and Anthony Faulkes, Odin's men went without mail and were as wild as dogs or wolves, biting their shields, being as strong as bears or bulls. Neither fire nor iron took effect on them. That is called berserk fury. A perfect match? Look closer. The saga's berserkers went without mail. Three of the four chess biters seem to wear long coats of mail. Their faces show no obvious fury. They look like the other pieces. And the saga was written down decades after the British Museum's date for the pieces. Nothing carved on them says berserker. So Caldwell and his colleagues call a straightforward berserker reading far from certain. Perhaps a joke about berserkers, they suggest. Or a sign of frustration with a slow game. The archaeologist Neil Price, on the other hand, has cited them as a rare depiction of the berserker. The queens have a puzzle of their own. Every one sits with her right hand to her face. Neil MacGregor of the British Museum read it as intense thought and wise counsel. But to us, he said, they look comically glum. And who carved them? The forensic anthropologist Caroline Wilkinson compared their faces. Fifty fell into five groups, possibly the work of five craftsmen. One of them, the authors write, can be reckoned a genius. And one bishop was never finished. His hair is only blocked out. The British Museum says the first Harry Potter film, in 2001, used a replica of the Lewis set for its wizard's chess. And in 2019, one more warder turned up. In 1964, an Edinburgh antiques dealer had bought it for £5. His family kept it, for decades in a drawer. Sotheby's catalogued it as attributed to the Lewis chessmen workshop, probably Norwegian. It has lost its left eye. On 2 July 2019, it sold in London for £735,000, with the buyer's premium. But Sotheby's own catalogue printed a twist. One radiocarbon report dated its ivory to between 1328 and 1434. Later than the date usually given to the hoard, the catalogue says. Perhaps, it suggests, the whole hoard is about eighty years younger. No piece in the two museums had been radiocarbon dated, it added. So is this warder one of them? We do not know. So what do we really have? 93 pieces of ivory from the Norse world, probably carved in Norway, perhaps in Trondheim, around 1200. Buried on Lewis by someone we cannot name, for a reason we do not know. And the warders really do bite their shields. Berserkers, or a joke about them, the pieces cannot tell us. Two questions for you. When you look at the shield biters, do you see berserkers, or a joke about berserkers? And the warder sold in 2019: one of the hoard, or a later piece?
