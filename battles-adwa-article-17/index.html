Battle of Adwa: The Treaty Line That Meant Two Things

One treaty. One article. Two languages. And they did not say the same thing. In the Italian text, the emperor of Ethiopia consents to use Italy's government for all his dealings with other powers. In the Amharic text, in one historian's almost literal rendering: it shall be possible for him to communicate with the assistance of the Italian government. A promise, or an option? Article 19 made both texts official, of the same authority. Italy told Europe it now spoke for Ethiopia. The emperor, Menelik, told Europe it did not. Seven years later, an Italian army marched by night toward a town called Adwa. So was Article 17 a trick, a slip of translation, or an excuse? And why did one name on a map matter so much that night?

The Treaty of Wuchale, 1889

First, the place where the two texts were signed. Wuchale, in Ethiopia, the second of May, 1889. Menelik had just claimed the crown. Italy held the Red Sea port of Massawa, and was pushing inland. Italy's envoy was Count Pietro Antonelli, and he came with gifts. Menelik's own chronicle explains them with a proverb. In our rendering: you lure the cat with milk, and the child with a ring. Antonelli brought what the king had ordered, it says, and things he had not asked for. Menelik lavished gold and elephant tusks on him. Of its twenty articles, number seventeen dealt with other powers.

Italy's notice to Europe

Five months later, that article went to work. On the twelfth of October, Italy's embassy in London sent it to Lord Salisbury, as a formal notice under the rules agreed in Berlin. The historian Carlo Giglio puts it plainly: the acknowledgement of that notice gave birth to the Italian protectorate. Menelik's chronicle, in our rendering: Antonelli's trick was discovered. He was writing everywhere that Ethiopia had placed itself under Italian protection. Menelik told the foreign governments that Ethiopia accepted no such protection. In 1893 he ordered coins with his own face, made at the Paris Mint. On the edge, a line from the Psalms: "Ethiopia stretches out her hands to God."

Did you know? Menelik's new coins, with his own face, were made at the Paris Mint, and his edict of 1893 puts a line from the Psalms on their edge: "Ethiopia stretches out her hands to God" (edict of 10 February 1893 and the chronicle, with de Coppet's notes; our English).

Trick or mistake? The historians

So who made one article say two things? Historians still argue. In 1964, Sven Rubenson blamed the Italian negotiator. Antonelli, Rubenson wrote, was not ignorant of Amharic, and had personally worded the Italian draft, which turned an offer to act as the emperor's postman into an obligation. The Amharic, with equal authority, never got that change. So for Rubenson the protectorate never had any validity at all. Carlo Giglio disagreed. Menelik refused an obligation, he wrote, and both men agreed on an option. The Amharic got it right. The Italian kept the old wording from Rome: consente, he consents. For Giglio, the prime minister, Francesco Crispi, made the trouble with that notice. A third historian, John Davis, calls it what could only have been a deliberate deception. Notice what they share: none finds a protectorate in the Amharic. They argue about blame, and intent.

Cartridges and a call to arms

Menelik did not go to war at once. He protested, and waited. He denounced the treaty only in February 1893. That, the chronicle's French editor notes, was just when two million cartridges, promised to him after an Italian mission, arrived. Davis agrees: he was waiting for the Italian weapons. In September 1895, he called the empire to arms. In our rendering of his chronicle: "since we must all die, I will not be grieved if I die." That winter, at Amba Alage, Major Pietro Toselli fell. Davis counts 1,500 African soldiers and 20 Italians dead with him. At Mekele, an Italian garrison surrendered when its water ran out.

Did you know? Menelik denounced the treaty in February 1893, just when two million cartridges, promised to him after an Italian mission, arrived, as the chronicle's French editor notes. John Davis agrees: he was waiting for the Italian weapons.

Crispi's telegram

By late February, both armies near Adwa were running out of supplies. The Italian commander, General Oreste Baratieri, counted about 16,700 fighting men. Menelik had, by different counts, 80,000 to 120,000. On the twenty-second of February, Rome signed a decree replacing Baratieri. He writes that it was kept from him. Three days later, the prime minister telegraphed the man his government had already replaced. In our rendering: This is a military consumption, not a war. Little skirmishes, in which we are always outnumbered. Heroism wasted for nothing. Consumption: the slow, wasting disease. Baratieri wrote that it filled his heart with disdain and bitterness. On the evening of the twenty-eighth, he asked his four senior generals: attack, or retreat? All were convinced retreat was impossible.

The night march

So they marched. Nine in the evening, the 29th of February, 1896. A leap day. Baratieri needed the moon to light the march, and gave the order late, because the camp was full of spies. Three brigades went ahead in the dark, a fourth behind. On the left was General Albertone, with the askari, Italy's African soldiers. His goal was a pass marked on the headquarters sketch as Chidane Meret. But on the ground, an Italian inquiry found, that name belonged to another place, six or seven kilometres further on. Albertone halted for an hour, then marched on. At dawn his brigade was far ahead, out of touch with the others, near the Ethiopian camp. One name. Two places. Whose fault? The inquiry called it a probable confusion, but blamed the brigade. Davis blames bad maps.

Did you know? The Italian army set out at nine in the evening on 29 February 1896, a leap day. Baratieri writes that he needed the moon to light the march, and gave the order late because the camp was full of spies.

The Battle of Adwa

Now the Ethiopian side. Menelik's chronicle says the noise of the firing was like rain that falls without stopping. Then the chronicler gives up. In our rendering: it is impossible to describe all that our eyes saw and our ears heard at the battle of Adwa. Adwa's earliest historian, George Berkeley, counted 56 Italian guns and 42 Ethiopian. Many of the modern Ethiopian rifles, Davis notes, had been supplied by the Italians. The Italian columns were picked off one by one. By mid-afternoon it was over. Of four Italian brigade generals, two were killed, one was captured, one was wounded. One Alpine battalion lost 15 of its 19 officers.

Did you know? Many of the modern Ethiopian rifles at Adwa, John Davis notes, had been supplied by the Italians. And of four Italian brigade generals, two were killed, one was captured and one was wounded (Claretie, preface to Baratieri's memoirs).

Empress Taytu

In the middle of it, the chronicle puts Empress Taytu. She had her own riflemen, and her own gunners. She came forward on foot, under a black umbrella, in mourning, its editor notes, instead of her red one, and parted her veil. When the rear ranks began to waver, she shouted. In our rendering: Courage! What is wrong with you? Victory is ours! Strike! Afterwards she had water carried to the wounded. Not only the Ethiopian wounded, the chronicle says, but the Italian wounded too. It is a court chronicle, written to praise. But it is how Menelik's court chose to remember her.

Did you know? On the field, the chronicle says, Empress Taytu had water carried to the wounded: not only the Ethiopian wounded, but the Italian wounded too. It is a court chronicle, written to praise.

The cost and the prisoners

The cost, as Davis gives it: 4,600 Italians and 1,000 askari killed, and between 4,000 and 6,000 Ethiopians. About 3,400 Italians and askari were taken prisoner. To their captors, the askari were traitors. About four hundred of them, the chronicle's editor writes, lost the right hand and the left foot: the punishment for high treason. He adds that Menelik is said to have tried, in vain, to spare them.

The peace of Addis Ababa

In Italy, the news brought riots. Within weeks, Crispi's government fell. Baratieri faced a court martial for abandoning his men. He was acquitted of the capital charge, but not cleared. The king, Davis writes, believed he should have been shot. On the twenty-sixth of October, Italy signed a peace at Addis Ababa. The treaty of Wuchale was annulled. Ethiopia was recognised as a sovereign and independent state. And this time the treaty spelled it out. In our rendering: written in Amharic and in French, the two texts absolutely in agreement. Menelik's chronicler sums it up: the envoy had Article 17 struck out, "the article that had been the cause of the war." Italy also paid ten million lire, which Menelik let it call the upkeep of the prisoners.

Did you know? Italy also paid ten million lire, which Menelik let it call the upkeep of the prisoners (John Davis; de Coppet's notes to the chronicle).

What Article 17 decided

So, a trick, a slip, or an excuse? The texts settle one thing: the Amharic never made Ethiopia a protectorate. Who made the Italian say more, and whether he meant to, is still argued. Davis calls Adwa not the first defeat of a European army in Africa, but the first decisive African victory. Italy invaded again in 1935, and looking back, he writes, it seems more a respite than a turning point.

Over to you

Antonelli, Crispi, or nobody at all: who made one article say two things? If you had been Baratieri, with that telegram from Rome, would you have marched that night?

Quick answers

What did Article 17 of the Treaty of Wuchale say?

One article, two languages, and they did not say the same thing. In the Italian text, the emperor of Ethiopia consents to use Italy's government for all his dealings with other powers. In the Amharic text, in Sven Rubenson's almost literal rendering: it shall be possible for him to communicate with the assistance of the Italian government.
