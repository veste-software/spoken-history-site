The Mystery of the Voynich Manuscript

This book is about six hundred years old, and to this day nobody can read a single word of its script. Its alphabet appears in no other book, and nobody who claims to have cracked it has convinced the experts. It is about the size of a large paperback, some 240 pages of calfskin, drawn on almost every page. There are plants that nobody has named for certain, wheels of stars, and small naked women bathing in green pools, joined by tubes. In 1639, one of its owners called it a riddle of the Sphinx. So what is it, who made it, and has anyone really read it? A laboratory in Arizona, five old letters from Prague and Rome, and a century of codebreakers will take us there. And under it all lies a question nobody has settled: is there anything to read at all?

What the Voynich manuscript is

Start with the book itself, the best witness we have. Today it is MS 408 of the Beinecke Library at Yale, and since 1969 it has left the building only once. 102 leaves survive, at least 14 are missing, and one page folds out to six panels wide. The parchment is calfskin, identified in 2012 from the proteins of ten of its leaves. The pictures fall into sections, from plants, stars and zodiac signs to the bathing women and jars of roots. Some of its letters look like ours, some like numerals, and some tall looped ones like nothing else. Yale's own catalogue once dated it to around 1500 or later, because some plants were thought to come from the Americas. Then someone cut four small slivers out of it.

Did you know? Since Hans Kraus gave it to Yale in 1969, the manuscript has left the Beinecke building only once, for an exhibition at the Folger Shakespeare Library in Washington in 2014 and 2015 (Beinecke Library).

Radiocarbon dating: 1404 to 1438

In 2009, Greg Hodgins, of the University of Arizona, sat down at Yale with the manuscript and a scalpel. He cut four samples, each about 1 millimetre by 6, from the edges of four pages in different sections, to test whether the book was written over many decades. In the laboratory in Tucson, the carbon told when the calves had died: most probably between 1404 and 1438, and the four samples agreed. That is where the six hundred years come from. Hodgins was careful about what it means. “We date when the animal lived”, he said, “not when the book was made.” One critic, Rich SantaColoma, points out that the range averages the four samples. Taken one by one, they stretch from 1365 to 1495. Even that wider range ends before 1500. So Roger Bacon, the English friar of the 1200s once named as the author, is out, and a modern forger would have needed a stack of untouched medieval parchment. The ink is very hard to carbon date, but Yale reports that the materials are consistent with books of the 1400s.

Did you know? Before the radiocarbon test, Yale's own catalogue placed the book around 1500 or later. Even the critic's widest range for the calfskin, 1365 to 1495, ends before 1500.

The letters: Prague, Rome and an emperor

The laboratory tells us when the calves lived. For the people who owned the book, we have letters. In 1639, a Prague alchemist named Georg Baresch wrote to Athanasius Kircher, a Jesuit scholar in Rome, famous for a book on the Coptic language. “There was in my library”, he wrote, “uselessly taking up space, a certain riddle of the Sphinx, a piece of writing in unknown characters.” He would not send the book itself on “a journey so long and full of perils”, so he had pages copied and sent those instead. Kircher had already answered the Jesuit who forwarded the copies. The writing, he said, “requires application rather than insight in its solver”. And “the itch of my mind working”, he wrote, “would have tried out some ideas on it if only many very urgent tasks did not call me away.” No answer to Baresch is known. He worked on it, a later letter says, until the end of his life, and left the book to his friend Johannes Marcus Marci, a professor of medicine in Prague. In August 1665, Marci, old and with his eyesight failing, sent the book itself to Kircher, with a letter that is still kept with it at Yale. “Such riddles”, he wrote, “only obey their very own Kircher.” And Marci passed on a story. A tutor at the royal court had told him the book belonged to the emperor Rudolf the Second, who gave the man who brought it six hundred ducats. The tutor thought the author was Roger Bacon. Marci wrote: “I suspend my judgement on the matter.” That is hearsay. But in the emperor's accounts, the researcher Stefan Guzy found that in 1599, a doctor in Augsburg sold Rudolf some manuscripts for a price recorded as 600 gold florins. A court record of the next year mentions a small barrel of strange books, held up at Augsburg on its way to the emperor's chamberlain. Guzy thinks our book may have been in that barrel. It cannot be proved. One man at Rudolf's court did sign it. On the first page, visible only under ultraviolet light, is the faded name of Jacobus de Tepenec, the emperor's pharmacist, made a nobleman in 1608. Marci tried too. In 2024, the manuscript expert Lisa Fagin Davis found three faded columns in the margin of that page, two of ordinary letters and one of the book's own, and argues the hand is Marci's. “I may have shrieked”, she said, “when I saw the marginal alphabets.” A friend of Marci's wrote to Rome twice more, hoping for news. Then the book vanishes into Jesuit libraries for nearly two hundred and fifty years.

Did you know? Kircher had already answered the Jesuit who forwarded Baresch's copies, in March 1639: he would have tried out some ideas on it, “if only many very urgent tasks did not call me away”.

Did you know? One name in the book is so faint that it shows only under ultraviolet light: Jacobus de Tepenec, the emperor's pharmacist, made a nobleman in 1608.

Wilfrid Voynich and the codebreakers

It came back into the light through a man who had escaped from Siberia. Wilfrid Voynich had been a Polish revolutionary. By his own account, confirmed in parts by police records, he fled his exile and in 1890 reached London with no money and no English. He became a dealer in rare books. In 1912, he bought a collection of manuscripts from the Jesuits near Rome, on a promise of “absolute secrecy”. He kept his word, and once told an audience in Michigan he had found the books in a castle in Austria. He called his prize the Roger Bacon cipher manuscript. In 1921, William Newbold, a professor of philosophy at the University of Pennsylvania, announced that he had cracked it. Bacon, he said, had used a microscope and a telescope, and had drawn the spiral of the Andromeda nebula, which nobody could make out before modern telescopes. But Newbold was not reading the letters. He was reading tiny irregularities at their edges, through a magnifying glass. In 1931, the scholar John Manly took the method apart, and those who had believed Newbold were left embarrassed. Then came the codebreakers. From 1944, William Friedman, one of the great cryptologists of his century, led study groups on it, and came to think it was an invented language. He never published a solution. In 1961, the New York dealer Hans Kraus bought the book for $24,500, and asked $160,000. Nobody paid it. In 1969, he gave it to Yale.

Did you know? Wilfrid Voynich kept his promise of secrecy so well that he once told an audience in Michigan he had found the books in a castle in Austria.

Did you know? In 1961 the New York dealer Hans Kraus paid $24,500 for the book and asked $160,000. Nobody paid it, and in 1969 he gave it to Yale.

Language, code or hoax?

So what can anyone know about a text that nobody can read? Quite a lot, from its patterns. Lisa Fagin Davis compared how single letters were made, stroke by stroke. “Like careless poker players”, she wrote, “scribes have tells.” She counts five scribes. She calls it a subjective analysis, but computer tests by others largely agree with her. The words behave like words: a few very common ones, many rare ones, in the pattern linguists call Zipf's law, and some cluster in particular sections, the way topic words do. But letter by letter, the text is strange. Its letters follow each other in unusually predictable ways, compared with European languages. The Yale linguists Claire Bowern and Luke Lindemann think it is most likely a real language in some kind of code. “We are not, at this stage, persuaded by any of the arguments in favor of Voynich being gibberish”, they wrote. Others are. In 2004, Gordon Rugg, a computer scientist at Keele University, showed how a table of syllables and a card with holes in it could churn out text that looks much like this. That does not prove a hoax. It shows that one could be made. In 2025, the science journalist Michael Greshko built a cipher, worked with dice and playing cards, that turns Latin into text behaving partly like the book's. It is almost certainly not how the book was made, he says. René Zandbergen, one of the best known researchers of the manuscript, calls himself “essentially undecided”. Of a hoax, he says: “I find it very hard to imagine how it could have been done.”

The decoding claims

Meanwhile, the solutions keep coming. In 2016, two computer scientists at the University of Alberta ran the text through an algorithm, and it pointed to Hebrew. Their Hebrew for the first line, after a couple of spelling corrections, came out of Google Translate as: “She made recommendations to the priest, man of the house and me and people.” A native speaker called it “not quite a coherent sentence”, and the authors allowed that it might simply be an artefact of their method. In 2019, the University of Bristol announced that a researcher, Gerard Cheshire, had succeeded where countless cryptographers had failed: the book, he said, was in a lost early Romance language. Lisa Fagin Davis answered on social media: “This is just more aspirational, circular, self-fulfilling nonsense.” The university took its story down, and said “the research was entirely the author's own work”. To this day, no proposed reading has won the specialists over.

Did you know? The 2016 Hebrew reading of the first line, after a couple of spelling corrections and Google Translate, came out as “She made recommendations to the priest, man of the house and me and people.”

What would settle it

So what do we really have? A book of calfskin from the early 1400s, written by several hands, in a script found nowhere else. A chain of owners from Prague to Rome, and a text that behaves partly like a language and partly like nothing we know. What would settle it? A second document in the same script, a key, or one word matched to one picture beyond doubt. Or the papers that went missing. Marci sent Baresch's attempts at decoding to Rome with the book, and they have never been found. Nor has Tepenec's list of his books, which might say where he got this one.

Verdict

My verdict: the Voynich manuscript is a real medieval book, on calfskin of the early 1400s, written by several scribes, and not a modern fake. Whether its text says anything is still open. To many linguists it looks like language, no hoax method yet explains it all, and nobody has read a word.

Quick answers

What is the Voynich manuscript?

A small book of calfskin, Beinecke MS 408 at Yale, about the size of a large paperback, with some 240 pages drawn on almost every page. Its alphabet appears in no other book. The pictures fall into sections: plants that nobody has named for certain, wheels of stars and zodiac signs, small naked women bathing in green pools joined by tubes, and jars of roots. A hundred and two leaves survive, at least fourteen are missing, and one page folds out to six panels wide. It is named after Wilfrid Voynich, the rare book dealer who bought it in 1912.

How old is the Voynich manuscript?

In 2009 Greg Hodgins, of the University of Arizona, cut four samples, each about one millimetre by six, from four pages in different sections. The carbon told when the calves had died: most probably between 1404 and 1438, and the four samples agreed. Hodgins was careful: “we date when the animal lived, not when the book was made.” The critic Rich SantaColoma points out that the range averages the four samples; taken one by one they stretch from 1365 to 1495, which still ends before 1500. Has the Voynich manuscript been decoded?

No proposed reading has won the specialists over. In 1921 William Newbold announced that he had cracked it, reading tiny irregularities at the edges of the letters through a magnifying glass; John Manly took the method apart in 1931. In 2016 two computer scientists at the University of Alberta found that their algorithm pointed to Hebrew, but their first line, through Google Translate, was “not quite a coherent sentence”, and they allowed it might be an artefact of their method. In 2019 the University of Bristol announced that Gerard Cheshire had found a lost early Romance language; Lisa Fagin Davis called it “aspirational, circular, self-fulfilling nonsense”, and the university took its story down.

Who owned the Voynich manuscript?

The earliest owner whose name is on the book is Jacobus de Tepenec, the emperor Rudolf the Second's pharmacist, whose faded name on the first page shows only under ultraviolet light. In 1639 Georg Baresch, an alchemist in Prague, called it “a certain riddle of the Sphinx” and sent copied pages to Athanasius Kircher in Rome. Baresch left it to his friend Johannes Marcus Marci, who sent the book itself to Kircher in 1665. Marci passed on a court tutor's story that Rudolf had paid 600 ducats for it; that is hearsay. In the emperor's accounts Stefan Guzy found a 1599 sale of manuscripts by a doctor in Augsburg, for 600 gold florins, and thinks our book may have been among them; it cannot be proved. Wilfrid Voynich bought it from the Jesuits near Rome in 1912, Hans Kraus bought it in 1961, and Kraus gave it to Yale in 1969.

Is the Voynich manuscript a hoax?

Nobody knows. The carbon date rules out a modern fake on modern parchment: a forger would have needed a stack of untouched medieval parchment. Whether the text says anything is still open. In 2004 Gordon Rugg, of Keele University, showed that a table of syllables and a card with holes could churn out text that looks much like it; that does not prove a hoax, it shows that one could be made. The Yale linguists Claire Bowern and Luke Lindemann think it is most likely a real language in some kind of code. René Zandbergen calls himself “essentially undecided”: “I find it very hard to imagine how it could have been done.”

Where it happened

  • Beinecke Rare Book and Manuscript Library, Yale University, New Haven (A.D. 1969): Where the manuscript is kept as Beinecke MS 408, since H. P. Kraus gave it to Yale in 1969; it has left the building only once (Beinecke).
  • Prague (A.D. 1639): Where Georg Baresch wrote to Athanasius Kircher about the book in 1639 and Johannes Marcus Marci sent it to him in 1665 (letters, tr. Philip Neal); Rudolf the Second's court, where Jacobus de Tepenec, who signed the book, was the emperor's pharmacist. A point in the city, not a building.
  • Collegio Romano, Rome (A.D. 1665): Kircher's Jesuit college in Rome, where Marci's letter of 1665 and the book were sent. Afterwards the book was in Jesuit libraries until Wilfrid Voynich bought it from the Jesuits near Rome in 1912 (the exact place is disputed).
  • Augsburg (A.D. 1599): Where a doctor sold Rudolf the Second some manuscripts in 1599 for a price recorded as 600 gold florins, and where a small barrel of strange books was held up in 1600 (Stefan Guzy, via The Art Newspaper 2023; Zandbergen). That our book was among them is Guzy's suggestion and cannot be proved.
  • University of Arizona, Tucson (A.D. 2009): The radiocarbon laboratory where Greg Hodgins's four samples were dated, 1404 to 1438 (University of Arizona, 2011). The samples were cut at Yale in 2009.
  • Soho Square, London (A.D. 1899): Wilfrid Voynich's rare book shop, photographed about 1899; he had reached London in 1890 (Zandbergen). The year stands for the photograph.

    Transcript

    Read the full narration

    The Voynich Manuscript. Not one word deciphered. This book is about six hundred years old, and to this day nobody can read a single word of its script. Its alphabet appears in no other book, and nobody who claims to have cracked it has convinced the experts. It is about the size of a large paperback, some 240 pages of calfskin, drawn on almost every page. There are plants that nobody has named for certain, wheels of stars, and small naked women bathing in green pools, joined by tubes. In 1639, one of its owners called it a riddle of the Sphinx. So what is it, who made it, and has anyone really read it? A laboratory in Arizona, five old letters from Prague and Rome, and a century of codebreakers will take us there. And under it all lies a question nobody has settled: is there anything to read at all? Start with the book itself, the best witness we have. Today it is MS 408 of the Beinecke Library at Yale, and since 1969 it has left the building only once. 102 leaves survive, at least 14 are missing, and one page folds out to six panels wide. The parchment is calfskin, identified in 2012 from the proteins of ten of its leaves. The pictures fall into sections, from plants, stars and zodiac signs to the bathing women and jars of roots. Some of its letters look like ours, some like numerals, and some tall looped ones like nothing else. Yale's own catalogue once dated it to around 1500 or later, because some plants were thought to come from the Americas. Then someone cut four small slivers out of it. In 2009, Greg Hodgins, of the University of Arizona, sat down at Yale with the manuscript and a scalpel. He cut four samples, each about 1 millimetre by 6, from the edges of four pages in different sections, to test whether the book was written over many decades. In the laboratory in Tucson, the carbon told when the calves had died: most probably between 1404 and 1438, and the four samples agreed. That is where the six hundred years come from. Hodgins was careful about what it means. We date when the animal lived, he said, not when the book was made. One critic, Rich SantaColoma, points out that the range averages the four samples. Taken one by one, they stretch from 1365 to 1495. Even that wider range ends before 1500. So Roger Bacon, the English friar of the 1200s once named as the author, is out, and a modern forger would have needed a stack of untouched medieval parchment. The ink is very hard to carbon date, but Yale reports that the materials are consistent with books of the 1400s. The laboratory tells us when the calves lived. For the people who owned the book, we have letters. In 1639, a Prague alchemist named Georg Baresch wrote to Athanasius Kircher, a Jesuit scholar in Rome, famous for a book on the Coptic language. There was in my library, he wrote, uselessly taking up space, a certain riddle of the Sphinx, a piece of writing in unknown characters. He would not send the book itself on a journey so long and full of perils, so he had pages copied and sent those instead. Kircher had already answered the Jesuit who forwarded the copies. The writing, he said, required application rather than insight. And the itch of my mind working, he wrote, would have tried out some ideas on it, if only many very urgent tasks did not call me away. No answer to Baresch is known. He worked on it, a later letter says, until the end of his life, and left the book to his friend Johannes Marcus Marci, a professor of medicine in Prague. In August 1665, Marci, old and with his eyesight failing, sent the book itself to Kircher, with a letter that is still kept with it at Yale. Such riddles, he wrote, only obey their very own Kircher. And Marci passed on a story. A tutor at the royal court had told him the book belonged to the emperor Rudolf the Second, who gave the man who brought it six hundred ducats. The tutor thought the author was Roger Bacon. Marci wrote: I suspend my judgement on the matter. That is hearsay. But in the emperor's accounts, the researcher Stefan Guzy found that in 1599, a doctor in Augsburg sold Rudolf some manuscripts for a price recorded as 600 gold florins. A court record of the next year mentions a small barrel of strange books, held up at Augsburg on its way to the emperor's chamberlain. Guzy thinks our book may have been in that barrel. It cannot be proved. One man at Rudolf's court did sign it. On the first page, visible only under ultraviolet light, is the faded name of Jacobus de Tepenec, the emperor's pharmacist, made a nobleman in 1608. Marci tried too. In 2024, the manuscript expert Lisa Fagin Davis found three faded columns in the margin of that page, two of ordinary letters and one of the book's own, and argues the hand is Marci's. I may have shrieked, she said, when I saw the marginal alphabets. A friend of Marci's wrote to Rome twice more, hoping for news. Then the book vanishes into Jesuit libraries for nearly two hundred and fifty years. If you are enjoying this type of content, please don't forget to subscribe. It came back into the light through a man who had escaped from Siberia. Wilfrid Voynich had been a Polish revolutionary. By his own account, confirmed in parts by police records, he fled his exile and in 1890 reached London with no money and no English. He became a dealer in rare books. In 1912, he bought a collection of manuscripts from the Jesuits near Rome, on a promise of absolute secrecy. He kept his word, and once told an audience in Michigan he had found the books in a castle in Austria. He called his prize the Roger Bacon cipher manuscript. In 1921, William Newbold, a professor of philosophy at the University of Pennsylvania, announced that he had cracked it. Bacon, he said, had used a microscope and a telescope, and had drawn the spiral of the Andromeda nebula, which nobody could make out before modern telescopes. But Newbold was not reading the letters. He was reading tiny irregularities at their edges, through a magnifying glass. In 1931, the scholar John Manly took the method apart, and those who had believed Newbold were left embarrassed. Then came the codebreakers. From 1944, William Friedman, one of the great cryptologists of his century, led study groups on it, and came to think it was an invented language. He never published a solution. In 1961, the New York dealer Hans Kraus bought the book for $24,500, and asked $160,000. Nobody paid it. In 1969, he gave it to Yale. So what can anyone know about a text that nobody can read? Quite a lot, from its patterns. Lisa Fagin Davis compared how single letters were made, stroke by stroke. Like careless poker players, she wrote, scribes have tells. She counts five scribes. She calls it a subjective analysis, but computer tests by others largely agree with her. The words behave like words: a few very common ones, many rare ones, in the pattern linguists call Zipf's law, and some cluster in particular sections, the way topic words do. But letter by letter, the text is strange. Its letters follow each other in unusually predictable ways, compared with European languages. The Yale linguists Claire Bowern and Luke Lindemann think it is most likely a real language in some kind of code. We are not, they wrote, persuaded by any of the arguments in favor of Voynich being gibberish. Others are. In 2004, Gordon Rugg, a computer scientist at Keele University, showed how a table of syllables and a card with holes in it could churn out text that looks much like this. That does not prove a hoax. It shows that one could be made. In 2025, the science journalist Michael Greshko built a cipher, worked with dice and playing cards, that turns Latin into text behaving partly like the book's. It is almost certainly not how the book was made, he says. René Zandbergen, one of the best known researchers of the manuscript, calls himself essentially undecided. Of a hoax, he says: I find it very hard to imagine how it could have been done. Meanwhile, the solutions keep coming. In 2016, two computer scientists at the University of Alberta ran the text through an algorithm, and it pointed to Hebrew. Their Hebrew for the first line, after a couple of spelling corrections, came out of Google Translate as: She made recommendations to the priest, man of the house and me and people. A native speaker called it not quite a coherent sentence, and the authors allowed that it might simply be an artefact of their method. In 2019, the University of Bristol announced that a researcher, Gerard Cheshire, had succeeded where countless cryptographers had failed: the book, he said, was in a lost early Romance language. Lisa Fagin Davis answered on social media: This is just more aspirational, circular, self-fulfilling nonsense. The university took its story down, and said the research was entirely the author's own work. To this day, no proposed reading has won the specialists over. So what do we really have? A book of calfskin from the early 1400s, written by several hands, in a script found nowhere else. A chain of owners from Prague to Rome, and a text that behaves partly like a language and partly like nothing we know. What would settle it? A second document in the same script, a key, or one word matched to one picture beyond doubt. Or the papers that went missing. Marci sent Baresch's attempts at decoding to Rome with the book, and they have never been found. Nor has Tepenec's list of his books, which might say where he got this one. My verdict: the Voynich manuscript is a real medieval book, on calfskin of the early 1400s, written by several scribes, and not a modern fake. Whether its text says anything is still open. To many linguists it looks like language, no hoax method yet explains it all, and nobody has read a word. A real language hidden in a clever code, or six hundred year old nonsense made to look like one? And if you could find one lost thing, Baresch's notes on his attempts or Tepenec's list of his books, which would it be?
