Ain Jalut, 1260: Slave Soldiers Against the Mongols

If I am killed by your hand, I consider it to be God's act, not yours. Those are among the last words of a Mongol general, captured by a spring in the Jezreel valley in 1260, as a Persian history written for the Mongol court gives them. He was speaking to the sultan of Egypt, who, by the Arabic accounts, had once been sold as a slave. And the sultan's chief emir had been captured as a boy, when the Mongols came for his people.

So was the general right? Did the Mamluks win this battle, or did the Mongols lose it? The historian John Masson Smith asked exactly that, in 1984.

The ultimatum

In 1260, the armies of Hulegu, a grandson of Genghis Khan, swept into Syria. Aleppo was stormed. The Syriac bishop Bar Hebraeus wrote: there took place in Aleppo a slaughter like unto that of Baghdad. Damascus opened its gates without a fight. Then Hulegu sent envoys to Cairo. In the Persian version, the message ends: "send tribute, come yourself ... otherwise be prepared for battle." Egypt's sultan, Qutuz, answered by killing the envoys. Al Maqrizi, an Egyptian historian writing almost two centuries later, says there were four. Each was cut in two. Their heads hung on a gate of Cairo. Among them, he says, was a boy. The sultan spared him, and made him one of his own Mamluks.

Did you know? Al-Maqrizi, writing almost two centuries later, says one of Hulegu's envoys was a boy whom the sultan spared and made one of his own Mamluks. At Ain Jalut, he says, the boy rode behind the sultan and aimed an arrow at him (al-Maqrizi, Kitab al-suluk, French tr. Quatremère, 1837).

How big was the Mongol army?

But Hulegu himself was gone. Far to the east, his brother, the Great Khan Mongke, had died. The struggle for his throne ended with another brother, Kublai, who later sent his fleets against Japan. Hulegu withdrew most of his troops, and left his general Kitbuqa to hold Syria. The Armenian historian Kirakos, who lived at the time, calls Kitbuqa a Christian and a Naiman by nationality, a man of great respect. How many men did he have? The numbers fall apart. The Persian history says several thousand horsemen. Bar Hebraeus says 10,000. Kirakos, 20,000. A later Persian writer, 30,000. Smith saw two units of 10,000, one of Mongols, one of local Syrian troops who had joined them. The real number, we do not know. The historian Reuven Amitai notes that the Mongol troops were scattered across the country, probably to graze their horses.

Did you know? The Mongol general Kitbuqa was a Christian, says the Armenian historian Kirakos, who lived at the time: "a Christian and a Naiman by nationality, a man of great respect" (Kirakos of Gandzak, History of the Armenians, tr. R. Bedrosian, 1986).

Slave soldiers

And the Egyptians? Many of their leaders were Mamluks: soldiers who had been bought as slaves. Take the officer Baybars. A fellow captive, the emir Baysari, told his story. Baybars was born on the Kipchak steppe. When the Mongols moved against his people, they fled across the sea to a king, who betrayed them, raided them, and sold them. In our rendering, Baysari says: I and he were among those captured, and he was then about fourteen years old. Sold, he was taken to Sivas, then Aleppo, and sold again in Cairo, to an emir called the Bunduqdar, whose name he kept. At Hama, says a much later story, a prince's mother looked him over from behind a curtain, and refused to buy him. There is evil in his eyes, she said. The Mongol storm had sold Baybars into the army that would face it. Qutuz, too, had been sold in Damascus. When his master slapped him, a story claims, he wept: he was the nephew of the Khwarazm Shah, of the children of kings.

Did you know? Baybars was sold in Cairo to an emir called the Bunduqdar, and kept his owner's name. A fellow captive, the emir Baysari, remembered that Baybars was about fourteen when they were taken (Ibn Shaddad, from Baysari, in al-Yunini's history; our rendering).

The Franks of Acre

To reach the Mongols, the Egyptian army marched up the coast, past the crusader city of Acre. The Franks there were furious with the Mongols, says a Frankish chronicle, over the damage they had done at Sidon. Another Frankish chronicle says most of the barons wanted to fight beside the Egyptians. The Master of the Teutonic Knights talked them out of it. The Egyptian historian Ibn al Furat gives the sultan's warning to them: if any horseman or footman of yours follows me, I shall kill you before I meet the Tartars. So the Franks did business. They agreed a price for any Mongol horses the Egyptians would capture. The chronicler called the Templar of Tyre adds: But as it turned out, once the Saracens had won they did no such thing.

Did you know? The Franks of Acre agreed a price for any Mongol horses the Egyptians would capture. "But as it turned out, once the Saracens had won they did no such thing" (the Templar of Tyre, tr. P. Crawford, 2003).

The battle at the spring

The armies met on a Friday, the 25th of Ramadan. In the Frankish chronicles, the 3rd of September, 1260. The spring lies at the foot of Mount Gilboa. Its Arabic name, Ain Jalut, means the Spring of Goliath. Baybars led the vanguard. Al Yunini, a Syrian scholar of the time, says he kept drawing the Mongols on, now advancing, now holding back. The Persian history makes it a trap. Qutuz hid his troops, pretended to withdraw, and when the Mongols chased him, the ambush was sprung from three sides. The Arabic chronicles tell a harder fight. At sunrise, says al Maqrizi, the valley was full of troops, drums, and the shouts of village peasants. The left wing of the Muslims broke. Al Maqrizi says Qutuz threw his helmet to the ground, cried with all his strength: O Islam! and charged. And the spared envoy boy? He rode behind the sultan, Maqrizi says, and aimed an arrow at him. He was cut down. In another version, the arrow hit the sultan's horse. Al Yunini says the sultan's horse was killed, and he refused an emir's horse. Scolded by a friend, he answered, in our rendering: As for me, I would have gone to Paradise. As for Islam, God would not have let it be lost. The Mongols broke. Some made a stand on a nearby hill, and were wiped out. Many who fled were killed by local peasants.

Did you know? Ain Jalut means the Spring of Goliath. The spring at the foot of Mount Gilboa is today Ma'ayan Harod National Park.

Kitbuqa's end: two versions

And Kitbuqa? The sources split. Al Maqrizi, Bar Hebraeus and a Frankish chronicle say he was killed in the battle. Al Yunini names the emir who, it is said, killed him. Of the histories we read, only the Persian one clearly has him captured, and brought before Qutuz with his hands bound. There he speaks his famous line. And then he boasts: "Hulegu Khan has 300,000 renowned horsemen like Kitbuqa. You may take one of them away." A boast in a speech, written decades later for Hulegu's descendants. Not a count.

Did you know? In the Persian history, the captured Kitbuqa boasts that Hulegu Khan has 300,000 renowned horsemen like him. It is a boast in a speech, written decades later for Hulegu's descendants, not a count (Rashid al-Din, tr. W. M. Thackston).

Did it stop the Mongols?

Today, the park's leaflet says this battle put a stop to the Mongol takeover of Europe and Asia. The Syrian prince Abu'l Fida wrote that people had despaired: the Mongols had never attacked an army without routing it. That was not true. In 1221, at Parwan, in what is now Afghanistan, the sultan Jalal al Din had met a Mongol army. Juvaini, who wrote for the Mongols, says: The Sultan was victorious, and the Mongol army returned to Chingiz Khan. Al Yunini names an earlier victor. He calls Qutuz the first who dared to face the Tatars and broke them, after the Khwarazm Shah. Nor were the Mongols finished. Amitai notes they occupied most of Syria twice, in 1260 and again in 1299, in a war of 60 years. What Ain Jalut marked, in the historian Charles Halperin's words, was the furthest advance of the Mongols in the Middle East.

Did you know? Juvaini, who wrote for the Mongols, recorded a Mongol defeat almost forty years before Ain Jalut: at Parwan in 1221, "the Sultan was victorious, and the Mongol army returned to Chingiz Khan" (Juvaini, tr. J. A. Boyle, 1958).

The murder of Qutuz

Qutuz did not enjoy it long. About seven weeks later, on the way home to Egypt, he went hunting. Baybars had asked for Aleppo, says al Maqrizi, and been refused. A man took the sultan's hand, as if to kiss it, and held on. Then the blows came. Who struck first? Al Maqrizi names another emir. Abu'l Fida says it was Baybars himself. At the camp, the sultan's deputy asked who had killed him. I did, said Baybars. And he was made sultan. Qutuz had ruled for less than a year. Eleven years later, Baybars took Krak des Chevaliers.

Verdict

So, did the Mamluks win, or did the Mongols lose? Both. A small, scattered Mongol force, left behind when its khan withdrew, met a sultan who chose to march, past a neutral Acre. Amitai calls it, in spite of the relatively small numbers, a crucial encounter, which set the stage for the region's history for many decades. Kitbuqa's last words may be the historian's, not his. But they ask the right question.

Where it happened

  • Ain Jalut (Ma'ayan Harod), at the foot of Mount Gilboa (A.D. 1260): The spring where the Mamluk army of Egypt met the Mongol force of Kitbuqa, on a Friday, the 25th of Ramadan, the 3rd of September 1260 in the Frankish chronicles. Where the lines stood is not known.
  • Aleppo (A.D. 1260): Stormed by Hulegu's armies in 1260; Bar Hebraeus compares the slaughter to that of Baghdad. Baybars passed through here as a captive boy.
  • Damascus (A.D. 1260): Opened its gates to the Mongols without a fight. Qutuz, too, had been sold here, by the Arabic accounts.
  • Cairo (A.D. 1260): Hulegu's envoys came here; al-Maqrizi says their heads hung on a gate of the city. Baybars was sold here to the Bunduqdar.
  • Acre (A.D. 1260): The crusader city the Egyptian army marched past; the Franks stayed out of the battle and agreed a price for Mongol horses.
  • Sidon : The Franks were furious with the Mongols over the damage they had done here, says a Frankish chronicle.
  • Sivas : Where the captured boy Baybars was taken after he was sold, on his way to Aleppo and Cairo.
  • Hama : In a much later story, a prince's mother here refused to buy the boy Baybars: there is evil in his eyes, she said.
  • Parwan, in what is now Afghanistan (A.D. 1221): The sultan Jalal al-Din beat a Mongol army here in 1221, as Juvaini, who wrote for the Mongols, records. Where exactly the battle was fought is not known; the pin marks the region.
  • Krak des Chevaliers (A.D. 1271): Taken by Baybars, eleven years after Ain Jalut.

He was speaking to the sultan of Egypt, who, by the Arabic accounts, had once been sold as a slave. And the sultan's chief emir had been captured as a boy, when the Mongols came for his people. So was the general right? Did the Mamluks win this battle, or did the Mongols lose it? The historian John Masson Smith asked exactly that, in 1984. In 1260, the armies of Hulegu, a grandson of Genghis Khan, swept into Syria. Aleppo was stormed. The Syriac bishop Bar Hebraeus wrote: there took place in Aleppo a slaughter like unto that of Baghdad. Damascus opened its gates without a fight. Then Hulegu sent envoys to Cairo. In the Persian version, the message ends: "send tribute, come yourself ... otherwise be prepared for battle." Egypt's sultan, Qutuz, answered by killing the envoys. Al Maqrizi, an Egyptian historian writing almost two centuries later, says there were four. Each was cut in two. Their heads hung on a gate of Cairo. Among them, he says, was a boy. The sultan spared him, and made him one of his own Mamluks. But Hulegu himself was gone. Far to the east, his brother, the Great Khan Mongke, had died. The struggle for his throne ended with another brother, Kublai, who later sent his fleets against Japan. Hulegu withdrew most of his troops, and left his general Kitbuqa to hold Syria. The Armenian historian Kirakos, who lived at the time, calls Kitbuqa a Christian and a Naiman by nationality, a man of great respect. How many men did he have? The numbers fall apart. The Persian history says several thousand horsemen. Bar Hebraeus says 10,000. Kirakos, 20,000. A later Persian writer, 30,000. Smith saw two units of 10,000, one of Mongols, one of local Syrian troops who had joined them. The real number, we do not know. The historian Reuven Amitai notes that the Mongol troops were scattered across the country, probably to graze their horses. And the Egyptians? Many of their leaders were Mamluks: soldiers who had been bought as slaves. Take the officer Baybars. A fellow captive, the emir Baysari, told his story. Baybars was born on the Kipchak steppe. When the Mongols moved against his people, they fled across the sea to a king, who betrayed them, raided them, and sold them. In our rendering, Baysari says: I and he were among those captured, and he was then about fourteen years old. Sold, he was taken to Sivas, then Aleppo, and sold again in Cairo, to an emir called the Bunduqdar, whose name he kept. At Hama, says a much later story, a prince's mother looked him over from behind a curtain, and refused to buy him. There is evil in his eyes, she said. The Mongol storm had sold Baybars into the army that would face it. Qutuz, too, had been sold in Damascus. When his master slapped him, a story claims, he wept: he was the nephew of the Khwarazm Shah, of the children of kings. If you are enjoying this type of content, please don't forget to subscribe. To reach the Mongols, the Egyptian army marched up the coast, past the crusader city of Acre. The Franks there were furious with the Mongols, says a Frankish chronicle, over the damage they had done at Sidon. Another Frankish chronicle says most of the barons wanted to fight beside the Egyptians. The Master of the Teutonic Knights talked them out of it. The Egyptian historian Ibn al Furat gives the sultan's warning to them: if any horseman or footman of yours follows me, I shall kill you before I meet the Tartars. So the Franks did business. They agreed a price for any Mongol horses the Egyptians would capture. The chronicler called the Templar of Tyre adds: But as it turned out, once the Saracens had won they did no such thing. The armies met on a Friday, the 25th of Ramadan. In the Frankish chronicles, the 3rd of September, 1260. The spring lies at the foot of Mount Gilboa. Its Arabic name, Ain Jalut, means the Spring of Goliath. Baybars led the vanguard. Al Yunini, a Syrian scholar of the time, says he kept drawing the Mongols on, now advancing, now holding back. The Persian history makes it a trap. Qutuz hid his troops, pretended to withdraw, and when the Mongols chased him, the ambush was sprung from three sides. The Arabic chronicles tell a harder fight. At sunrise, says al Maqrizi, the valley was full of troops, drums, and the shouts of village peasants. The left wing of the Muslims broke. Al Maqrizi says Qutuz threw his helmet to the ground, cried with all his strength: O Islam! and charged. And the spared envoy boy? He rode behind the sultan, Maqrizi says, and aimed an arrow at him. He was cut down. In another version, the arrow hit the sultan's horse. Al Yunini says the sultan's horse was killed, and he refused an emir's horse. Scolded by a friend, he answered, in our rendering: As for me, I would have gone to Paradise. As for Islam, God would not have let it be lost. The Mongols broke. Some made a stand on a nearby hill, and were wiped out. Many who fled were killed by local peasants. And Kitbuqa? The sources split. Al Maqrizi, Bar Hebraeus and a Frankish chronicle say he was killed in the battle. Al Yunini names the emir who, it is said, killed him. Of the histories we read, only the Persian one clearly has him captured, and brought before Qutuz with his hands bound. There he speaks his famous line. And then he boasts: "Hulegu Khan has 300,000 renowned horsemen like Kitbuqa. You may take one of them away." A boast in a speech, written decades later for Hulegu's descendants. Not a count. Today, the park's leaflet says this battle put a stop to the Mongol takeover of Europe and Asia. The Syrian prince Abu'l Fida wrote that people had despaired: the Mongols had never attacked an army without routing it. That was not true. In 1221, at Parwan, in what is now Afghanistan, the sultan Jalal al Din had met a Mongol army. Juvaini, who wrote for the Mongols, says: The Sultan was victorious, and the Mongol army returned to Chingiz Khan. Al Yunini names an earlier victor. He calls Qutuz the first who dared to face the Tatars and broke them, after the Khwarazm Shah. Nor were the Mongols finished. Amitai notes they occupied most of Syria twice, in 1260 and again in 1299, in a war of 60 years. What Ain Jalut marked, in the historian Charles Halperin's words, was the furthest advance of the Mongols in the Middle East. Qutuz did not enjoy it long. About seven weeks later, on the way home to Egypt, he went hunting. Baybars had asked for Aleppo, says al Maqrizi, and been refused. A man took the sultan's hand, as if to kiss it, and held on. Then the blows came. Who struck first? Al Maqrizi names another emir. Abu'l Fida says it was Baybars himself. At the camp, the sultan's deputy asked who had killed him. I did, said Baybars. And he was made sultan. Qutuz had ruled for less than a year. Eleven years later, Baybars took Krak des Chevaliers. So, did the Mamluks win, or did the Mongols lose? Both. A small, scattered Mongol force, left behind when its khan withdrew, met a sultan who chose to march, past a neutral Acre. Amitai calls it, in spite of the relatively small numbers, a crucial encounter, which set the stage for the region's history for many decades. Kitbuqa's last words may be the historian's, not his. But they ask the right question. Two questions for you. If Hulegu's whole army had stayed in Syria, could the Mamluks still have won?
