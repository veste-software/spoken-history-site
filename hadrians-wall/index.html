Hadrian's Wall: Turf, Stone and Milecastles

Staffordshire, England. 2003. A metal detector turns up a little bronze pan, decorated with bright enamel. It is about 9 centimetres across. Small enough to hold in one hand. Around the rim runs a line of Latin, with no clear beginning and no clear end. In it, four names: Mais. Congavata. Uxelodunum. Camboglanna. They are Roman forts. All four stood at the western end of Hadrian's Wall, more than 200 kilometres to the north. And four more words. Rigore vali Aeli Draconis. Vali means: of the Wall. And Aelius was Hadrian's own family name. So this little pan may tell us something no ancient book does. What the Romans called the Wall.

Did you know? The Roman name of Hadrian's Wall may survive on a souvenir pan about 9 cm across, found in Staffordshire in 2003: rigore vali Aeli Draconis. Whether Aeli means the Aelian Wall or is part of a man's name, Aelius Draco, is disputed (RIB Brit. 35.24; David Breeze).

This is Hadrian's Wall. 80 Roman miles of stone and turf, right across Britain, from the river Tyne to the Solway. Today: what it was, what has been dug up, and what the texts really say. And the first surprise: the ancient writers almost never name Hadrian as its builder.

One sentence in the Historia Augusta

Of the ancient texts we know, one sentence gives it to Hadrian. It is in the Historia Augusta, a collection of the lives of the emperors. Hadrian, it says, went to Britain, set many things right, and was the first to build a wall, 80 miles long, to separate the barbarians from the Romans. But handle it with care. The collection was written more than 200 years after Hadrian, and most scholars now think by one author, who pretends to be six. And in the very next sentence, it says Hadrian sacked his secretary for letters. His name was Suetonius. The same Suetonius who told us how Caesar died, in our pugio episode.

Did you know? The only ancient sentence we know that credits Hadrian with the Wall is followed at once by the sacking of his secretary for letters, Suetonius, the biographer of the Caesars (Historia Augusta, Life of Hadrian 11.2 to 11.3).

Severus, Bede, and a muddle

Later writers muddled it. The same collection, in its life of the emperor Septimius Severus, says that Severus built a wall across Britain, from sea to sea. A note in its Loeb edition of 1921 says this probably means a restoration of Hadrian's wall. And the monk Bede, at Jarrow, just a few miles from the Wall's eastern end, gives a different story again. Severus, he says, built a rampart of turf. The stone wall was built by the Romans, with the Britons' help, at the very end of Roman Britain. Eight feet broad, twelve feet high, and, he says, still to be seen. Hadrian is not in his story at all.

The proof is in the stones

So how do we know it was Hadrian? From the stones themselves. This inscription probably comes from milecastle 38, in the middle of the Wall. The work of the emperor Caesar Trajan Hadrian Augustus. The Second Legion Augusta built it, under Aulus Platorius Nepos, the governor. Platorius Nepos governed Britain from about 122 to about 126. 122 is the year Hadrian came to Britain. And, says the archaeologist David Breeze, the Wall was built on his orders after that visit.

Before the Wall: the Stanegate

Hadrian did not start from nothing. Across this narrow neck of Britain, forts already stood along a road we now call the Stanegate, between Corbridge and Carlisle. Vindolanda, from our fort episode, was one of them. Paul Bidwell argues that Hadrian's first plan took in much of that older line. The Wall itself ran a little further north.

The plan: a gate every mile

As first planned, the Wall was a wall of stone or turf, with a wide, deep ditch in front of it. At every Roman mile, about one and a half kilometres, there was a gate, guarded by a small fort we call a milecastle. Between each pair of milecastles stood two towers, called turrets. A lookout every third of a mile. A barrier with a gate roughly every mile. About eighty gates through a wall meant to separate. In the east, the stone wall was planned 10 Roman feet thick. About 3 metres. How high? Nowhere does it survive to its full height. From the remains of two milecastles, Peter Hill and Brian Dobson worked out a design height of about 15 Roman feet. Some 4.5 metres.

Turf in the west

But the west was different. Beyond the river Irthing, the Wall was first built of turf. About the western 30 miles of the 80. A rampart 20 Roman feet wide, about 6 metres, with milecastles of turf and timber. Why turf? The sources we read don't settle it. Later, the turf was replaced in stone. At Birdoswald, the new stone wall even took a different line.

The plan changes in the stones

And then, while the work was still going on, the plan changed. The stone wall was narrowed. To 8 Roman feet, says Breeze, or less. You can still see it. The turrets were built before the wall reached them, with short stubs of wall at either side, ready to be joined. At Brunton turret, the stub on one side is broad. The wall that came up to it later is narrow, about 1.8 metres instead of 3. A change of plan, frozen in the masonry.

Who built it: signed by the century

Who built it? Mainly the three legions in Britain, says Breeze, with help from auxiliary units and the fleet. And they signed their work. Small building stones name the unit that built a length of wall. This one was found near Chesters. From the sixth cohort, the century of Lousius Suavis. The standard edition of Roman inscriptions from Britain lists 5 more stones naming the same century, some of them more than 20 kilometres further west. The legions did not even build their milecastle gates alike. Hill and Dobson see three designs, from three legions. Hadrian's instructions, they write, cannot have been very detailed.

Forts and the Vallum

Then came the biggest change of all. Before the work was finished, 14 new forts were added, on the line of the Wall itself. Some went straight over what had already been built. At Housesteads, a turret now lies inside the fort walls. At Birdoswald, a turret was partly torn down to make room. And behind the Wall, to the south, came a vast earthwork: a ditch between two banks, along almost the whole length. We call it the Vallum. It could be crossed only at the forts, on causeways, through gates. Hill and Dobson put it bluntly. The forts and the Vallum are clear evidence that the original plan was flawed. Why it changed is argued over. Bidwell sees a response to resistance, and open warfare, beyond the frontier and behind it.

Branches on the berm

Now a discovery made only in this century. Around the year 2000, rescue excavations at Shields Road, in Newcastle, found pits on the strip of ground between the Wall and its ditch. Three rows of them, staggered, each pit holding the bases of two obstacles. Probably forked branches, with sharpened ends. Caesar, at the siege of Alesia from our scorpion episode, says his soldiers called such things cippi. A thicket of sharpened branches, right in front of the Wall. They have since been found at several places on Tyneside. Did they run the whole length? Nobody knows. Very little of that strip has ever been dug.

Did you know? Since rescue excavations at Shields Road in Newcastle around 2000, three staggered rows of pits for obstacles, probably forked and sharpened branches, have been found in front of the Wall on Tyneside. Caesar says his soldiers at Alesia called such obstacles cippi (Bidwell 2018; Caesar, Gallic War 7.73).

What was it for? A walkway on top?

So what was the Wall for? The Historia Augusta says: to separate the barbarians from the Romans. Hill and Dobson saw it differently. Its strength, they wrote, lay in forbidding passage by hoof or wheel, except at the authorised and controlled gateways. Stopping men on foot was impossible. And was there a walkway along the top, with battlements, as in so many pictures? We do not know. No part of the Wall survives to its full height. It was wide enough for a walkway, says Breeze. Hill and Dobson thought a parapet likely, but not battlements for fighting, and they weighed a top that simply sloped, to shed the rain. A book published in 2025 still has whole chapters on the question. So our drawings of the top are reconstructions.

After Hadrian

The Wall had stood for only about ten years when Hadrian died. His successor, Antoninus Pius, built a new wall far to the north, in Scotland. It lasted about twenty years. Then the army came back to Hadrian's Wall, and stayed. The latest coins found along it were struck between 403 and 406.

The first souvenirs, and the Wall's name

Which brings us back to the pan. It is not alone. A cup found in 1725, in a well at a Roman villa in Wiltshire, names five forts of the Wall, and shows a wall with towers in enamel. Another, found at Amiens in France in 1949, names six. A book edited by David Breeze calls them the first souvenirs. And in 2026, another was announced, from Spain. By one count, the group now numbers six. So, rigore vali Aeli Draconis. Rigore is a surveyor's word: along the line. Vali: of the Wall. Then either: of the Aelian Wall, and a man called Draco. Or a man called Aelius Draco, perhaps the soldier who had it made. Breeze takes the first reading: the Wall was called after Hadrian's family. The pan's editor thinks that possible too, but if it was the name, it did not last. If Breeze is right, the Wall's Roman name has come down to us on a souvenir pan.

What we really have

So, what do we really have? 80 Roman miles of foundations. Inscriptions naming Hadrian and his governor. Stones signed by the centuries that built them. A plan you can watch changing in the masonry. Pits for sharpened branches. A handful of souvenirs. What we don't have: the full height, the shape of the top, the reason for the turf, or a sure Roman name.

Verdict, and what comes next

So that's Hadrian's Wall. Not a sealed barrier, but a wall full of gates, rebuilt and rethought while it was still going up.

Quick answers

Who built Hadrian's Wall?

Mainly the three legions in Britain, with help from auxiliary units and the fleet, says David Breeze. Building inscriptions name the emperor Hadrian and his governor Aulus Platorius Nepos, who governed Britain from about 122 to about 126, and small centurial stones name the units that built each length.

What do the ancient texts say about Hadrian's Wall?

Of the texts we know, one sentence credits Hadrian: the Historia Augusta says he was the first to build a wall, eighty miles long, to separate the barbarians from the Romans. That collection was written more than 200 years later. Its life of Septimius Severus says Severus built a wall from sea to sea, and Bede, in the eighth century, gives Severus a turf rampart and dates the stone wall to the end of Roman Britain.

How high was Hadrian's Wall?

Nowhere does it survive to its full height. From the remains of two milecastles, Peter Hill and Brian Dobson worked out a design height of about fifteen Roman feet, some four and a half metres. Whether there was a walkway with a parapet on top is still debated.

Why was part of Hadrian's Wall built of turf?

West of the river Irthing, about the western thirty of the eighty miles, the Wall was first built as a turf rampart twenty Roman feet wide, with milecastles of turf and timber. The sources we read do not settle why. It was later replaced in stone.

What was the Roman name of Hadrian's Wall?

We do not know for sure. A small enamelled pan found in Staffordshire in 2003 reads rigore vali Aeli Draconis. David Breeze reads it as the Aelian Wall, after Hadrian's family name Aelius; it can also be read as the name of a man, Aelius Draco.

Where it happened

  • Wallsend (Segedunum) (A.D. 125): The eastern end of the Wall. Pleiades 89288.
  • Bowness-on-Solway (Maia) (A.D. 125): The western end of the Wall; named on the pan as Mais. Pleiades 89239.
  • Milecastle 38 (Hotbank) (A.D. 122): Probable find place of RIB 1638, naming Hadrian and Platorius Nepos.
  • Housesteads (Vercovicium) (A.D. 125): A fort added on the line of the Wall over turret 36b.
  • Birdoswald (Banna) (A.D. 125): Turf Wall country; turret 49a partly demolished for the fort.
  • Brunton turret (26b) (A.D. 122): Broad wing wall and narrow curtain. Position: between the Pleiades points of milecastles 26 and 27.
  • Shields Road, Newcastle (A.D. 125): Obstacle pits on the berm found in rescue excavations in 2000 to 2001.
  • Vindolanda (A.D. 100): A fort on the Stanegate, before the Wall.
  • Near Ilam, Staffordshire (A.D. 130): Find place of the Staffordshire Moorlands pan (2003); exact spot not published.
  • Jarrow (A.D. 731): Bede's monastery.

Read the full narration

Hadrian's Wall. Turf, stone, and a gate every mile. Staffordshire, England. 2003. A metal detector turns up a little bronze pan, decorated with bright enamel. It is about 9 centimetres across. Small enough to hold in one hand. Around the rim runs a line of Latin, with no clear beginning and no clear end. In it, four names: Mais. Congavata. Uxelodunum. Camboglanna. They are Roman forts. All four stood at the western end of Hadrian's Wall, more than 200 kilometres to the north. And four more words. Rigore vali Aeli Draconis. Vali means: of the Wall. And Aelius was Hadrian's own family name. So this little pan may tell us something no ancient book does. What the Romans called the Wall. This is Hadrian's Wall. 80 Roman miles of stone and turf, right across Britain, from the river Tyne to the Solway. Today: what it was, what has been dug up, and what the texts really say. And the first surprise: the ancient writers almost never name Hadrian as its builder. Of the ancient texts we know, one sentence gives it to Hadrian. It is in the Historia Augusta, a collection of the lives of the emperors. Hadrian, it says, went to Britain, set many things right, and was the first to build a wall, 80 miles long, to separate the barbarians from the Romans. But handle it with care. The collection was written more than 200 years after Hadrian, and most scholars now think by one author, who pretends to be six. And in the very next sentence, it says Hadrian sacked his secretary for letters. His name was Suetonius. The same Suetonius who told us how Caesar died, in our pugio episode. Later writers muddled it. The same collection, in its life of the emperor Septimius Severus, says that Severus built a wall across Britain, from sea to sea. A note in its Loeb edition of 1921 says this probably means a restoration of Hadrian's wall. And the monk Bede, at Jarrow, just a few miles from the Wall's eastern end, gives a different story again. Severus, he says, built a rampart of turf. The stone wall was built by the Romans, with the Britons' help, at the very end of Roman Britain. Eight feet broad, twelve feet high, and, he says, still to be seen. Hadrian is not in his story at all. So how do we know it was Hadrian? From the stones themselves. This inscription probably comes from milecastle 38, in the middle of the Wall. The work of the emperor Caesar Trajan Hadrian Augustus. The Second Legion Augusta built it, under Aulus Platorius Nepos, the governor. Platorius Nepos governed Britain from about 122 to about 126. 122 is the year Hadrian came to Britain. And, says the archaeologist David Breeze, the Wall was built on his orders after that visit. Hadrian did not start from nothing. Across this narrow neck of Britain, forts already stood along a road we now call the Stanegate, between Corbridge and Carlisle. Vindolanda, from our fort episode, was one of them. Paul Bidwell argues that Hadrian's first plan took in much of that older line. The Wall itself ran a little further north. As first planned, the Wall was a wall of stone or turf, with a wide, deep ditch in front of it. At every Roman mile, about one and a half kilometres, there was a gate, guarded by a small fort we call a milecastle. Between each pair of milecastles stood two towers, called turrets. A lookout every third of a mile. A barrier with a gate roughly every mile. About eighty gates through a wall meant to separate. In the east, the stone wall was planned 10 Roman feet thick. About 3 metres. How high? Nowhere does it survive to its full height. From the remains of two milecastles, Peter Hill and Brian Dobson worked out a design height of about 15 Roman feet. Some 4.5 metres. But the west was different. Beyond the river Irthing, the Wall was first built of turf. About the western 30 miles of the 80. A rampart 20 Roman feet wide, about 6 metres, with milecastles of turf and timber. Why turf? The sources we read don't settle it. Later, the turf was replaced in stone. At Birdoswald, the new stone wall even took a different line. And then, while the work was still going on, the plan changed. The stone wall was narrowed. To 8 Roman feet, says Breeze, or less. You can still see it. The turrets were built before the wall reached them, with short stubs of wall at either side, ready to be joined. At Brunton turret, the stub on one side is broad. The wall that came up to it later is narrow, about 1.8 metres instead of 3. A change of plan, frozen in the masonry. If you are enjoying this type of content, please don't forget to subscribe. Who built it? Mainly the three legions in Britain, says Breeze, with help from auxiliary units and the fleet. And they signed their work. Small building stones name the unit that built a length of wall. This one was found near Chesters. From the sixth cohort, the century of Lousius Suavis. The standard edition of Roman inscriptions from Britain lists 5 more stones naming the same century, some of them more than 20 kilometres further west. The legions did not even build their milecastle gates alike. Hill and Dobson see three designs, from three legions. Hadrian's instructions, they write, cannot have been very detailed. Then came the biggest change of all. Before the work was finished, 14 new forts were added, on the line of the Wall itself. Some went straight over what had already been built. At Housesteads, a turret now lies inside the fort walls. At Birdoswald, a turret was partly torn down to make room. And behind the Wall, to the south, came a vast earthwork: a ditch between two banks, along almost the whole length. We call it the Vallum. It could be crossed only at the forts, on causeways, through gates. Hill and Dobson put it bluntly. The forts and the Vallum are clear evidence that the original plan was flawed. Why it changed is argued over. Bidwell sees a response to resistance, and open warfare, beyond the frontier and behind it. Now a discovery made only in this century. Around the year 2000, rescue excavations at Shields Road, in Newcastle, found pits on the strip of ground between the Wall and its ditch. Three rows of them, staggered, each pit holding the bases of two obstacles. Probably forked branches, with sharpened ends. Caesar, at the siege of Alesia from our scorpion episode, says his soldiers called such things cippi. A thicket of sharpened branches, right in front of the Wall. They have since been found at several places on Tyneside. Did they run the whole length? Nobody knows. Very little of that strip has ever been dug. So what was the Wall for? The Historia Augusta says: to separate the barbarians from the Romans. Hill and Dobson saw it differently. Its strength, they wrote, lay in forbidding passage by hoof or wheel, except at the authorised and controlled gateways. Stopping men on foot was impossible. And was there a walkway along the top, with battlements, as in so many pictures? We do not know. No part of the Wall survives to its full height. It was wide enough for a walkway, says Breeze. Hill and Dobson thought a parapet likely, but not battlements for fighting, and they weighed a top that simply sloped, to shed the rain. A book published in 2025 still has whole chapters on the question. So our drawings of the top are reconstructions. The Wall had stood for only about ten years when Hadrian died. His successor, Antoninus Pius, built a new wall far to the north, in Scotland. It lasted about twenty years. Then the army came back to Hadrian's Wall, and stayed. The latest coins found along it were struck between 403 and 406. Which brings us back to the pan. It is not alone. A cup found in 1725, in a well at a Roman villa in Wiltshire, names five forts of the Wall, and shows a wall with towers in enamel. Another, found at Amiens in France in 1949, names six. A book edited by David Breeze calls them the first souvenirs. And in 2026, another was announced, from Spain. By one count, the group now numbers six. So, rigore vali Aeli Draconis. Rigore is a surveyor's word: along the line. Vali: of the Wall. Then either: of the Aelian Wall, and a man called Draco. Or a man called Aelius Draco, perhaps the soldier who had it made. Breeze takes the first reading: the Wall was called after Hadrian's family. The pan's editor thinks that possible too, but if it was the name, it did not last. If Breeze is right, the Wall's Roman name has come down to us on a souvenir pan. So, what do we really have? 80 Roman miles of foundations. Inscriptions naming Hadrian and his governor. Stones signed by the centuries that built them. A plan you can watch changing in the masonry. Pits for sharpened branches. A handful of souvenirs. What we don't have: the full height, the shape of the top, the reason for the turf, or a sure Roman name. So that's Hadrian's Wall. Not a sealed barrier, but a wall full of gates, rebuilt and rethought while it was still going up. What do you think it was for? To keep people out, or to control who came through its gates?
