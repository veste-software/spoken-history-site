A.D. 330 to A.D. 360

The King of Aksum Who Changed Gods on His Coins

One king, two gold coins: on the first, a crescent and a disc. On the second, a cross. The crescent and disc belonged to the old religion of the Red Sea lands. The cross belonged to Christ. Between them, the king of Aksum changed his god, and said so in gold. The historian Stuart Munro-Hay thought he had very strong claims to be the first ruler anywhere to use the Christian cross on his coins. Others are not so sure, and we will see why.

So when did this king change gods? Why? And how much can a coin and a stone really tell us? Aksum, on the northern edge of the Ethiopian plateau, the fourth century A.D.

Mani's four great kingdoms

First, why should anyone have cared about Aksum? A book of the Persian teacher Mani's sayings, written down by his followers, gives one answer. There are four great kingdoms in the world. The first is the kingdom of the land of Babylon and of Persia. The second is the kingdom of the Romans. The third is the kingdom of the Axumites. The fourth, Silis, may be China. And of all four, the book says: there is none that surpasses them.

Before the coins

That standing came from the sea. Nearly three centuries before Ezana, a Greek merchant's handbook, the Periplus of the Erythraean Sea, describes the Red Sea port of Adulis. From there, it says, it is eight days inland to the city of the people called Auxumites. To that place all the ivory is brought. The coast was governed by a man named Zoscales: miserly in his ways and always striving for more, but otherwise upright, and acquainted with Greek literature. Nor did they mint coins. Brass, the merchant writes, was used in cut pieces instead of coin.

Did you know? The Periplus says the coast was governed by a man named Zoscales: "miserly in his ways and always striving for more, but otherwise upright, and acquainted with Greek literature" (chapter 5, tr. Schoff 1912).

Aksum's gold

That changed late in the third century, when Aksum's kings began to strike their own coins in gold, silver and bronze. The first gold pieces were struck at half the weight of a Roman gold coin: about 2.7 grams, in gold meant to be pure. And it travelled. A hoard reported from south-west India held 27 Aksumite gold coins, and many were pierced twice beside the king's head, for mounting. No other African state south of the Sahara, Munro-Hay notes, issued coins until Kilwa, possibly in the mid-tenth century.

Did you know? A hoard reported from south-west India held 27 Aksumite gold coins, and many were pierced twice beside the king's head, for mounting (Wolfgang Hahn).

Son of the invincible Ares

Now the king himself. Ezana's early gold says, in Greek: King Ezanas. On the back: of the Aksumites, man of Alene. Above his head, the crescent and disc. His stones say more, in Greek and in his own language, Ge'ez. In Greek he is king of the Aksumites, Himyarites, and Raeidan, the Ethiopians, the Sabaeans, and more. King of kings, son of the invincible god Ares. In the Ge'ez versions, the god is Mahrem. Himyar and Saba lay in Arabia. Munro-Hay wrote that it seems certain Ezana did not actually control any of them.

The same stone tells a story. When the Beja revolted, Ezana sent his brothers Sazana and Adepha against them. They brought in six Beja kings with their peoples. For four months, the king says, about 4,400 people were fed 22,000 loaves a day. Then he settled them in his own land, and gave the six kings more than 25,000 cattle. And he thanked his god: to the invincible Ares who begat me, we consecrated a statue of gold, one of silver, and three of bronze. Remember that brother, Sazana.

Did you know? Ezana says about 4,400 Beja people were fed 22,000 loaves a day for four months, then settled them in his own land and gave their six kings more than 25,000 cattle. These are the king's own numbers, on his own stone.

Two boys from Tyre

Christianity, in the best-known story, arrived with two boys from Tyre. The church historian Rufinus told it early in the fifth century. A philosopher of Tyre, Meropius, sailed to what Rufinus calls India, with two young relatives, Frumentius and Aedesius. The ship put in for water. The custom there, Rufinus says, was to kill every Roman found if the treaty with Rome was broken. Everyone with him was killed, except the boys. They were found studying under a tree and preparing their lessons. The king made Aedesius his cupbearer, and Frumentius his treasurer and secretary. When the king died, leaving an infant son, the queen begged them with tears to help her govern until he grew up. Later, in Alexandria, Bishop Athanasius made Frumentius bishop and sent him back. And his source? These facts I know, he writes, not from the vulgar report but from the mouth of Aedesius himself. But Rufinus never names the king, or Aksum. Munro-Hay took the boy king to be Ezana. The scholar Aaron Butts finds that hard to square with King Ousanas, whom he takes to be Ezana's older brother.

Did you know? In Rufinus' story the two boys were spared because they were "found studying under a tree and preparing their lessons".

A Roman emperor writes to Ezana

For a letter from the time itself, we go to Athanasius. Around 356, he quoted a letter from the emperor Constantius II. Constantius Victor Maximus Augustus, to Æzanes and Sazanes. Ezana, and Sazana, the brother from the stone, as Butts identifies him. The emperor wanted their bishop: Send therefore speedily into Egypt the Bishop Frumentius. The man who made him bishop, the letter says, is guilty of ten thousand crimes. And our fear is lest he should pass over into Auxumis and corrupt your people. Did Aksum send him? No source we read says. But a Roman emperor wrote to Ezana by name. And there was a bishop in Aksum.

Did you know? The emperor's letter says that Athanasius, the man who made Frumentius bishop, "is guilty of ten thousand crimes" (Athanasius, Apology to Constantius 31).

Servant of Christ

Now back to Ezana's own words. In 1969, a stone with thirty-three lines of Greek was found at Aksum. By faith in God and by the power of the Father and the Son and the Holy Ghost. The son of the invincible Ares now calls himself servant of Christ. A Ge'ez stone tells the same war at length. The Noba, along the Nile, had boasted: They will not dare to cross the Takaze. He fought them at the Takaze, he says, and chased them for 23 days. In the Nile, many lost their lives, no-one knows the number. He counts the prisoners: 214 men, and 415 women. The Greek names the Father, the Son and the Holy Ghost. The Ge'ez calls on the Lord of Heaven. Ezana, Butts writes, seems to have been politically calculating in projecting his Christianity in different ways to different audiences. And the coins fit. All of Ezana's coins with crosses are gold. He did not put crosses on his silver or copper. And the gold was the coin that went abroad.

Did you know? Ezana counted his prisoners from the war against the Noba: 214 men and 415 women (his Ge'ez inscription, RIE 189).

When did he change?

So when did he change? Here the experts part. In 1991, Munro-Hay put it around 333, because some cross coins follow the weight used before Constantine reformed Rome's coinage in 324. The numismatist Wolfgang Hahn disagrees. Those coins already follow Constantine's new weight, he argues, and the change could have come in the later 340s. Yet under Constantine, the historian David Woods notes, some Roman mint officials occasionally put crosses among their issue marks. Still, Hahn writes that Christian imagery develops earlier on Aksum's coins than on Rome's. Butts adds a warning: the coins need not run in a neat line from crescent to cross. Their order is set by the scholars' story. Bronze coins with no king's name, from Ezana's time or just after, carry a cross and a motto: may this please the people.

Did you know? The motto on the nameless bronze coins, "may this please the people", is the first example of the typical Aksumite coin motto (Munro-Hay 1991).

The stela that fell

One more change stands in the ground. Aksum's kings marked their tombs with ever taller stone stelae. The largest, says the archaeologist David Phillipson, probably fell and broke while being erected. It would have stood about 30 metres, and weighed over 500 tonnes. Butts thinks it likely belonged to Ezana or to Ousanas. After it, the giant stelae seem to stop, about when Christianity arrived. He calls Ezana the first African king to adopt Christianity, and among the first kings anywhere.

Did you know? The largest stela of Aksum would have stood about 30 metres and weighed over 500 tonnes, and it probably fell and broke while being erected (David Phillipson).

Verdict

So, what do we really know? Son of Ares, then servant of Christ. The crescent, then the cross. And a Roman emperor writing to him by name. When he changed, and why, scholars still argue. But on his coins and stones we can still watch a king choose his words.

Over to you

Ezana put the cross on his gold, the coins that went abroad, but not on the silver and copper used at home. Faith, a message to the world, or both? Rufinus says he heard the story of the two boys from Aedesius himself. Would you trust it?

Quick answers

Who was King Ezana of Aksum?

Ezana was king of Aksum, on the northern edge of the Ethiopian plateau, in the fourth century A.D. His early gold says, in Greek: King Ezanas; on the back: of the Aksumites, man of Alene, with the crescent and disc above his head. In Greek his stones make him king of the Aksumites, Himyarites, and Raeidan, the Ethiopians, the Sabaeans, and more, king of kings, son of the invincible god Ares; in the Ge'ez versions the god is Mahrem. Himyar and Saba lay in Arabia, and Munro-Hay wrote that it seems certain Ezana did not actually control any of them. On a Greek stone found in 1969 he calls himself servant of Christ.

Was Ezana the first ruler to put the Christian cross on coins?

Scholars disagree. In 1991 the historian Stuart Munro-Hay thought Ezana had very strong claims to be the first ruler anywhere to use the Christian cross on his coins, because some cross coins follow the weight used before Constantine reformed Rome's coinage in 324. The numismatist Wolfgang Hahn disagrees: Aksum's gold had already been lightened to match Constantine's new coin under Ousanas, Ezana's predecessor, and kept so under Ezana until after his conversion, he argues, and the change could have come in the later 340s. Hahn also notes that Munro-Hay himself later leaned to a later chronology. The historian David Woods notes that under Constantine some Roman mint officials occasionally included crosses among their issue marks. Still, Hahn writes that Christian imagery develops earlier on Aksum's coins than on Rome's.

How did Christianity come to Aksum?

In the best-known story, told by the church historian Rufinus early in the fifth century, it arrived with two boys from Tyre, Frumentius and Aedesius. Everyone on their ship was killed except the boys, who were found studying under a tree; the king made Aedesius his cupbearer and Frumentius his treasurer and secretary, and later Bishop Athanasius in Alexandria made Frumentius bishop and sent him back. Rufinus says he heard it from Aedesius himself, but he never names the king, or Aksum. Around 356 Athanasius quoted a letter from the emperor Constantius II to Aezanes and Sazanes, demanding Bishop Frumentius: so a Roman emperor wrote to Ezana by name, and there was a bishop in Aksum.

Why did Ezana put crosses only on his gold coins?

Aaron Butts writes that all of Ezana's coins with crosses are gold, and that he did not put crosses on his silver or copper (he notes two doubtful single pieces as possible exceptions); the gold was the coin that went abroad. His stones differ in the same way: the Greek names the Father, the Son and the Holy Ghost, while the Ge'ez calls on the Lord of Heaven. Ezana, Butts writes, seems to have been politically calculating in projecting his Christianity in different ways to different audiences. Whether it was faith, a message to the world, or both, and when he changed, scholars still argue.

What happened to the largest stela of Aksum?

Aksum's kings marked their tombs with ever taller stone stelae. The largest, says the archaeologist David Phillipson, probably fell and broke while being erected; it would have stood about 30 metres and weighed over 500 tonnes. Aaron Butts thinks it likely belonged to Ezana or to Ousanas. After it, the giant stelae seem to stop, about when Christianity arrived.

Where it happened

  • Aksum (A.D. 340): Ezana's royal city on the northern edge of the Ethiopian plateau, today a town in Tigray, Ethiopia (coordinates of the modern town). The year stands for Ezana's reign, about 330 to 360 A.D.
  • Adulis (A.D. 60): The Red Sea port described in the Periplus of the Erythraean Sea, first century A.D., eight days from the city of the Auxumites (the archaeological site near Zula, Eritrea). The year is approximate.
  • Tyre (A.D. 340): Home of the philosopher Meropius and the two boys Frumentius and Aedesius in Rufinus' story. Rufinus gives no year; the year stands for Ezana's reign.
  • Alexandria (A.D. 356): Where Bishop Athanasius made Frumentius bishop; around 356 Athanasius quoted the emperor's letter to Ezana and Sazana.

    What we did not use, and why

  • "The first coins anywhere to carry the cross" stated as fact (Wikipedia; CoinWeek's "possibly"): not used; on air only Munro-Hay's own words, attributed, with the

    • dispute (Hahn, Luegmeyer, Woods, Butts).

  • "The first Christian kingdom": not claimed. Butts' "first African king to adopt Christianity" is quoted as his.
  • Wikipedia's statement that the trilingual "Ezana Stone" records the conversion: it records the pre-Christian Beja campaign (Uhlig 2001; Munro-Hay 1991), so it is

    • not used for the conversion.

  • "King Ezana's stela" for the stela still standing at Aksum: a popular name; Phillipson dates that stela to the late third or early fourth century, so we do not

    • say Ezana raised it.

  • The Kebra Nagast traditions (the Queen of Sheba, Menelik) and the tradition of the Ark of the Covenant at Aksum: outside this episode's evidence; not told.
  • Hatke's date for the Noba campaign (4 March 360) is his calculation from the stone's day and month; kept for the article, not voiced.
  • UNESCO's World Heritage page for Aksum and the Metropolitan Museum's Aksum essay refused our requests; their figures for the stelae were not used.

    Transcript

    Aksum. From crescent to cross. One king, two gold coins: on the first, a crescent and a disc. On the second, a cross. The crescent and disc belonged to the old religion of the Red Sea lands. The cross belonged to Christ. Between them, the king of Aksum changed his god, and said so in gold. The historian Stuart Munro-Hay thought he had very strong claims to be the first ruler anywhere to use the Christian cross on his coins. Others are not so sure, and we will see why. So when did this king change gods? Why? And how much can a coin and a stone really tell us? Aksum, on the northern edge of the Ethiopian plateau, the fourth century A.D. First, why should anyone have cared about Aksum? A book of the Persian teacher Mani's sayings, written down by his followers, gives one answer. There are four great kingdoms in the world. The first is the kingdom of the land of Babylon and of Persia. The second is the kingdom of the Romans. The third is the kingdom of the Axumites. The fourth, Silis, may be China. And of all four, the book says: there is none that surpasses them. That standing came from the sea. Nearly three centuries before Ezana, a Greek merchant's handbook, the Periplus of the Erythraean Sea, describes the Red Sea port of Adulis. From there, it says, it is eight days inland to the city of the people called Auxumites. To that place all the ivory is brought. The coast was governed by a man named Zoscales: miserly in his ways and always striving for more, but otherwise upright, and acquainted with Greek literature. Nor did they mint coins. Brass, the merchant writes, was used in cut pieces instead of coin. That changed late in the third century, when Aksum's kings began to strike their own coins in gold, silver and bronze. The first gold pieces were struck at half the weight of a Roman gold coin: about 2.7 grams, in gold meant to be pure. And it travelled. A hoard reported from south-west India held 27 Aksumite gold coins, and many were pierced twice beside the king's head, for mounting. No other African state south of the Sahara, Munro-Hay notes, issued coins until Kilwa, possibly in the mid-tenth century. Now the king himself. Ezana's early gold says, in Greek: King Ezanas. On the back: of the Aksumites, man of Alene. Above his head, the crescent and disc. His stones say more, in Greek and in his own language, Ge'ez. In Greek he is king of the Aksumites, Himyarites, and Raeidan, the Ethiopians, the Sabaeans, and more. King of kings, son of the invincible god Ares. In the Ge'ez versions, the god is Mahrem. Himyar and Saba lay in Arabia. Munro-Hay wrote that it seems certain Ezana did not actually control any of them. The same stone tells a story. When the Beja revolted, Ezana sent his brothers Sazana and Adepha against them. They brought in six Beja kings with their peoples. For four months, the king says, about 4,400 people were fed 22,000 loaves a day. Then he settled them in his own land, and gave the six kings more than 25,000 cattle. And he thanked his god: to the invincible Ares who begat me, we consecrated a statue of gold, one of silver, and three of bronze. Remember that brother, Sazana. Christianity, in the best-known story, arrived with two boys from Tyre. The church historian Rufinus told it early in the fifth century. A philosopher of Tyre, Meropius, sailed to what Rufinus calls India, with two young relatives, Frumentius and Aedesius. The ship put in for water. The custom there, Rufinus says, was to kill every Roman found if the treaty with Rome was broken. Everyone with him was killed, except the boys. They were found studying under a tree and preparing their lessons. The king made Aedesius his cupbearer, and Frumentius his treasurer and secretary. When the king died, leaving an infant son, the queen begged them with tears to help her govern until he grew up. Later, in Alexandria, Bishop Athanasius made Frumentius bishop and sent him back. And his source? These facts I know, he writes, not from the vulgar report but from the mouth of Aedesius himself. But Rufinus never names the king, or Aksum. Munro-Hay took the boy king to be Ezana. The scholar Aaron Butts finds that hard to square with King Ousanas, whom he takes to be Ezana's older brother. For a letter from the time itself, we go to Athanasius. Around 356, he quoted a letter from the emperor Constantius II. Constantius Victor Maximus Augustus, to Æzanes and Sazanes. Ezana, and Sazana, the brother from the stone, as Butts identifies him. The emperor wanted their bishop: Send therefore speedily into Egypt the Bishop Frumentius. The man who made him bishop, the letter says, is guilty of ten thousand crimes. And our fear is lest he should pass over into Auxumis and corrupt your people. Did Aksum send him? No source we read says. But a Roman emperor wrote to Ezana by name. And there was a bishop in Aksum. By faith in God and by the power of the Father and the Son and the Holy Ghost. The son of the invincible Ares now calls himself servant of Christ. A Ge'ez stone tells the same war at length. The Noba, along the Nile, had boasted: They will not dare to cross the Takaze. He fought them at the Takaze, he says, and chased them for twenty-three days. In the Nile, many lost their lives, no-one knows the number. He counts the prisoners: 214 men, and 415 women. The Greek names the Father, the Son and the Holy Ghost. The Ge'ez calls on the Lord of Heaven. Ezana, Butts writes, seems to have been politically calculating in projecting his Christianity in different ways to different audiences. And the coins fit. All of Ezana's coins with crosses are gold. He did not put crosses on his silver or copper. And the gold was the coin that went abroad. So when did he change? Here the experts part. In 1991, Munro-Hay put it around 333, because some cross coins follow the weight used before Constantine reformed Rome's coinage in 324. The numismatist Wolfgang Hahn disagrees. Those coins already follow Constantine's new weight, he argues, and the change could have come in the later 340s. Yet under Constantine, the historian David Woods notes, some Roman mint officials occasionally put crosses among their issue marks. Still, Hahn writes that Christian imagery develops earlier on Aksum's coins than on Rome's. Butts adds a warning: the coins need not run in a neat line from crescent to cross. Their order is set by the scholars' story. Bronze coins with no king's name, from Ezana's time or just after, carry a cross and a motto: may this please the people. One more change stands in the ground. Aksum's kings marked their tombs with ever taller stone stelae. The largest, says the archaeologist David Phillipson, probably fell and broke while being erected. It would have stood about 30 metres, and weighed over 500 tonnes. Butts thinks it likely belonged to Ezana or to Ousanas. After it, the giant stelae seem to stop, about when Christianity arrived. He calls Ezana the first African king to adopt Christianity, and among the first kings anywhere. So, what do we really know? Son of Ares, then servant of Christ. The crescent, then the cross. And a Roman emperor writing to him by name. When he changed, and why, scholars still argue. But on his coins and stones we can still watch a king choose his words. Ezana put the cross on his gold, the coins that went abroad, but not on the silver and copper used at home. Faith, a message to the world, or both?
