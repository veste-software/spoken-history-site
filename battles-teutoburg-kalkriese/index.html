Under a collapsed wall in northern Germany lay a Roman mule, still wearing its bell. A small bronze bell, and the rings of its bit, just where they had hung. It had died of a broken neck. The excavators think the bank fell on it during the fight, before looters could take the metal. Nobody disputes that a Roman army died here, at Kalkriese. The question is which one.

Where was the Teutoburg Forest?

Germany, A.D. 9. Three legions, with their cavalry and auxiliaries, were wiped out, and their general, Varus, was dead. Velleius Paterculus, who had served in Germany, called it the heaviest disaster on foreign soil since Crassus in Parthia. Tacitus gives the place a name: the Teutoburg forest. He never says exactly where it was. The hills that carry that name today, says the Kalkriese museum, were only renamed after it in the 17th century. One German history has a chapter called: "More than 700 theories". None of the writers was there. Velleius wrote 21 years later. Tacitus, about a century. Cassius Dio, about two.

Did you know? The hills that carry the name Teutoburg Forest today were only renamed after it in the seventeenth century, says the Kalkriese museum. One German history has a chapter called: More than seven hundred theories.

Varus and the warning

Velleius on Varus, as governor of Syria: He entered the rich province a poor man, but left it a rich man and the province poor. In Germany, Velleius says, Varus believed that people who could not be subdued by the sword could be soothed by the law. Often at his table, says Dio, sat a young noble of the Cherusci: Arminius. He had served with the Romans, and was, Velleius says, a Roman citizen with the rank of a knight. Another noble, Segestes, warned Varus. At a banquet, Tacitus says, he urged him to arrest Arminius, the other chiefs, and Segestes himself. Segestes had a grudge: Arminius had carried off his daughter, who was promised to another man. Varus refused to believe it. Velleius: After this first warning, there was no time left for a second.

Did you know? Arminius, who led the Germans against Varus, had served with the Romans and was, Velleius says, a Roman citizen with the rank of a knight.

The march in the storm

Dio tells the end as a march. A rising far away drew Varus out, through what he took for friendly country. He had many wagons with him, as in time of peace, and not a few women and children, and a crowd of servants. Then came violent rain and wind. In Dio's words, the tops of the trees kept breaking off and falling down. The Germans struck from all sides, through thickets they knew. The Romans camped, burned or left most of their wagons, and fought on. They were still advancing, Dio says, when the fourth day dawned. The rain had soaked their shields.

Varus' head and Augustus' cry

Velleius gives Varus' end in one line: The general had more courage to die than to fight. He ran himself through with his sword. His head was cut off and taken to Maroboduus, king of the Marcomanni, who sent it on to Augustus. Florus, writing more than a century later, says the Germans saved their worst cruelty for the Roman lawyers. One had his tongue cut out and his mouth sewn shut. At last, you viper, a German said, you have ceased to hiss. And Augustus? People said, Suetonius reports, that he let his hair and beard grow for months, and sometimes beat his head against a door, crying: Quintilius Varus, give me back my legions!

1987: Tony Clunn's coins

In March 1987, a British army officer, Tony Clunn, asked the county archaeologist for permission to search with a metal detector. The archaeologist, Wolfgang Schlüter, later wrote: The fact he had actually asked permission before conducting the work spoke for him. That July, by the edge of a great bog, Clunn found 105 Roman silver coins, from a hoard broken up by the plough. Theodor Mommsen had pointed to this area a century earlier, because farmers kept finding Roman coins. He did not prevail. Then Clunn found lead sling bullets. Coins can come by trade, Schlüter reasoned. Sling bullets meant soldiers.

Did you know? The Kalkriese finds began with good manners. In 1987 a British army officer, Tony Clunn, asked the county archaeologist for permission before searching with a metal detector. What the Kalkriese field holds

The main field, the Oberesch, lies where the passage between Kalkriese hill and the Great Bog is narrowest. Across the field, the excavators traced a bank of turf and sand, about 400 metres long, zigzagging, with gaps to run out through. Nearly 6,000 Roman objects have come out of the ground at Kalkriese. On the main field, hardly any is complete. The field was looted. The most famous, the iron face mask of a cavalry helmet, was once covered in silver foil. The plunderers cut it off. In front of the bank lay a second mule, with a large bronze bell and a chain. Its bell, the excavators think, had been used to mend a wagon pole. Straw of peas and oats, one report says, survived inside it. Wagons, as Dio said. In 2018, a block of soil from here held a Roman segmented cuirass, a prisoner's shackle at its neck.

What the coins can date

Then the coins, about 2,000 of them by 2019, a science magazine reported. The coin expert Frank Berger pointed out that none of them is later than Varus. Some copper coins carry a small stamp, the letters V, A, R, long read as Varus: a cash gift from the general to his men. But a coin in the museum at Rennes, in France, is reported to have that stamp struck over a mark of Tiberius, who took power after Varus died. And coins stay in use for decades. The museum says its finds cannot be dated to within six years. Six years. Hold on to that number.

Six years later: Tacitus' field of bones

Six years after the disaster, in A.D. 15, the general Germanicus led an army to the field. Tacitus describes Varus' first camp, then a half ruined wall and a shallow ditch, where the broken remnant had taken cover. In the plain between were bleaching bones, scattered or in little heaps, as the men had fallen, fleeing or standing fast. Human skulls were nailed to the tree trunks. Survivors told where the eagles were taken, and where Varus died by his own hand. No man knew, Tacitus says, whether he buried a stranger or a kinsman. At Kalkriese, the excavators found eight pits filled with the bones of men, mules and horses. The bones had lain in the open for years before they were buried. Between two and ten years, says one study. Almost all are from men of about twenty to forty. In one pit, two skulls had been laid with care on long bones.

Did you know? The bones in the Kalkriese pits had lain in the open for years before they were buried: between two and ten years, says one study. In one pit, two skulls had been laid with care on long bones.

Varus or Caecina?

The excavators read these pits as the burial Tacitus describes. But they admit no trace of Germanicus' burial mound has been found. And that same year, Germanicus' general Caecina was attacked at what Tacitus calls the long bridges, a narrow causeway through marshes. The historian Reinhard Wolters argued the coins could fit that battle too. Peter Kehne put his view in a title: Much speaks against Mommsen. Everything against Kalkriese. And the bank? Later digs found V-shaped ditches, the kind Roman marching camps have. Now the museum thinks the Romans may have camped here, and been overrun. A recent soil study even suggests the bank could be medieval. Then, in 2026, a metal study found the Kalkriese bronzes close to those from the old base of the Nineteenth Legion, one of Varus' three. A second study finds final proof still lacking, and no trace of the women and children Dio describes.

Marcus Caelius: a face from the battle

One man of the Varus war does have a face. A stone found near Xanten, on the Rhine, in 1620, shows Marcus Caelius, a centurion of the Eighteenth Legion, wearing his decorations. It says: "He fell in the Varian War." He was 53½ years old. His brother Publius set it up. The stone also says: His bones may be interred here. Tacitus names the Nineteenth Legion. This stone names the Eighteenth.

Did you know? The stone of Marcus Caelius says he was fifty-three and a half years old. His brother Publius set it up, and the stone also says: His bones may be interred here.

Verdict

So, is Kalkriese the Teutoburg forest? A Roman army was destroyed here, around the time of Varus. Most German specialists, a science magazine reported, think it was one scene of his defeat. Proof is still missing. But the ground gives what the writers could not: a mule with a broken neck, a stripped mask, bones that lay for years in the rain. Whose battle do you think Kalkriese is: Varus', or Caecina's six years later?

Quick answers

Where was the Battle of the Teutoburg Forest?

We do not know for certain. Tacitus gives the place a name, the Teutoburg forest, but never says exactly where it was, and the hills that carry that name today, says the Kalkriese museum, were only renamed after it in the seventeenth century. One German history has a chapter called: More than seven hundred theories. The main candidate is Kalkriese, near Osnabrück in northern Germany. A Roman army was destroyed there, around the time of Varus. Most German specialists, a science magazine reported, think it was one scene of his defeat. Proof is still missing.

What was found at Kalkriese?

In 1987 a British army officer, Tony Clunn, found 105 Roman silver coins by the edge of a great bog, then lead sling bullets. Since then nearly six thousand Roman objects have come out of the ground at Kalkriese; on the main field hardly any is complete, because the field was looted. The excavators traced a bank of turf and sand about four hundred metres long. Under it lay a Roman mule, still wearing its bell, with a broken neck. The most famous find, the iron face mask of a cavalry helmet, was once covered in silver foil; the plunderers cut it off. In 2018 a block of soil held a Roman segmented cuirass, a prisoner's shackle at its neck. And eight pits held the bones of men, mules and horses.

Is Kalkriese Varus' battlefield, or Caecina's?

That is disputed. The coin expert Frank Berger pointed out that none of the coins is later than Varus. But a coin at Rennes is reported to have the VAR stamp struck over a mark of Tiberius, who took power after Varus died, and the museum itself says its finds cannot be dated to within six years. In A.D. 15, six years after the disaster, Germanicus' general Caecina was attacked at what Tacitus calls the long bridges, and the historian Reinhard Wolters argued the coins could fit that battle too. In 2026 a metal study found the Kalkriese bronzes close to those from the old base of the Nineteenth Legion, one of Varus' three. A second study finds final proof still lacking, and no trace of the women and children Dio describes.

What happened to Varus?

Velleius Paterculus gives his end in one line: The general had more courage to die than to fight. He ran himself through with his sword. His head was cut off and taken to Maroboduus, king of the Marcomanni, who sent it on to Augustus. People said, Suetonius reports, that Augustus let his hair and beard grow for months, and sometimes beat his head against a door, crying: Quintilius Varus, give me back my legions!

Who was Marcus Caelius?

A centurion of the Eighteenth Legion, one of Varus' three. His gravestone, found near Xanten, on the Rhine, in 1620, shows him wearing his military decorations. It says: He fell in the Varian War. He was fifty-three and a half years old. His brother Publius set it up, and the stone also says: His bones may be interred here.

Where it happened

  • Kalkriese, the Oberesch field (A.D. 9): Where the passage between Kalkriese hill and the Great Bog is narrowest. A Roman army was destroyed here around the time of Varus; whether it was his, or Caecina's six years later, is disputed.
  • The hills called the Teutoburg Forest today : Tacitus names the Teutoburg forest but never says exactly where it was. These hills, says the Kalkriese museum, were only renamed after it in the seventeenth century. The pin marks the region.
  • Osnabrück (A.D. 1987): Tony Clunn asked the county archaeologist of Osnabrück, Wolfgang Schlüter, for permission to search with a metal detector in March 1987.
  • Near Xanten, on the Rhine (A.D. 1620): The gravestone of Marcus Caelius, centurion of the Eighteenth Legion, who fell in the Varian War, was found near here in 1620.
  • Dangstetten : The old base of the Nineteenth Legion. In 2026 a metal study found the Kalkriese bronzes close to those from here.
  • Rennes, France : A coin in the museum here has the VAR stamp struck over a mark of the emperor Tiberius, as reported.

Teutoburg Forest. The mule under the wall. Under a collapsed wall in northern Germany lay a Roman mule, still wearing its bell. A small bronze bell, and the rings of its bit, just where they had hung. It had died of a broken neck. The excavators think the bank fell on it during the fight, before looters could take the metal. Nobody disputes that a Roman army died here, at Kalkriese. The question is which one. Germany, A.D. 9. Three legions, with their cavalry and auxiliaries, were wiped out, and their general, Varus, was dead. Velleius Paterculus, who had served in Germany, called it the heaviest disaster on foreign soil since Crassus in Parthia. Tacitus gives the place a name: the Teutoburg forest. He never says exactly where it was. The hills that carry that name today, says the Kalkriese museum, were only renamed after it in the 17th century. One German history has a chapter called: "More than 700 theories". None of the writers was there. Velleius wrote 21 years later. Tacitus, about a century. Cassius Dio, about two. Velleius on Varus, as governor of Syria: He entered the rich province a poor man, but left it a rich man and the province poor. In Germany, Velleius says, Varus believed that people who could not be subdued by the sword could be soothed by the law. Often at his table, says Dio, sat a young noble of the Cherusci: Arminius. He had served with the Romans, and was, Velleius says, a Roman citizen with the rank of a knight. Another noble, Segestes, warned Varus. At a banquet, Tacitus says, he urged him to arrest Arminius, the other chiefs, and Segestes himself. Segestes had a grudge: Arminius had carried off his daughter, who was promised to another man. Varus refused to believe it. Velleius: After this first warning, there was no time left for a second. Dio tells the end as a march. A rising far away drew Varus out, through what he took for friendly country. He had many wagons with him, as in time of peace, and not a few women and children, and a crowd of servants. Then came violent rain and wind. In Dio's words, the tops of the trees kept breaking off and falling down. The Germans struck from all sides, through thickets they knew. The Romans camped, burned or left most of their wagons, and fought on. They were still advancing, Dio says, when the fourth day dawned. The rain had soaked their shields. Velleius gives Varus' end in one line: The general had more courage to die than to fight. He ran himself through with his sword. His head was cut off and taken to Maroboduus, king of the Marcomanni, who sent it on to Augustus. Florus, writing more than a century later, says the Germans saved their worst cruelty for the Roman lawyers. One had his tongue cut out and his mouth sewn shut. At last, you viper, a German said, you have ceased to hiss. And Augustus? People said, Suetonius reports, that he let his hair and beard grow for months, and sometimes beat his head against a door, crying: Quintilius Varus, give me back my legions! In March 1987, a British army officer, Tony Clunn, asked the county archaeologist for permission to search with a metal detector. The archaeologist, Wolfgang Schlüter, later wrote: The fact he had actually asked permission before conducting the work spoke for him. That July, by the edge of a great bog, Clunn found 105 Roman silver coins, from a hoard broken up by the plough. Theodor Mommsen had pointed to this area a century earlier, because farmers kept finding Roman coins. He did not prevail. Then Clunn found lead sling bullets. Coins can come by trade, Schlüter reasoned. Sling bullets meant soldiers. The main field, the Oberesch, lies where the passage between Kalkriese hill and the Great Bog is narrowest. Across the field, the excavators traced a bank of turf and sand, about 400 metres long, zigzagging, with gaps to run out through. Nearly 6,000 Roman objects have come out of the ground at Kalkriese. On the main field, hardly any is complete. The field was looted. The most famous, the iron face mask of a cavalry helmet, was once covered in silver foil. The plunderers cut it off. In front of the bank lay a second mule, with a large bronze bell and a chain. Its bell, the excavators think, had been used to mend a wagon pole. Straw of peas and oats, one report says, survived inside it. Wagons, as Dio said. In 2018, a block of soil from here held a Roman segmented cuirass, a prisoner's shackle at its neck. Then the coins, about 2,000 of them by 2019, a science magazine reported. The coin expert Frank Berger pointed out that none of them is later than Varus. Some copper coins carry a small stamp, the letters V, A, R, long read as Varus: a cash gift from the general to his men. But a coin in the museum at Rennes, in France, is reported to have that stamp struck over a mark of Tiberius, who took power after Varus died. And coins stay in use for decades. The museum says its finds cannot be dated to within six years. Six years. Hold on to that number. Six years after the disaster, in A.D. 15, the general Germanicus led an army to the field. Tacitus describes Varus' first camp, then a half ruined wall and a shallow ditch, where the broken remnant had taken cover. In the plain between were bleaching bones, scattered or in little heaps, as the men had fallen, fleeing or standing fast. Human skulls were nailed to the tree trunks. Survivors told where the eagles were taken, and where Varus died by his own hand. No man knew, Tacitus says, whether he buried a stranger or a kinsman. At Kalkriese, the excavators found eight pits filled with the bones of men, mules and horses. The bones had lain in the open for years before they were buried. Between two and ten years, says one study. Almost all are from men of about twenty to forty. In one pit, two skulls had been laid with care on long bones. The excavators read these pits as the burial Tacitus describes. But they admit no trace of Germanicus' burial mound has been found. And that same year, Germanicus' general Caecina was attacked at what Tacitus calls the long bridges, a narrow causeway through marshes. The historian Reinhard Wolters argued the coins could fit that battle too. Peter Kehne put his view in a title: Much speaks against Mommsen. Everything against Kalkriese. And the bank? Later digs found V-shaped ditches, the kind Roman marching camps have. Now the museum thinks the Romans may have camped here, and been overrun. A recent soil study even suggests the bank could be medieval. Then, in 2026, a metal study found the Kalkriese bronzes close to those from the old base of the Nineteenth Legion, one of Varus' three. A second study finds final proof still lacking, and no trace of the women and children Dio describes. One man of the Varus war does have a face. A stone found near Xanten, on the Rhine, in 1620, shows Marcus Caelius, a centurion of the Eighteenth Legion, wearing his decorations. It says: "He fell in the Varian War." He was 53½ years old. His brother Publius set it up. The stone also says: His bones may be interred here. Tacitus names the Nineteenth Legion. This stone names the Eighteenth. So, is Kalkriese the Teutoburg forest? A Roman army was destroyed here, around the time of Varus. Most German specialists, a science magazine reported, think it was one scene of his defeat. Proof is still missing. But the ground gives what the writers could not: a mule with a broken neck, a stripped mask, bones that lay for years in the rain. Whose battle do you think Kalkriese is: Varus', or Caecina's six years later? And Segestes: a loyal friend of Rome, or a father taking revenge on the man who took his daughter?
