Domesday Book: Why Was It Made?

Gloucester. Christmas, 1085. King William, conqueror of England nineteen years before, holds court here for five days. Then, says the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, he had very deep consultation with his council about this land: how it was occupied, and by what sort of men. And he sent his men over all England, into each shire. So very narrowly ... did he commission them to trace it out, the chronicler writes, that ... not even an ox, nor a cow, nor a swine was there left, that was not set down in his writ. The chronicler finds it shameful even to tell. The king, he says, thought it no shame to do it.

The oldest of the public records

What his men wrote down became Domesday Book. Two volumes, now at The National Archives in Kew. The archive's catalogue calls them the oldest of the public records. There is nothing like it in England, the archive says, until the censuses of the 1800s. But it is not a census of people. It lists land and what it was worth, and it counts mostly heads of households, not every person. And why was it made? No source from the time says exactly. Historians still argue.

Is Domesday Book the oldest record in England? Scope matters. The National Archives' catalogue calls it the oldest of the public records, and the archive's exhibition calls it Britain's earliest public record. That is not the same as the oldest document, or a census: it lists land and what it was worth, and counts mostly heads of households.

Why that winter?

First, why that winter? In 1085, says the Chronicle, men reported that Cnut, King of Denmark, was coming to win this land. William came over from Normandy with so large an army, the chronicler writes, that men wondered how this land could feed all that force. His subjects had to feed the soldiers, each according to his land. And the coast was laid waste, so an invader would find nothing to seize. The Danes never came. But The National Archives draws the link: to pay that army, William needed to know what his kingdom could give.

How the survey worked

The survey probably began in January 1086. England, except the far north, was split into circuits, groups of shires. Seven or more, says The National Archives, each with three or four royal commissioners. Barons, churchmen and sheriffs sent in lists of manors and men. Then came a second team. Robert, Bishop of Hereford, wrote at the time that other investigators were sent after the others, as strangers into unfamiliar shires, to find fault with the other men's survey, and report the guilty to the king. In the shire courts, juries swore to the answers. Half English, half French, says The National Archives. And the questions survive, at the head of a collection known as the Ely inquest. What is the manor called? Who held it in King Edward's time? Who holds it now? How many hides? How many ploughs? How many villans, how many cottars, how many slaves? How many mills, how many fisheries? How much was it worth, and how much now? And the last one: if more can be had from it than is had now. All of it three times: under King Edward, when King William gave it, and now.

One entry, decoded

Here is one answer, from Oxfordshire. Cogges. Held from Bishop Odo of Bayeux by a man called Wadard. A Wadard is also stitched into the Bayeux Tapestry, one of Odo's men. That he is the same man is not proven, says the historian Hugh Thomas, but as far as he knows, no one has seriously challenged it. Five hides for the tax. Land for eight ploughs. Two plough teams on the lord's land. Three slaves. A mill, worth ten shillings. Meadow, pasture and wood. Its value: ten pounds when King Edward was alive. Ten pounds now. And Wadard, says Thomas, held forty estates in Domesday Book, mostly from Odo. The tapestry shows how the Normans told the conquest. Domesday shows what they held, twenty years on.

Exon: the draft that kept the animals

Now the books. And a twist. The earliest surviving manuscript of the survey is not at Kew. It is at Exeter Cathedral: Exon Domesday. It covers just five shires of the south-west. Its pages are smaller, but even incomplete, it has more leaves than Great Domesday, which covers thirty-one. About two dozen scribes wrote it, says the cathedral, most of them, to judge by their handwriting and spelling, French-speaking. Possibly in a matter of months in 1086. And Exon counts the animals. Sheep, goats, cows, horses, pigs. Then a scribe condensed it for the king's great book. Comparing the two, the historian Stephen Baxter finds he cut the manor descriptions by about 40 per cent. And he left out the livestock. In Great Domesday, says the Hull Domesday Project, livestock appears only now and then, apart from plough oxen, and pigs used to measure woodland. And even the drafts counted the lords' animals, not the peasants'. The chronicler's pigs were real. But not every one was set down.

Did you know? The draft is bigger than the book. Exon Domesday covers just five shires of the south-west, yet even incomplete it has more leaves than Great Domesday, which covers thirty-one; its pages are smaller (Exeter Cathedral, "Exon Domesday").

Did you know? The animals the chronicler complained about mostly vanished from the king's book. Exon counts sheep, goats, cows, horses and pigs; comparing the two, Stephen Baxter finds the scribe of Great Domesday cut the manor descriptions by about 40 per cent and left out the livestock. Even the drafts counted only the lords' animals, not the peasants' (Baxter, English Historical Review 2020; Hull Domesday Project).

Great and Little Domesday

Great Domesday is mostly the work of one main scribe, with a second working closely beside him, says The National Archives. 413 leaves of parchment. Essex, Norfolk and Suffolk were never condensed. Their draft was kept instead: Little Domesday, written by about six scribes. Its last page gives a date. "In the 1086th year from the incarnation of [our] Lord [and] in the 20th [year] of the reign of [King] William, this survey was made ...". And some of England is missing. No London. No Winchester, where the book was first kept. No County Durham, no Northumberland.

When was the book written?

And when was Great Domesday written? Scholars disagree. Little Domesday's last page dates the survey to 1086. Not the great book. Stephen Baxter thinks the main scribe began in the autumn of 1086 and worked for about a year, and that news of William's death, on 9 September 1087, changed the plan. David Roffe argues the book was not started much before 1088, under William's son, William Rufus.

Disputed: the date of Great Domesday. The National Archives says the exact timeline has been much debated by historians, and Stephen Baxter writes that there is no decisive evidence to prove when it was written. Its catalogue also notes the suggestion that the book was compiled under William II, in or after 1089.

Why was it made? The historians

Now the real question. Why? For the historian J. H. Round, the survey was about the geld, the land tax. V. H. Galbraith said no: the whole point was the book, a record of the king's lands and his lordship. James Holt looked at what came next. On 1 August 1086, at Salisbury, says the Chronicle, all the landholders of any account bowed to William, became his men, and swore him oaths. For Holt, that oath was the climax of the survey. David Roffe splits it in two. The survey was the answer to the Danish threat, about tax and knight service. The book came later, for other reasons. Sally Harvey sees taxes too, but also a check on the king's officials, and security for the landholders. Her book is called Domesday: Book of Judgement. And Stephen Baxter calls the survey a fiscal exercise in the broadest sense: to get the most from every source of royal income. The barons went along, he thinks, because they got something back: a written record of their holdings, much like a confirmation charter. The National Archives' catalogue calls it all three: a tax inventory, a statement of who held what from whom, and a legal record of who held land by right.

Why 'Domesday'?

And the name? It is not in the book. At first it was the Book of Winchester, after the treasury where it was kept. About ninety years after the survey, Richard FitzNigel, an official of the Exchequer, explained. This book is called by the natives Domesday, he wrote. By metaphor, the day of judgement. Because when the book is appealed to, its sentence cannot be scorned or avoided with impunity. So Domesday was not King William's word. It was what people called his book. And the man who wrote most of Great Domesday? He left no name. Only his handwriting. In 2025, Baxter, Julia Crick and Chris Lewis proposed one: Gerard, William's last chancellor, later Archbishop of York. A proposal, not yet proven.

Did you know? "Domesday" was not King William's name for his book. The word is not in the book itself (The National Archives); it was first the Book of Winchester, and about ninety years later Richard FitzNigel wrote that the natives called it Domesday, by metaphor, the day of judgement (Dialogue of the Exchequer I.16, tr. E. F. Henderson, 1896).

Did you know? The main scribe of Great Domesday may have a name. In 2025 Stephen Baxter, Julia Crick and Chris Lewis proposed Gerard, William's last chancellor, later Archbishop of York. If confirmed, he would be one of the few named people directly linked to the survey; for now it is a proposal, not yet proven (University of Oxford news, 2 July 2025).

Verdict

So why was Domesday Book made? The sources never say exactly. We have the king's questions, and his men's answers. And the questions ask, manor by manor, who held the land under King Edward, and who holds it now. Was Domesday a tax book, a register of who held what, or a book of judgement?

Quick answers

Why was Domesday Book made?

No source from the time says exactly, and historians still argue. For J. H. Round the survey was about the geld, the land tax. V. H. Galbraith said the whole point was the book, a record of the king's lands and his lordship. James Holt saw the oath of Salisbury on 1 August 1086 as the climax of the survey. David Roffe splits it in two: the survey answered the Danish threat, the book came later for other reasons. Sally Harvey sees taxes, but also a check on the king's officials and security for the landholders. Stephen Baxter calls it a fiscal exercise in the broadest sense. The National Archives' catalogue calls it all three: a tax inventory, a statement of who held what from whom, and a legal record of who held land by right.

Why is it called Domesday Book?

The word is not in the book. At first it was the Book of Winchester, after the treasury where it was kept. About ninety years after the survey, Richard FitzNigel, an official of the Exchequer, wrote that it was called by the natives Domesday, by metaphor, the day of judgement, because when the book is appealed to, its sentence cannot be scorned or avoided with impunity. So Domesday was not King William's word; it was what people called his book.

What did Domesday Book record?

Land and what it was worth. The questions that survive in the Ely inquest ask what the manor is called, who held it in King Edward's time and who holds it now, how many hides and ploughs, how many villans, cottars and slaves, how many mills and fisheries, and how much it was worth then and now: all of it three times, under King Edward, when King William gave it, and now. It is not a census of people: it counts mostly heads of households. And though the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle complained that not even an ox, a cow or a swine was left out, livestock appears in Great Domesday only now and then, says the Hull Domesday Project; the drafts, such as Exon Domesday, counted the lords' animals, not the peasants'.

When was Domesday Book written?

The survey probably began in January 1086, and the last page of Little Domesday dates the survey to 1086. When Great Domesday itself was written, scholars disagree. Stephen Baxter thinks the main scribe began in the autumn of 1086 and worked for about a year, and that news of William's death, on 9 September 1087, changed the plan. David Roffe argues the book was not started much before 1088, under William's son, William Rufus.

Who wrote Domesday Book?

Great Domesday is mostly the work of one main scribe, with a second working closely beside him, says The National Archives. Little Domesday was written by about six scribes, and the Exon Domesday draft at Exeter by about two dozen, says the cathedral. The main scribe of Great Domesday left no name, only his handwriting. In 2025 Stephen Baxter, Julia Crick and Chris Lewis proposed that he was Gerard, William's last chancellor, later Archbishop of York: a proposal, not yet proven.

Where it happened

  • Gloucester (A.D. 1085): King William held court here for five days at Christmas 1085 and ordered the survey (Anglo-Saxon Chronicle; The National Archives).
  • Salisbury (the Chronicle's Sarum) (A.D. 1086): On 1 August 1086 the landholders of any account bowed to William here and swore him oaths (Anglo-Saxon Chronicle). For James Holt, the climax of the survey. The marker is approximate.
  • Hereford (A.D. 1086): Robert, Bishop of Hereford, wrote at the time of the second team sent to find fault with the first survey.
  • Ely (A.D. 1086): The questions of the survey survive at the head of the collection known as the Ely inquest.
  • Cogges, Oxfordshire (A.D. 1086): Held from Bishop Odo of Bayeux by Wadard: five hides, land for eight ploughs, a mill worth ten shillings, worth ten pounds then and now.
  • Exeter (A.D. 1086): Exeter Cathedral keeps Exon Domesday, the earliest surviving manuscript of the survey (Stephen Baxter).
  • Winchester (A.D. 1086): The book was first kept in the treasury here and first called the Book of Winchester; the city itself is not in Great Domesday (The National Archives).
  • Kew (The National Archives) (A.D. 1086): Where Great and Little Domesday are kept today.

    Read the full narration

    Domesday Book. Why William counted England. Gloucester. Christmas, 1085. King William, conqueror of England nineteen years before, holds court here for five days. Then, says the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, he had very deep consultation with his council about this land: how it was occupied, and by what sort of men. And he sent his men over all England, into each shire. So very narrowly ... did he commission them to trace it out, the chronicler writes, that ... not even an ox, nor a cow, nor a swine was there left, that was not set down in his writ. The chronicler finds it shameful even to tell. The king, he says, thought it no shame to do it. What his men wrote down became Domesday Book. Two volumes, now at The National Archives in Kew. The archive's catalogue calls them the oldest of the public records. There is nothing like it in England, the archive says, until the censuses of the 1800s. But it is not a census of people. It lists land and what it was worth, and it counts mostly heads of households, not every person. And why was it made? No source from the time says exactly. Historians still argue. First, why that winter? In 1085, says the Chronicle, men reported that Cnut, King of Denmark, was coming to win this land. William came over from Normandy with so large an army, the chronicler writes, that men wondered how this land could feed all that force. His subjects had to feed the soldiers, each according to his land. And the coast was laid waste, so an invader would find nothing to seize. The Danes never came. But The National Archives draws the link: to pay that army, William needed to know what his kingdom could give. The survey probably began in January 1086. England, except the far north, was split into circuits, groups of shires. Seven or more, says The National Archives, each with three or four royal commissioners. Barons, churchmen and sheriffs sent in lists of manors and men. Then came a second team. Robert, Bishop of Hereford, wrote at the time that other investigators were sent after the others, as strangers into unfamiliar shires, to find fault with the other men's survey, and report the guilty to the king. In the shire courts, juries swore to the answers. Half English, half French, says The National Archives. And the questions survive, at the head of a collection known as the Ely inquest. What is the manor called? Who held it in King Edward's time? Who holds it now? How many hides? How many ploughs? How many villans, how many cottars, how many slaves? How many mills, how many fisheries? How much was it worth, and how much now? And the last one: if more can be had from it than is had now. All of it three times: under King Edward, when King William gave it, and now. Here is one answer, from Oxfordshire. Cogges. Held from Bishop Odo of Bayeux by a man called Wadard. A Wadard is also stitched into the Bayeux Tapestry, one of Odo's men. That he is the same man is not proven, says the historian Hugh Thomas, but as far as he knows, no one has seriously challenged it. Five hides for the tax. Land for eight ploughs. Two plough teams on the lord's land. Three slaves. A mill, worth ten shillings. Meadow, pasture and wood. Its value: ten pounds when King Edward was alive. Ten pounds now. And Wadard, says Thomas, held forty estates in Domesday Book, mostly from Odo. The tapestry shows how the Normans told the conquest. Domesday shows what they held, twenty years on. Now the books. And a twist. The earliest surviving manuscript of the survey is not at Kew. It is at Exeter Cathedral: Exon Domesday. It covers just five shires of the south-west. Its pages are smaller, but even incomplete, it has more leaves than Great Domesday, which covers thirty-one. About two dozen scribes wrote it, says the cathedral, most of them, to judge by their handwriting and spelling, French-speaking. Possibly in a matter of months in 1086. And Exon counts the animals. Sheep, goats, cows, horses, pigs. Then a scribe condensed it for the king's great book. Comparing the two, the historian Stephen Baxter finds he cut the manor descriptions by about 40 per cent. And he left out the livestock. In Great Domesday, says the Hull Domesday Project, livestock appears only now and then, apart from plough oxen, and pigs used to measure woodland. And even the drafts counted the lords' animals, not the peasants'. The chronicler's pigs were real. But not every one was set down. Great Domesday is mostly the work of one main scribe, with a second working closely beside him, says The National Archives. 413 leaves of parchment. Essex, Norfolk and Suffolk were never condensed. Their draft was kept instead: Little Domesday, written by about six scribes. Its last page gives a date. "In the 1086th year from the incarnation of [our] Lord [and] in the 20th [year] of the reign of [King] William, this survey was made ...". And some of England is missing. No London. No Winchester, where the book was first kept. No County Durham, no Northumberland. And when was Great Domesday written? Scholars disagree. Little Domesday's last page dates the survey to 1086. Not the great book. Stephen Baxter thinks the main scribe began in the autumn of 1086 and worked for about a year, and that news of William's death, on 9 September 1087, changed the plan. David Roffe argues the book was not started much before 1088, under William's son, William Rufus. Now the real question. Why? For the historian J. H. Round, the survey was about the geld, the land tax. V. H. Galbraith said no: the whole point was the book, a record of the king's lands and his lordship. James Holt looked at what came next. On 1 August 1086, at Salisbury, says the Chronicle, all the landholders of any account bowed to William, became his men, and swore him oaths. For Holt, that oath was the climax of the survey. David Roffe splits it in two. The survey was the answer to the Danish threat, about tax and knight service. The book came later, for other reasons. Sally Harvey sees taxes too, but also a check on the king's officials, and security for the landholders. Her book is called Domesday: Book of Judgement. And Stephen Baxter calls the survey a fiscal exercise in the broadest sense: to get the most from every source of royal income. The barons went along, he thinks, because they got something back: a written record of their holdings, much like a confirmation charter. The National Archives' catalogue calls it all three: a tax inventory, a statement of who held what from whom, and a legal record of who held land by right. And the name? It is not in the book. At first it was the Book of Winchester, after the treasury where it was kept. About ninety years after the survey, Richard FitzNigel, an official of the Exchequer, explained. This book is called by the natives Domesday, he wrote. By metaphor, the day of judgement. Because when the book is appealed to, its sentence cannot be scorned or avoided with impunity. So Domesday was not King William's word. It was what people called his book. And the man who wrote most of Great Domesday? He left no name. Only his handwriting. In 2025, Baxter, Julia Crick and Chris Lewis proposed one: Gerard, William's last chancellor, later Archbishop of York. A proposal, not yet proven. So why was Domesday Book made? The sources never say exactly. We have the king's questions, and his men's answers. And the questions ask, manor by manor, who held the land under King Edward, and who holds it now. Was Domesday a tax book, a register of who held what, or a book of judgement? And why do you think London and Winchester were left out?
