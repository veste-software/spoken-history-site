Salamis, 480 BC: Xerxes' Scribes Took Names

A king sat below a mountain, watching his navy fight. Whenever one of his ships did something brave, his scribes wrote down the captain's name. His father's name, too, and his city. The king was Xerxes of Persia. The strait below him was Salamis, beside Athens. Probably late September, 480 B.C. We know about the scribes from Herodotus, who wrote some fifty years later, from what people told him. He is not the only one who wrote this battle down. A playwright staged it eight years later. A stone tells a third version. They do not agree. And what the king saw from his seat led to rewards, and to beheadings. So what did it mean, that day, to be watched by the king?

Did you know? Herodotus' own words on the scribes: “Whenever Xerxes, as he sat beneath the mountain opposite Salamis which is called Aegaleos, saw one of his own men achieve some feat in the battle, he inquired who did it, and his scribes wrote down the captain's name with his father and city of residence.” (Histories 8.90, tr. A. D. Godley, 1920, as revised on Perseus)

Silver and an oracle

It starts with silver. The mines at Laurion paid Athens a windfall: ten drachmas a man. A politician named Themistocles talked them out of it. Build two hundred ships instead, he said, for the war with Aegina, the island next door. With Xerxes coming, the oracle at Delphi gave Athens a dark answer. A wooden wall would save them. And: Divine Salamis, you will bring death to women's sons. Themistocles argued that if it meant the Athenians, the god would have said cruel Salamis, not divine. The wooden wall was the fleet. So Athens left. Herodotus says the Athenians fed a sacred snake on the Acropolis a honey cake every month. This time the cake lay untouched. The priestess took it to mean the goddess had left. The people left faster.

A man without a city

The Persians took the Acropolis and burned it. The Greek fleet lay off Salamis. Many commanders wanted to fall back to the Isthmus and defend the Peloponnese. Themistocles spoke before the meeting had even opened. The Corinthian general, Adeimantus, cut in: Themistocles, at the games those who start before the signal are beaten with rods. Those left behind, said Themistocles, win no crown. Adeimantus called him a man without a city, since Athens had fallen. Themistocles threatened to take the Athenians off to Italy. The Spartan in command, Eurybiades, stayed.

The message in the night

The others still wanted to leave. So Themistocles sent a man by boat to the Persians. His name was Sicinnus. He was Themistocles' servant, and looked after his sons. The Athenian general is on the king's side, he told them. The Greeks are terrified, and plan flight. The Persians believed him, and through the night they closed the exits of the strait. Herodotus names him. But Aeschylus, eight years on, tells the same trick: A Hellene, from the Athenian host, came to your son Xerxes. Then a ship from the island of Tenos deserted the Persian side, and told the Greeks the truth: they were surrounded. For that, Herodotus says, the Tenians were engraved on the Greek victory monument at Delphi. Its bronze column stands in Istanbul, and their name is still on it.

Dawn in the strait

The ships were triremes: 170 oars, one man to each oar. A full size reconstruction, Olympias, was first rowed in 1987: 37 metres long, and just under 9 knots in a sprint. Its builders had no wreck to copy. A holed trireme did not sink to the bottom. It floated. The Persians had rowed all night. Here is the dawn, in Aeschylus: "A loud cheer like a song of triumph first rang out from the Hellenes ... clear from the island crags, an echo returned an answering cry." Then the shout: "On, you men of Hellas! Free your native land. Free your children, your wives ..."

Did you know? In Aeschylus the Greek shout goes on: “Free your children, your wives, the temples of your fathers' gods, and the tombs of your ancestors. Now you are fighting for all you have.” (Persians, tr. H. W. Smyth, 1926, as revised on Perseus)

Fighting for the king's eyes

Xerxes watched from the shore. And his men knew it. Herodotus says they were far braver than they had been off Euboea, each one thinking that the king was watching him. That had a price. Ships at the back pushed forward to show the king a brave deed, and crashed into their own ships falling back. Few Greeks drowned, Herodotus says, because they could swim. Many on the Persian side could not. The king's own brother, Ariabignes, died. And the king rewarded. Two captains from Samos took Greek ships. Theomestor was made ruler of Samos. Phylacus was written down as a benefactor of the king, and given much land.

Did you know? The king's benefactors had a title of their own. Herodotus, after naming Phylacus: “The king's benefactors are called “orosangae” in the Persian language.” (Histories 8.85, tr. Godley, revised on Perseus)

Artemisia

One captain the king did see was a woman: Artemisia, ruler of Halicarnassus, the city Herodotus came from. Before the battle, she alone had advised Xerxes not to fight at sea. Their men are as much stronger than your men by sea, she said, as men are stronger than women. Now an Athenian ship was chasing her, and allied ships blocked her way. So she rammed one: a ship of her own side, with the king of Calynda on board. It sank. Was it an old quarrel, or a plan, or bad luck? Herodotus says he cannot tell. The Athenian captain took her for a friend, and turned away. On the shore, the king's men told him she had sunk an enemy. Xerxes, it is said, replied: My men have become women, and my women men. Nobody from the sunken ship lived to tell him otherwise. The Athenians had offered 10,000 drachmas for her, alive.

Did you know? Herodotus says why the Athenians wanted Artemisia so badly: “there was a prize offered of ten thousand drachmas to whoever took her alive, since they were indignant that a woman waged war against Athens.” (Histories 8.93, tr. Godley, revised on Perseus)

Heads off

Not everyone came away so well. Some Phoenicians whose ships were lost came to the king and blamed the Ionian Greeks on his side for treason. While they spoke, an Ionian ship from Samothrace was sunk, and its crew boarded and took the Greek ship that had rammed them. Xerxes saw it. He ordered the Phoenicians' heads cut off, so that cowards would not slander braver men. In Aeschylus, he had warned his captains the night before: if the Greeks escaped, every captain would lose his head.

Who wrote Salamis down?

First, the play. Aeschylus' Persians won first prize in Athens in 472 B.C. A future leader of Athens, Pericles, paid for the chorus. Did Aeschylus fight at Salamis? A writer six centuries later says so, and that his own epitaph named only Marathon. He puts Xerxes on a high headland by the sea. He gives Xerxes a thousand ships, and 207 fast ones. Herodotus gives 1,207. For the Greeks: 310 in the play, 380 in Herodotus. Modern historians have proposed anything from about three hundred Persian ships to the full twelve hundred. We do not know. Herodotus collected stories, and he says whose. The Athenians told him the Corinthians fled at the start. The Corinthians denied it, and the rest of Greece, he writes, backed them.

The Troezen stone

Then the stone. A marble slab from Troezen, published in 1960, claims to give the decree Themistocles proposed. Women and children to Troezen. The old, and property, to Salamis. Two hundred ships, half of them to Artemisium. Even the exiles called back, to Salamis. If that is true, Athens planned to abandon the city before the first battles were fought. Herodotus has the order given only after them. But the stone was carved about two hundred years after Salamis. The scholar D. M. Lewis saw no reason to suspect a forgery. Others think the whole text was composed when it was carved. How much of the real decree is in it, we do not know.

After the battle

After the battle, Xerxes began a causeway to Salamis, as if to fight again. Herodotus says it was cover for going home. The Greek admirals met to choose, by ballot, the man who had done best. Each of them named himself first. Most named Themistocles second. Years later, Themistocles fled Greece as an outlaw. He wrote to Xerxes' son, the new king: I, Themistocles, am come to you, who did your house more harm than any of the Hellenes. He ended his life as a Persian governor. The king gave him one city for his bread, one for his wine, and one for the rest of his food.

Did you know? The vote at the Isthmus was held at the altar of Poseidon, and Herodotus sums it up: “So they each gained but one vote, while Themistocles far outstripped them in votes for the second place.” (Histories 8.123, tr. Godley, revised on Perseus)

Three records

So who wrote Salamis down? A king's scribes, to reward and punish. A poet, for an Athenian audience. A stone, two centuries on. And a historian who said why he wrote: "so that things done by man not be forgotten in time."

Quick answers

What happened at the Battle of Salamis?

Probably in late September 480 B.C., the Greek fleet lay off Salamis, beside Athens, after the Persians had taken the Acropolis and burned it. Many commanders wanted to fall back to the Isthmus. Themistocles sent his servant Sicinnus by boat to the Persians, to tell them the Greeks were terrified and planned flight; the Persians believed him and through the night closed the exits of the strait. At dawn the Greeks rowed out. Xerxes watched from the shore, and Herodotus says his men were far braver than they had been off Euboea, each one thinking that the king was watching him. Ships at the back pushed forward to show the king a brave deed and crashed into their own ships falling back. Few Greeks drowned, Herodotus says, because they could swim; many on the Persian side could not, and the king's own brother, Ariabignes, died.

Where did Xerxes watch the Battle of Salamis from?

Herodotus says Xerxes sat beneath the mountain opposite Salamis called Aegaleos, and whenever one of his ships did something brave, his scribes wrote down the captain's name, his father's name and his city. Aeschylus, in his play Persians, puts Xerxes on a high headland by the sea. The exact spot is not known.

How many ships fought at Salamis?

We do not know. Aeschylus' play gives Xerxes a thousand ships and two hundred and seven fast ones; Herodotus gives 1,207. For the Greeks, the play gives 310 and Herodotus 380. Modern historians have proposed anything from about three hundred Persian ships to the full twelve hundred.

Who was Artemisia at the Battle of Salamis?

Artemisia was ruler of Halicarnassus, the city Herodotus came from, and a captain in Xerxes' fleet. Before the battle, she alone had advised Xerxes not to fight at sea. In the battle, with an Athenian ship chasing her and allied ships blocking her way, she rammed a ship of her own side, with the king of Calynda on board, and it sank. Herodotus says he cannot tell whether it was an old quarrel, a plan or bad luck. The king's men told Xerxes she had sunk an enemy, and he is said to have replied: My men have become women, and my women men. The Athenians had offered ten thousand drachmas for her, alive.

Is the Troezen Decree genuine?

Nobody knows how much of it is. The marble slab from Troezen, published in 1960, claims to give the decree Themistocles proposed: women and children to Troezen, the old and property to Salamis, two hundred ships, half of them to Artemisium. But the stone was carved about two hundred years after Salamis. The scholar D. M. Lewis saw no reason to suspect a forgery; others think the whole text was composed when it was carved. How much of the real decree is in it, we do not know.

Where it happened

  • The strait of Salamis (480 B.C.): Where the fleets fought, probably late September 480 B.C. The exact lines of the fleets are disputed; today's coastline differs somewhat from the ancient shore.
  • Mount Aegaleos (480 B.C.): Herodotus says Xerxes sat beneath the mountain opposite Salamis called Aegaleos, and his scribes wrote down the names of captains who did well. Aeschylus puts him on a high headland by the sea. The exact spot is not known.
  • Psyttaleia (480 B.C.): The islet at the mouth of the strait where, Herodotus says, many of the Persians landed during the night before the battle.
  • The Acropolis, Athens (480 B.C.): Where Herodotus says the Athenians fed a sacred snake a honey cake every month. The Persians took the Acropolis and burned it.
  • Laurion (480 B.C.): The silver mines whose windfall, ten drachmas a man, Themistocles talked the Athenians into spending on two hundred ships.
  • Delphi (480 B.C.): The oracle that answered: divine Salamis, you will bring death to women's sons. The Greek victory monument stood here; its bronze column is now in Istanbul.
  • Troezen (480 B.C.): Where the stone with the decree was found. The decree sends the women and children to Troezen. The stone was carved about two hundred years after Salamis.
  • The Serpent Column, Hippodrome, Istanbul (479 B.C.): The bronze column of the Greek victory monument from Delphi. Herodotus says the Tenians were engraved on it for the ship that deserted and told the Greeks they were surrounded.
  • Halicarnassus (Bodrum) (480 B.C.): Artemisia's city, and the city Herodotus came from.
  • Tenos (Tinos) (480 B.C.): The island of the ship that deserted the Persian side before the battle.

Many on the Persian side could not. The king's own brother, Ariabignes, died. And the king rewarded. Two captains from Samos took Greek ships. Theomestor was made ruler of Samos. Phylacus was written down as a benefactor of the king, and given much land. One captain the king did see was a woman: Artemisia, ruler of Halicarnassus, the city Herodotus came from. Before the battle, she alone had advised Xerxes not to fight at sea. Their men are as much stronger than your men by sea, she said, as men are stronger than women. Now an Athenian ship was chasing her, and allied ships blocked her way. So she rammed one: a ship of her own side, with the king of Calynda on board. It sank. Was it an old quarrel, or a plan, or bad luck? Herodotus says he cannot tell. The Athenian captain took her for a friend, and turned away. On the shore, the king's men told him she had sunk an enemy. Xerxes, it is said, replied: My men have become women, and my women men. Nobody from the sunken ship lived to tell him otherwise. The Athenians had offered ten thousand drachmas for her, alive. Not everyone came away so well. Some Phoenicians whose ships were lost came to the king and blamed the Ionian Greeks on his side for treason. While they spoke, an Ionian ship from Samothrace was sunk, and its crew boarded and took the Greek ship that had rammed them. Xerxes saw it. He ordered the Phoenicians' heads cut off, so that cowards would not slander braver men. In Aeschylus, he had warned his captains the night before: if the Greeks escaped, every captain would lose his head. If you are enjoying this type of content, please don't forget to subscribe. First, the play. Aeschylus' Persians won first prize in Athens in 472 B.C. A future leader of Athens, Pericles, paid for the chorus. Did Aeschylus fight at Salamis? A writer six centuries later says so, and that his own epitaph named only Marathon. He puts Xerxes on a high headland by the sea. He gives Xerxes a thousand ships, and 207 fast ones. Herodotus gives 1,207. For the Greeks: 310 in the play, 380 in Herodotus. Modern historians have proposed anything from about three hundred Persian ships to the full twelve hundred. We do not know. Herodotus collected stories, and he says whose. The Athenians told him the Corinthians fled at the start. The Corinthians denied it, and the rest of Greece, he writes, backed them. Then the stone. A marble slab from Troezen, published in 1960, claims to give the decree Themistocles proposed. Women and children to Troezen. The old, and property, to Salamis. Two hundred ships, half of them to Artemisium. Even the exiles called back, to Salamis. If that is true, Athens planned to abandon the city before the first battles were fought. Herodotus has the order given only after them. But the stone was carved about two hundred years after Salamis. The scholar D. M. Lewis saw no reason to suspect a forgery. Others think the whole text was composed when it was carved. How much of the real decree is in it, we do not know. After the battle, Xerxes began a causeway to Salamis, as if to fight again. Herodotus says it was cover for going home. The Greek admirals met to choose, by ballot, the man who had done best. Each of them named himself first. Most named Themistocles second. Years later, Themistocles fled Greece as an outlaw. He wrote to Xerxes' son, the new king: I, Themistocles, am come to you, who did your house more harm than any of the Hellenes. He ended his life as a Persian governor. The king gave him one city for his bread, one for his wine, and one for the rest of his food. So who wrote Salamis down? A king's scribes, to reward and punish. A poet, for an Athenian audience. A stone, two centuries on. And a historian who said why he wrote: "so that things done by man not be forgotten in time." Artemisia's ram: a desperate accident, or the coolest move of the whole battle? And the Troezen stone: a copy of the decree Athens really voted, or a story carved two hundred years later?
