Dig beside one of Easter Island's famous heads, and you find a body, standing upright in the ground. From 2010, the Easter Island Statue Project dug two of them down to their bases, at the quarry of Rano Raraku. One was about 7 metres tall, the other over 8, both buried by soil that had washed down the slope over the centuries. The project's director, Jo Anne Van Tilburg, put it in one line: Easter Island statues have bodies! And who carved them is no mystery. The people of the island did. The mystery is how. They had stone tools, and no farm animal bigger than a chicken. Yet they moved statues of many tonnes across the island. And then, one day, they stopped. The islanders had their own answer to the first question. The statues walked.

Did you know? On present evidence the first settlers brought only two animals: the Pacific rat and the chicken (Terry Hunt, 2007). Statues of many tonnes were moved with no farm animal bigger than a chicken.

Rapa Nui. Easter Island. 3,700 kilometres from South America, and more than 1,900 from the nearest inhabited island. Polynesian voyagers had reached it by about A.D. 1250, on the latest estimates. How did the statues move, and why did the carving stop? Three ship journals, one experiment and a quarrel will take us there.

A thousand statues from one volcano

First, what we are looking at. There are about a thousand statues, called moai. Ninety-five in every hundred were cut from one volcano, Rano Raraku. About half of them never left its slopes. An average moai is about 4 metres tall, and weighs about 12.5 tonnes. The biggest ever set up, Paro, is almost 10 metres tall and about 74 tonnes. It was moved about 6 kilometres from the quarry. And in the quarry lies a giant over 20 metres long, never finished. Katherine Routledge, who worked on the island in 1914 and 1915, wrote that it is difficult to believe it was ever intended to move such a mass.

Did you know? About half of Easter Island's roughly one thousand statues never left the slopes of Rano Raraku, the volcano where ninety-five in every hundred were cut (Easter Island Statue Project).

What the first Europeans saw

The first Europeans to see the statues could not explain them either. On Easter Day, 1722, three Dutch ships sighted the island, and named it for the day. Their commander, Jacob Roggeveen, wrote: these stone figures caused us to be filled with wonder. How, he asked, could people destitute of heavy or thick timber, and also of stout cordage, have erected them? Then a piece was taken off one, and he solved it, wrongly: they were made of clay, he decided, with small smooth flints stuck on. They are solid volcanic stone. The landing turned to blood. Some of his men opened fire without orders, and left ten or twelve islanders dead. In 1774, James Cook came. He wrote: We could hardly conceive how these islanders, wholly unacquainted with any mechanical power, could raise such stupendous figures. And he decided that the islanders of his day had most certainly had no hand in them. Twelve years later, the French captain La Pérouse disagreed. With long levers and a stone beneath them, he wrote, a hundred men would be sufficient to raise them. Thus the wonder disappears.

Did you know? After a piece was taken off one statue, Jacob Roggeveen decided in 1722 that they were made of clay, "or some kind of rich earth", with small smooth flints stuck on (his log, tr. Corney 1908, p. 16). They are solid volcanic stone.

Who carved them

But the doubt that the islanders could have done it never quite went away. You can still hear that someone else made the statues: a lost people from far away, or even visitors from space. No evidence supports it. In 2024, the genomes of fifteen earlier islanders, from museum collections, were published in Nature. They are Polynesian, and most closely related to the Rapa Nui of today. And the carvers left their tools behind. Routledge's team found some forty stone chisels while digging out a single statue.

Did you know? Katherine Routledge's team found some forty stone chisels while digging out a single statue (Routledge 1919, p. 185).

They walked: the oral tradition

So how did they move them? The Rapa Nui had their own account, and Routledge wrote it down. There was an old woman, the cook of the image makers, who moved the statues by mana, by supernatural power. One day, while she was away, the workers ate a fine lobster, and left her none. They forgot to hide the remains. She arose in her wrath, told all the statues to fall down, and the work came to a standstill. Another account, quoted by a visitor before her, says it in one line: They walked, and some fell by the way. Routledge herself presumed that rollers were used. But she added that the old carvers, could they see us, would ask: But how could you do it any other way?

The walking experiments

For decades, most archaeologists pictured the statues lying down, dragged on wooden sledges or rollers. In 1986, a Czech engineer, Pavel Pavel, working with Thor Heyerdahl, tried it the islanders' way. His team shuffled an upright statue forward with ropes. It moved, but slowly, and its base wore away. In 2012, the archaeologists Carl Lipo and Terry Hunt tried again, with a different shape. Statues left by the old roads are not like the ones on the platforms. Their bases are wide and D shaped, and they lean forward. So the team copied one of them, scaled down to 4.35 tonnes. At first it would not move. They dropped it several times. Then eighteen people took three ropes, one on each side and one behind, and rocked it from side to side. With every rock, the leaning statue swung forward, like a fridge walked across a kitchen. It covered 100 metres in 40 minutes.

Did you know? The 2012 copy would not move at first: the team dropped it several times before eighteen people with three ropes rocked it forward (Lipo and Hunt 2025).

Roads, fallen statues and the critics

The roads seem to fit. They run out from the quarry, about 4.5 metres wide, dug in a shallow curve that, Lipo and Hunt argue, keeps a rocking statue in line. Lipo and Hunt studied 62 statues lying along them. Where the road runs downhill, most of the fallen lie on their faces. Where it climbs, more lie on their backs. As if they toppled mid stride. In 2025, in the Journal of Archaeological Science, they wrote: The moai walked. Not everyone agrees. Van Tilburg, whose team dug up the bodies, told a reporter in 2012: What they did was a stunt and not an experiment. Would upright methods work on the island's rough ground? Not very likely, she wrote. The archaeologist Colin Richards and his colleagues read the road statues another way: set up on purpose along paths to a sacred quarry, not dropped on the way. And a four tonne copy is not Paro. Lipo and Hunt calculate that even the largest could be kept moving by a modest crew. That is a calculation, not a test.

Why did the carving stop?

So, the first mystery: walking fits best, and it is still argued. Now the second. Why did the carving stop? The famous answer is ecocide. In 2005, Jared Diamond called Easter Island the clearest example of a society that destroyed itself by overexploiting its own resources. The palm forest did vanish. But Terry Hunt also blamed the Pacific rat, which came with the settlers and gnawed the palm nuts. On his numbers, the rats could have passed three million. And the people did not collapse before the Europeans came, on the newest evidence. In 2020, a team dating the stone platforms found that building went on steadily until after the Dutch arrived in 1722. The ancient genomes agree. They show no sign of a crash in the 1600s. The catastrophe came from outside. In the 1860s, Peruvian slave raiders kidnapped about a third of the people. Then came smallpox. The islanders fell to an estimated 110.

Did you know? On Terry Hunt's numbers, the island's rats could have passed three million (Hunt 2007: "could have exceeded 3.1 million", an estimate).

The stolen friend

Many statues were down long before. In 1774, Cook's men had already found fallen and broken ones. Islanders told Routledge some were thrown down by people. When the last one was carved, and why the carving ended, nobody knows. One left the island. In 1868, the crew of the British ship Topaze took a statue from Orongo, a ceremonial centre. It is basalt, one of only fourteen moai made of that stone, and almost 2.5 metres tall. Its name, Hoa Hakananai'a, is translated by the British Museum as lost, hidden or stolen friend. Its back is carved with birds, the signs of the birdman religion, which, the museum says, followed the age of the statues.

Did you know? The British Museum translates the name Hoa Hakananai'a as "lost, hidden or stolen friend".

What we really know

So what do we really have? Statues with bodies. Curved roads. A tradition that says they walked, and a copy that did. What would settle it? Digs beneath the road statues, to tell a breakdown from a monument. A full size test of the road shape. And firm dates for the last carving.

Verdict

My verdict: walking fits more of the evidence than any other idea on the table. But it is not proven for the giants, and nobody yet knows why the carving stopped.

Over to you

Would you walk a seventy-four tonne statue upright on ropes, or lay it down on a sledge? The old woman and the lobster: just a story, or a memory of the day the work stopped?

Quick answers

Do the Easter Island heads have bodies?

Yes. The famous heads on the slope of Rano Raraku are whole statues, buried by soil that had washed down the slope over the centuries. Where it happened

  • Ahu Te Pito Kura (Paro) (A.D. 1722): Where Paro lies, the biggest statue ever set up, almost 10 m and about 74 tonnes, moved about 6 km from the quarry (NOVA after Van Tilburg).   • British Museum, London (A.D. 1869): Where Hoa Hakananai'a, one of only fourteen basalt moai, has been since 1869 (British Museum Oc1869,1005.1).

    Transcript

    Read the full narration

    Easter Island. The heads have bodies. Dig beside one of Easter Island's famous heads, and you find a body, standing upright in the ground. From 2010, the Easter Island Statue Project dug two of them down to their bases, at the quarry of Rano Raraku. One was about 7 metres tall, the other over 8, both buried by soil that had washed down the slope over the centuries. The project's director, Jo Anne Van Tilburg, put it in one line: Easter Island statues have bodies! And who carved them is no mystery. The people of the island did. The mystery is how. They had stone tools, and no farm animal bigger than a chicken. Yet they moved statues of many tonnes across the island. And then, one day, they stopped. The islanders had their own answer to the first question. The statues walked. Rapa Nui. Easter Island. 3,700 kilometres from South America, and more than 1,900 from the nearest inhabited island. Polynesian voyagers had reached it by about A.D. 1250, on the latest estimates. How did the statues move, and why did the carving stop? Three ship journals, one experiment and a quarrel will take us there. First, what we are looking at. There are about a thousand statues, called moai. Ninety-five in every hundred were cut from one volcano, Rano Raraku. About half of them never left its slopes. An average moai is about 4 metres tall, and weighs about 12.5 tonnes. The biggest ever set up, Paro, is almost 10 metres tall and about 74 tonnes. It was moved about 6 kilometres from the quarry. And in the quarry lies a giant over 20 metres long, never finished. Katherine Routledge, who worked on the island in 1914 and 1915, wrote that it is difficult to believe it was ever intended to move such a mass. The first Europeans to see the statues could not explain them either. On Easter Day, 1722, three Dutch ships sighted the island, and named it for the day. Their commander, Jacob Roggeveen, wrote: these stone figures caused us to be filled with wonder. How, he asked, could people destitute of heavy or thick timber, and also of stout cordage, have erected them? Then a piece was taken off one, and he solved it, wrongly: they were made of clay, he decided, with small smooth flints stuck on. They are solid volcanic stone. The landing turned to blood. Some of his men opened fire without orders, and left ten or twelve islanders dead. In 1774, James Cook came. He wrote: We could hardly conceive how these islanders, wholly unacquainted with any mechanical power, could raise such stupendous figures. And he decided that the islanders of his day had most certainly had no hand in them. Twelve years later, the French captain La Pérouse disagreed. With long levers and a stone beneath them, he wrote, a hundred men would be sufficient to raise them. Thus the wonder disappears. But the doubt that the islanders could have done it never quite went away. You can still hear that someone else made the statues: a lost people from far away, or even visitors from space. No evidence supports it. In 2024, the genomes of fifteen earlier islanders, from museum collections, were published in Nature. They are Polynesian, and most closely related to the Rapa Nui of today. And the carvers left their tools behind. Routledge's team found some forty stone chisels while digging out a single statue. So how did they move them? The Rapa Nui had their own account, and Routledge wrote it down. There was an old woman, the cook of the image makers, who moved the statues by mana, by supernatural power. One day, while she was away, the workers ate a fine lobster, and left her none. They forgot to hide the remains. She arose in her wrath, told all the statues to fall down, and the work came to a standstill. Another account, quoted by a visitor before her, says it in one line: They walked, and some fell by the way. Routledge herself presumed that rollers were used. But she added that the old carvers, could they see us, would ask: But how could you do it any other way? For decades, most archaeologists pictured the statues lying down, dragged on wooden sledges or rollers. In 1986, a Czech engineer, Pavel Pavel, working with Thor Heyerdahl, tried it the islanders' way. His team shuffled an upright statue forward with ropes. It moved, but slowly, and its base wore away. In 2012, the archaeologists Carl Lipo and Terry Hunt tried again, with a different shape. Statues left by the old roads are not like the ones on the platforms. Their bases are wide and D shaped, and they lean forward. So the team copied one of them, scaled down to 4.35 tonnes. At first it would not move. They dropped it several times. Then eighteen people took three ropes, one on each side and one behind, and rocked it from side to side. With every rock, the leaning statue swung forward, like a fridge walked across a kitchen. It covered 100 metres in 40 minutes. If you are enjoying this type of content, please don't forget to subscribe. The roads seem to fit. They run out from the quarry, about 4.5 metres wide, dug in a shallow curve that, Lipo and Hunt argue, keeps a rocking statue in line. Lipo and Hunt studied 62 statues lying along them. Where the road runs downhill, most of the fallen lie on their faces. Where it climbs, more lie on their backs. As if they toppled mid stride. In 2025, in the Journal of Archaeological Science, they wrote: The moai walked. Not everyone agrees. Van Tilburg, whose team dug up the bodies, told a reporter in 2012: What they did was a stunt and not an experiment. Would upright methods work on the island's rough ground? Not very likely, she wrote. The archaeologist Colin Richards and his colleagues read the road statues another way: set up on purpose along paths to a sacred quarry, not dropped on the way. And a four tonne copy is not Paro. Lipo and Hunt calculate that even the largest could be kept moving by a modest crew. That is a calculation, not a test. So, the first mystery: walking fits best, and it is still argued. Now the second. Why did the carving stop? The famous answer is ecocide. In 2005, Jared Diamond called Easter Island the clearest example of a society that destroyed itself by overexploiting its own resources. The palm forest did vanish. But Terry Hunt also blamed the Pacific rat, which came with the settlers and gnawed the palm nuts. On his numbers, the rats could have passed three million. And the people did not collapse before the Europeans came, on the newest evidence. In 2020, a team dating the stone platforms found that building went on steadily until after the Dutch arrived in 1722. The ancient genomes agree. They show no sign of a crash in the 1600s. The catastrophe came from outside. In the 1860s, Peruvian slave raiders kidnapped about a third of the people. Then came smallpox. The islanders fell to an estimated 110. Many statues were down long before. In 1774, Cook's men had already found fallen and broken ones. Islanders told Routledge some were thrown down by people. When the last one was carved, and why the carving ended, nobody knows. One left the island. In 1868, the crew of the British ship Topaze took a statue from Orongo, a ceremonial centre. It is basalt, one of only fourteen moai made of that stone, and almost two and a half metres tall. Its name, Hoa Hakananai'a, is translated by the British Museum as lost, hidden or stolen friend. Its back is carved with birds, the signs of the birdman religion, which, the museum says, followed the age of the statues. So what do we really have? Statues with bodies. Curved roads. A tradition that says they walked, and a copy that did. What would settle it? Digs beneath the road statues, to tell a breakdown from a monument. A full size test of the road shape. And firm dates for the last carving. My verdict: walking fits more of the evidence than any other idea on the table. But it is not proven for the giants, and nobody yet knows why the carving stopped. Would you walk a seventy-four tonne statue upright on ropes, or lay it down on a sledge? And the old woman and the lobster: just a story, or a memory of the day the work stopped?
