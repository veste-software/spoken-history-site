Cannae, 216 BC: The Gold Rings Hannibal Sent to Carthage

Hannibal's brother had a heap of gold rings poured out in the entrance of the senate house at Carthage. Proof of a victory in Italy, he said. Only Roman knights wore such rings, and only the first men among them. How big was the heap? Some writers, says the Roman historian Livy, put it at three and a half modii, a Roman grain measure. The more likely story, Livy adds, is that it was no more than one. Even the proof of the battle comes with two numbers.

Cannae is famous as the perfect trap, drawn crisp and certain in countless diagrams. But what do the writers who tell it really say? How sure can we be? And why did Rome not give up? Apulia, in southern Italy, 216 B.C.

Two writers, neither there

Our two main witnesses never saw the battle. Polybius was a Greek held in Rome, who lived in the house of the Aemilius Paullus family and taught its sons. With one of them, Scipio, he became so close that, in his own words, the report about them spread to Italy and Greece. Remember that family. Livy wrote close to two centuries later, from older writers, and often says when they disagree.

Two consuls, one army

That summer, Polybius says, the senate decided to field eight legions, a thing which had never been done before by the Romans. His figures give about 80,000 foot and over 6,000 horse. Hannibal had about 40,000 foot and 10,000 horse. The two consuls commanded on alternate days. Lucius Aemilius Paullus, the grandfather of Polybius' friend, wanted to wait. Gaius Terentius Varro, a newcomer backed by the people, wanted to fight. Livy says he had promised to finish the war the very day he caught sight of the enemy. But that comes from writers who knew how the day would end.

Did you know? Polybius says the senate decided to bring eight legions into the field that year, "a thing which had never been done before by the Romans" (Polybius, Histories 3.107.9, tr. W. R. Paton).

Plutarch, three centuries later, tells a story from that morning. Hannibal rode up a ridge to watch the Romans form up. One of his companions, Gisco, said their numbers amazed him. Hannibal answered that something more amazing had escaped him: in all this multitude there is no one who is called Gisco. The joke ran down the ridge, and great numbers laughed.

The trap, as Polybius tells it

Now the battle as Polybius describes it. Our animation follows his words, and it is one reading of them. On Varro's day, at sunrise, the Romans crossed the river and formed up facing south, the ranks packed many times deeper than they were wide. Hannibal crossed too. Celts and Spaniards in the centre, African foot on either side, horsemen on both wings. The Gauls stood naked, Polybius says. The Spaniards wore short tunics bordered with purple. Then Hannibal pushed his centre forward into a crescent, bulging toward the Romans. By the river, the horsemen did not wheel about. Having once met, they dismounted and fought man to man. In the centre the legions drove the crescent back, and kept pushing, until the Africans stood on both their flanks. The Africans turned inward. And Hannibal's cavalry, having broken the Roman horse, rode round and struck the legions from behind. The Romans, Polybius writes, were gradually huddled in and surrounded.

Did you know? By the river, Polybius says, there were none of the normal wheeling evolutions of horsemen: "having once met they dismounted and fought man to man" (Polybius, Histories 3.115.2 to 3, tr. W. R. Paton).

What only Livy tells

Livy adds things Polybius does not mention. About five hundred Numidian horsemen rode over as if to desert, with swords hidden under their armour. Sent to the rear, they waited. Then they picked up shields from the dead and attacked the Roman line from behind. We cannot tell where Livy found these stories.

How did Paullus die?

And the consul Paullus? Polybius says he rode to the centre and fell in the thick of the fight, after receiving several dreadful wounds. And he adds: if there ever was a man who did his duty by his country, both all through his life and in these last times, it was he. Livy has another scene. Paullus, hit hard by a sling shot at the start, is found sitting on a stone, covered in blood. An officer, Lentulus, offers him his horse. Paullus refuses, and sends him to tell the senate to fortify Rome. Then fleeing men and the enemy sweep over him. Which is true? We do not know. The historian Darío Sánchez Vendramini argues that Polybius evidently magnified Paullus, and that the ancient writers lacked direct and precise information about his conduct in the battle. Others, such as Arthur Eckstein, accept Polybius' account in general. And Varro? He escaped with a few dozen horsemen. Polybius says he disgraced himself by his flight.

Did you know? In Livy's version, the consul Paullus was hit hard by a sling shot at the very start of the battle (Livy, History of Rome 22.49.1).

How many died?

How many died? Here they part completely. Polybius: about 70,000 Romans dead, and about 10,000 captured. Livy: 45,500 foot and 2,700 horse killed. The historian Adrian Goldsworthy writes that either Polybius' figures for the size of the Roman army or for the casualties they suffered are wrong, since they contradict each other. He thinks the death toll is the mistake. The reader's letter he was replying to defends the 70,000. Of another dispute about Cannae, Livy wrote: It is easier to feel astonishment at such discrepancies amongst our authorities than to determine what is the truth.

Where was it?

Where did they die? Even that is open. The village of Cannae stood on a hill by the river Aufidus, today the Ofanto. The geologist William Mahaney, who walked the site in 2004, notes that there are several versions of where the battlefield lay. And the river itself, he writes, has moved about half a kilometre since. So the perfect diagram has no fixed ground under it.

Did you know? The river of Cannae, the Aufidus, today the Ofanto, has moved about half a kilometre since the battle, the geologist William Mahaney writes (Studia Quaternaria, 2024).

The next morning

The next morning, Livy says, some men were found with their heads buried in the earth. They had, it seemed, dug holes and smothered themselves. And a Numidian was pulled out alive from under a dead Roman. His ears and nose were torn. The Roman, Livy says, could no longer hold a weapon, and had used his teeth. Livy wrote this close to two centuries later. We cannot check it.

The rings at Carthage

Hannibal's cavalry commander, Maharbal, wanted to ride on Rome at once. When Hannibal held back, Maharbal said, in Livy's Latin: You know how to win, Hannibal. You do not know how to use victory. Hannibal's brother Mago went home to Carthage. Then the rings were poured out. Livy's sources say three and a half modii, or one. Pliny the Elder says three. The historian Florus, two. And Pliny read the heap the other way: to him it proved that gold rings were by then in common use. Then a senator, Hanno, an old enemy of Hannibal's family, asked Mago some questions. Had a single Latin city gone over to them? Had a single man of the thirty-five Roman tribes deserted? No, said Mago. Had anyone in Rome even mentioned the word peace? Again, no. The speech is Livy's, written long after. But the question was the right one.

Did you know? Pliny the Elder read the heap of rings the other way: to him it proved that gold rings were by then in common use in Rome (Pliny, Natural History 33.20; our rendering).

Why Rome did not break

At Canusium, about 10,000 survivors gathered. A rich woman of the town, Busa, gave them grain, clothes and provisions for the road. After the war the senate honoured her. There, says Livy, young nobles talked of leaving Italy. A young officer, Publius Scipio, went to their house, held his drawn sword over them, and made them swear not to abandon the republic. Livy calls him the general destined to end this war. He married the dead consul's daughter. In Rome, the senate refused to ransom the prisoners Hannibal held. Polybius says Hannibal's joy at his victory in the battle was not so great as his dejection, when he saw with amazement how steadfast and high-spirited were the Romans. When Varro came home, Livy says, crowds met him, and thanks were voted to him because he had not despaired of the republic. And no one in Rome, Livy says, spoke of peace.

Did you know? Busa, a rich woman of Canusium, gave the survivors grain, clothes and provisions for the road, and after the war the senate honoured her (Livy, History of Rome 22.52.7).

Verdict

So, how much do we really know about Cannae? Rome lost a huge army in one day. Paullus died, Varro got away, and Rome would not give in. On that, the sources we read agree. The perfect trap, the exact numbers, the hero's death: those are pictures drawn from the words of writers who disagree. Even the heap of gold comes in four sizes. Whom do you believe on the Roman dead: Polybius, with about 70,000, or Livy, with about 48,000?

Quick answers

What happened at the Battle of Cannae?

In Apulia, in southern Italy, in 216 B.C., Hannibal surrounded a Roman army. As Polybius describes it, the Romans crossed the river at sunrise and formed up facing south, the ranks packed many times deeper than they were wide. Hannibal put Celts and Spaniards in the centre, African foot on either side and horsemen on both wings, and pushed his centre forward into a crescent. The legions drove the crescent back until the Africans stood on both their flanks. The Africans turned inward, and Hannibal's cavalry, having broken the Roman horse, struck the legions from behind. The Romans, Polybius writes, were gradually huddled in and surrounded. Our animation follows his words, and it is one reading of them.

How many Romans died at Cannae?

The sources part completely. Polybius gives about 70,000 Romans dead and about 10,000 captured. Livy gives 45,500 foot and 2,700 horse killed, about 48,000. The historian Adrian Goldsworthy writes that either Polybius' figures for the size of the Roman army or for the casualties are wrong, since they contradict each other; he thinks the death toll is the mistake. The reader's letter he was replying to defends the 70,000. Polybius' own figures give the Romans about 80,000 foot and over 6,000 horse, and Hannibal about 40,000 foot and 10,000 horse.

What were the gold rings Hannibal sent to Carthage?

After the battle, Hannibal's brother Mago had a heap of gold rings poured out in the entrance of the senate house at Carthage, as proof of a victory in Italy. Only Roman knights wore such rings, he said, and only the first men among them. Some writers, says Livy, put the heap at three and a half modii, a Roman grain measure; the more likely story, he adds, is that it was no more than one. Pliny the Elder says three, the historian Florus two. And Pliny read the heap the other way: to him it proved that gold rings were by then in common use.

How did the consul Aemilius Paullus die at Cannae?

We do not know. Polybius says he rode to the centre and fell in the thick of the fight, after receiving several dreadful wounds. Livy has another scene: Paullus, hit hard by a sling shot at the start, is found sitting on a stone, covered in blood, refuses an officer's horse and sends him to tell the senate to fortify Rome, before fleeing men and the enemy sweep over him. The historian Darío Sánchez Vendramini argues that Polybius, who lived in the house of Paullus' family, evidently magnified Paullus, and that the ancient writers lacked direct and precise information about his conduct in the battle; others, such as Arthur Eckstein, accept Polybius' account in general.

Where was the Battle of Cannae fought?

Near the village of Cannae, which stood on a hill by the river Aufidus, today the Ofanto, in Apulia in southern Italy. Exactly where the armies met is open: the geologist William Mahaney, who walked the site in 2004, notes that there are several versions of where the battlefield lay, and that the river itself has moved about half a kilometre since. So the famous diagram has no fixed ground under it.

Where it happened

  • Cannae, the hill of Canne by the Ofanto (the ancient Aufidus) (216 B.C.): The village of Cannae stood on a hill by the river Aufidus, today the Ofanto. The pin marks the hill, the traditional site; where the battlefield itself lay is debated (there are several versions, Mahaney 2024), and the river has moved about half a kilometre since.
  • Canusium (Canosa di Puglia) (216 B.C.): About ten thousand survivors gathered here, Livy says; Busa fed and clothed them, and the young Publius Scipio made the nobles swear not to abandon the republic.
  • Carthage (216 B.C.): Hannibal's brother Mago had the gold rings poured out in the entrance of the senate house here, Livy says; the senator Hanno asked him his questions.
  • Rome (216 B.C.): The senate refused to ransom the prisoners Hannibal held, and thanked Varro because he had not despaired of the republic, Livy says.

The historian Darío Sánchez Vendramini argues that Polybius evidently magnified Paullus, and that the ancient writers lacked direct and precise information about his conduct in the battle. Others, such as Arthur Eckstein, accept Polybius' account in general. And Varro? He escaped with a few dozen horsemen. Polybius says he disgraced himself by his flight. How many died? Here they part completely. Polybius: about 70,000 Romans dead, and about 10,000 captured. Livy: 45,500 foot and 2,700 horse killed. The historian Adrian Goldsworthy writes that either Polybius' figures for the size of the Roman army or for the casualties they suffered are wrong, since they contradict each other. He thinks the death toll is the mistake. The reader's letter he was replying to defends the 70,000. Of another dispute about Cannae, Livy wrote: It is easier to feel astonishment at such discrepancies amongst our authorities than to determine what is the truth. Where did they die? Even that is open. The village of Cannae stood on a hill by the river Aufidus, today the Ofanto. The geologist William Mahaney, who walked the site in 2004, notes that there are several versions of where the battlefield lay. And the river itself, he writes, has moved about half a kilometre since. So the perfect diagram has no fixed ground under it. The next morning, Livy says, some men were found with their heads buried in the earth. They had, it seemed, dug holes and smothered themselves. And a Numidian was pulled out alive from under a dead Roman. His ears and nose were torn. The Roman, Livy says, could no longer hold a weapon, and had used his teeth. Livy wrote this close to two centuries later. We cannot check it. Hannibal's cavalry commander, Maharbal, wanted to ride on Rome at once. When Hannibal held back, Maharbal said, in Livy's Latin: You know how to win, Hannibal. You do not know how to use victory. Hannibal's brother Mago went home to Carthage. Then the rings were poured out. Livy's sources say three and a half modii, or one. Pliny the Elder says three. The historian Florus, two. And Pliny read the heap the other way: to him it proved that gold rings were by then in common use. Then a senator, Hanno, an old enemy of Hannibal's family, asked Mago some questions. Had a single Latin city gone over to them? Had a single man of the thirty-five Roman tribes deserted? No, said Mago. Had anyone in Rome even mentioned the word peace? Again, no. The speech is Livy's, written long after. But the question was the right one. At Canusium, about 10,000 survivors gathered. A rich woman of the town, Busa, gave them grain, clothes and provisions for the road. After the war the senate honoured her. There, says Livy, young nobles talked of leaving Italy. A young officer, Publius Scipio, went to their house, held his drawn sword over them, and made them swear not to abandon the republic. Livy calls him the general destined to end this war. He married the dead consul's daughter. In Rome, the senate refused to ransom the prisoners Hannibal held. Polybius says Hannibal's joy at his victory in the battle was not so great as his dejection, when he saw with amazement how steadfast and high-spirited were the Romans. When Varro came home, Livy says, crowds met him, and thanks were voted to him because he had not despaired of the republic. And no one in Rome, Livy says, spoke of peace. So, how much do we really know about Cannae? Rome lost a huge army in one day. Paullus died, Varro got away, and Rome would not give in. On that, the sources we read agree. The perfect trap, the exact numbers, the hero's death: those are pictures drawn from the words of writers who disagree. Even the heap of gold comes in four sizes. Whom do you believe on the Roman dead: Polybius, with about 70,000, or Livy, with about 48,000? And was Rome's refusal to ransom its own soldiers strength, or cruelty?
