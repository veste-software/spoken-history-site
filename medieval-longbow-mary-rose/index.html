The Solent, off Portsmouth. 19 July 1545. King Henry VIII is at dinner on board his flagship when a French fleet appears. He leaves in a hurry. Towards evening, one of his great ships, the Mary Rose, heels over and goes down. The emperor's ambassador wrote a few days later. Of the 500 men on board, all were drowned except about 25 or 30. A Fleming who survived told him the water came in through the lowest row of gun ports, left open after firing. The Mary Rose Trust says the king watched her sink from Southsea Castle. On her lowest deck, in the stern, lay chests of longbows. Some had already been carried up, ready for action. The seabed kept them for more than four hundred years.

How many were on board? The sources disagree. The ambassador wrote of 500 men, and a list of 1545 also gives 500; the Anthony Roll of 1546 gives 415, and later accounts 600 and 700. From the remains, the Mary Rose Trust counts at least 179 people and probably about 300, and it calls the contemporary figure of 500 sensible. Survivors: about 25 or 30 in the ambassador's letter; the Mary Rose Trust gives about 30.

The only precisely dated longbows

Today: what a real English war bow was, what came up from the wreck, and what the texts really say. Because, says the Mary Rose Trust, her bows are the only precisely dated English longbows we have. 172 of them came up. A research project at the University of Southampton calls them the largest collection that has been discovered. One warning first. 1545 is Tudor England, after the Middle Ages by the usual reckoning. Whether these bows are the bows of Agincourt, a hundred and thirty years earlier, is a question we will come back to.

What is a longbow?

So, what is a longbow? Every bow from the ship is made from a single piece of yew. The bowyer kept two kinds of wood in that one piece. On the back, the sapwood from the outside of the tree, which copes best with being stretched. On the belly, facing the archer, the heartwood, which copes best with being squeezed. The Mary Rose Trust calls the result a perfect, natural spring. And they are long. From 1.84 to 2.11 metres.

What came up from the wreck

The divers raised four chests for longbows, and seven for arrows. A list of the ship's stores gives 250 bows. 172 have been found. It lists 9,600 arrows. More than 2,300 complete arrows came up, and thousands of broken pieces. Most are poplar. The iron heads have rusted away, and the feathers of goose or swan are gone. Some arrows were still held in a leather disc with holes for 24, part of a bag worn on the belt. 864 bowstrings were listed on board. Only one, perhaps two, have been found, and only in fragments. These were not heirlooms. The Trust says the bows were mass produced, for the king's ships and his forts.

Did you know? The ship's papers list 864 bowstrings. Only a fragment of one, possibly two, has ever been recovered, identified as hemp or linen (Mary Rose Trust, "Longbow chests" and "Archery and longbows on the Mary Rose").

The first bow, 1979

The first bow came up in 1979. A slightly bent, almost black shaft of wood, 6 foot 5 long. The excavators called in the actor Robert Hardy, who had written a history of the longbow. Was it a longbow, or a pike staff? His answer, as he told it later: Good heavens, it is a longbow. And he nearly fainted. For the first drying, the bows were kept in his own cellar.

Did you know? The first Mary Rose longbow, a black shaft 6 feet 5 inches long, was identified in 1979 by the actor Robert Hardy, who had written a history of the longbow. In his own words: "Good heavens it is a longbow", and he nearly fainted. For their first drying, the bows were kept in his own cellar (J. Readwin, "Actor and Archer: The double life of Robert Hardy", Mary Rose Trust blog, 3 August 2025).

How hard to draw? The estimates

So how hard were they to draw? Here the arguments begin. You cannot simply measure the originals. Hardy's team was allowed to bend a few on a testing machine, until they broke. The wood had decayed. The bows had lost around half their strength. So a researcher at Groningen, Dr Kooi, built a mathematical model of a bow. Tested on a replica, it came within one percent. Applied to the originals, it gave about 100 to 185 pounds. Hardy's team thought most were 150 to 160. Not everyone agrees. The Mary Rose Trust's own collection page gives 65 to 175 pounds, with a peak at 110. 110 pounds is like holding 50 kilos at full draw. 160 is more than 70. Either way, these are estimates, made from damaged wood.

The men who drew them

And the men who drew them? The ship also gave up the bones of her crew. From about 9,000 bones, the bioarchaeologist Ann Stirland pieced together 92 fairly complete skeletons. At least 179 people. One man lay on the main deck, a broken longbow beside him, a leather wristguard over his lower arm. The wristguard carries the badges of Henry VIII and Katherine of Aragon. The Trust thinks he may have been a royal archer. He was in his early twenties, 1.79 metres tall, taller than most. Even the shortest bow was longer than he was. The middle of his spine was twisted, and his right finger bones have grooves that the Trust says may come from drawing a longbow again and again. And his teeth suggest he grew up somewhere hotter than England. A study of 2021 names North Africa or Iberia, but says it cannot pinpoint where.

Did you know? The man the Trust calls the royal archer may have grown up far from England. His teeth suggest a climate hotter than England's; the 2021 isotope study names northeastern Morocco, parts of Algeria and southern Tunisia, or Iberia as possibilities, and adds that pinpointing origins is not possible (Scorrer and others, Royal Society Open Science 8, 2021: 202106).

Do the bones prove archery?

Stirland noticed something across the crew. In more than 14 in every 100, the tip of the shoulder blade had never fused to the rest. Mostly on the left, the bow arm. She linked it to long, hard use of the heavy bows. But the same condition turns up in people who never drew a war bow. In a study of nearly 1,200 skeletons from an American collection, 8 in every 100 had it. Stirland herself, from the shoulder bones, called many archers on board a reasonable inference. An inference, not a proof.

The law behind the bow

Behind those men stood the law. In 1363, Edward III ordered every able-bodied man to practise with bow and arrows on feast days, and banned football, handball and cock fighting. The art, says the order, is almost wholly disused, and the realm is like to be kept without archers. A few years before the Mary Rose sank, a new act of Henry VIII went further. Almost every man under sixty had to keep a bow and arrows in his house, and practise with long bows. Every household with boys from seven to seventeen had to give each a bow and two arrows. It even set prices. A boy's yew bow, no more than a shilling. Yew was dear. The act orders bowyers outside London to make four bows of other wood for every one of yew. It also rules on yew staves brought into the realm from abroad. Where the Mary Rose's own yew grew, we found no published study to tell us.

Did you know? Under Henry VIII's act of 1541/2, every household with boys from seven to seventeen had to give each of them a bow and two arrows, and a bowyer could charge no more than twelve pence, one shilling, for a yew bow for a boy of eight to fourteen (Statutes of the Realm, vol. III, 33 Henry VIII c. 9).

And the medieval longbow?

So what can a Tudor ship tell us about the medieval longbow? Very few medieval longbows survive. Robert Kaiser, in a thesis on the longbow, put it simply: because it was so common, few longbows survive. And the written sources do not describe one standard bow. Gerald of Wales travelled through Wales in 1188, and praised the archers of Gwent. At Abergavenny, he says, their arrows went through an oak door almost a palm thick. The arrows were left in the door, in memory. But their bows, he says, were not of yew. They were of wild elm, rough and unpolished, but stiff and strong. And his word is simply: bow. So how close were the bows of Agincourt to these? We do not know. The Mary Rose gives us one moment, 1545, that we can measure. And in that moment, the bows went down beside the ship's guns, which she had just fired.

What we really have

So, what do we really have? 172 yew bows, all lost on a single day. Thousands of arrows. Draw weights that are estimates, from damaged wood. And bones that may, or may not, show the archers.

Verdict, and two questions

So that's the longbow of the Mary Rose. My reading: a mass-made bow of one piece of yew, and heavy to draw. How heavy is still argued. And it is a Tudor bow. It cannot tell us everything about Agincourt. How heavy were the Mary Rose bows really: nearer 110 pounds, or 160?

Quick answers

How hard was an English longbow to draw?

Nobody can simply measure the originals: the wood of the Mary Rose bows had decayed, and the few that Robert Hardy's team bent on a testing machine had lost around half their strength. So every figure is an estimate. Dr Kooi of Groningen built a mathematical model of a bow, which came within one percent on a replica; applied to the originals, it gave about 100 to 185 pounds, and Hardy's team thought most were 150 to 160. The Mary Rose Trust's own collection page gives 65 to 175 pounds, with a peak at 110. A pull of 110 pounds is like holding 50 kilos at full draw.

How many longbows were found on the Mary Rose?

172. A list of the ship's stores gives 250 bows, and 172 have been found, together with more than 2,300 complete arrows and thousands of broken pieces; the divers raised four chests for longbows and seven for arrows. The Mary Rose Trust calls them the only precisely dated English longbows, and a research project at the University of Southampton calls them the largest collection that has been discovered.

What were English longbows made of?

Every bow from the Mary Rose is made from a single piece of yew. The bowyer kept two kinds of wood in that one piece: on the back, the sapwood from the outside of the tree, which copes best with being stretched; on the belly, facing the archer, the heartwood, which copes best with being squeezed. Yew was dear: an act of Henry VIII of 1541/2 ordered bowyers outside London to make four bows of other wood for every one of yew. And Gerald of Wales, in 1188, says the bows of the archers of Gwent were not of yew but of wild elm.

Do the Mary Rose skeletons prove the crew were archers?

Not on their own. The bioarchaeologist Ann Stirland found that in more than 14 in every 100 of the crew, the tip of the shoulder blade had never fused to the rest, mostly on the left, the bow arm, and she linked it to long, hard use of the heavy bows. But the same condition turns up in people who never drew a war bow: in a study of nearly 1,200 skeletons from an American collection, 8 in every 100 had it. Stirland herself, from the shoulder bones, called many archers on board a reasonable inference: an inference, not a proof.

Are the Mary Rose bows the same as the longbows of Agincourt?

We do not know. The Mary Rose sank in 1545, in Tudor England, 130 years after Agincourt, and very few medieval longbows survive. The written sources do not describe one standard bow: Gerald of Wales, who travelled through Wales in 1188, says the bows of the archers of Gwent were of wild elm, rough and unpolished, but stiff and strong. The Mary Rose gives us one moment, 1545, that we can measure.

Where it happened

  • The Solent, off Portsmouth (A.D. 1545): The Mary Rose heeled over and sank here towards evening on 19 July 1545 (Van der Delft's letter, Letters and Papers XX i no. 1263). The marker is Portsmouth, not the wreck site: we have no coordinates for the wreck in our sources.
  • Southsea Castle (A.D. 1545): The Mary Rose Trust says Henry VIII watched the sinking from here. The castle has been much altered since 1545.
  • Abergavenny (A.D. 1188): Gerald of Wales says Welsh archers shot through an oak door of the castle almost a palm thick, and the arrows were left in the door in memory (Itinerarium Kambriae I.4).
  • Agincourt (Azincourt) (A.D. 1415): The battle of 1415, 130 years before the Mary Rose sank. How close its bows were to the Mary Rose bows, we do not know.

    English Longbows. The largest find, from the wreck of the Mary Rose. The Solent, off Portsmouth. 19 July 1545. King Henry the Eighth is at dinner on board his flagship when a French fleet appears. He leaves in a hurry. Towards evening, one of his great ships, the Mary Rose, heels over and goes down. The emperor's ambassador wrote a few days later. Of the 500 men on board, all were drowned except about 25 or 30. A Fleming who survived told him the water came in through the lowest row of gun ports, left open after firing. The Mary Rose Trust says the king watched her sink from Southsea Castle. On her lowest deck, in the stern, lay chests of longbows. Some had already been carried up, ready for action. The seabed kept them for more than four hundred years. Today: what a real English war bow was, what came up from the wreck, and what the texts really say. Because, says the Mary Rose Trust, her bows are the only precisely dated English longbows we have. 172 of them came up. A research project at the University of Southampton calls them the largest collection that has been discovered. One warning first. 1545 is Tudor England, after the Middle Ages by the usual reckoning. Whether these bows are the bows of Agincourt, a hundred and thirty years earlier, is a question we will come back to. So, what is a longbow? Every bow from the ship is made from a single piece of yew. The bowyer kept two kinds of wood in that one piece. On the back, the sapwood from the outside of the tree, which copes best with being stretched. On the belly, facing the archer, the heartwood, which copes best with being squeezed. The Mary Rose Trust calls the result a perfect, natural spring. And they are long. From 1.84 to 2.11 metres. The divers raised four chests for longbows, and seven for arrows. A list of the ship's stores gives 250 bows. 172 have been found. It lists 9,600 arrows. More than 2,300 complete arrows came up, and thousands of broken pieces. Most are poplar. The iron heads have rusted away, and the feathers of goose or swan are gone. Some arrows were still held in a leather disc with holes for 24, part of a bag worn on the belt. 864 bowstrings were listed on board. Only one, perhaps two, have been found, and only in fragments. These were not heirlooms. The Trust says the bows were mass produced, for the king's ships and his forts. The first bow came up in 1979. A slightly bent, almost black shaft of wood, 6 foot 5 long. The excavators called in the actor Robert Hardy, who had written a history of the longbow. Was it a longbow, or a pike staff? His answer, as he told it later: Good heavens, it is a longbow. And he nearly fainted. For the first drying, the bows were kept in his own cellar. If you are enjoying this type of content, please don't forget to subscribe. So how hard were they to draw? Here the arguments begin. You cannot simply measure the originals. Hardy's team was allowed to bend a few on a testing machine, until they broke. The wood had decayed. The bows had lost around half their strength. So a researcher at Groningen, Dr Kooi, built a mathematical model of a bow. Tested on a replica, it came within one percent. Applied to the originals, it gave about 100 to 185 pounds. Hardy's team thought most were 150 to 160. Not everyone agrees. The Mary Rose Trust's own collection page gives 65 to 175 pounds, with a peak at 110. 110 pounds is like holding 50 kilos at full draw. 160 is more than 70. Either way, these are estimates, made from damaged wood. And the men who drew them? The ship also gave up the bones of her crew. From about 9,000 bones, the bioarchaeologist Ann Stirland pieced together 92 fairly complete skeletons. At least 179 people. One man lay on the main deck, a broken longbow beside him, a leather wristguard over his lower arm. The wristguard carries the badges of Henry the Eighth and Katherine of Aragon. The Trust thinks he may have been a royal archer. He was in his early twenties, 1.79 metres tall, taller than most. Even the shortest bow was longer than he was. The middle of his spine was twisted, and his right finger bones have grooves that the Trust says may come from drawing a longbow again and again. And his teeth suggest he grew up somewhere hotter than England. A study of 2021 names North Africa or Iberia, but says it cannot pinpoint where. Stirland noticed something across the crew. In more than 14 in every 100, the tip of the shoulder blade had never fused to the rest. Mostly on the left, the bow arm. She linked it to long, hard use of the heavy bows. But the same condition turns up in people who never drew a war bow. In a study of nearly 1,200 skeletons from an American collection, 8 in every 100 had it. Stirland herself, from the shoulder bones, called many archers on board a reasonable inference. An inference, not a proof. Behind those men stood the law. In 1363, Edward III ordered every able-bodied man to practise with bow and arrows on feast days, and banned football, handball and cock fighting. The art, says the order, is almost wholly disused, and the realm is like to be kept without archers. A few years before the Mary Rose sank, a new act of Henry the Eighth went further. Almost every man under sixty had to keep a bow and arrows in his house, and practise with long bows. Every household with boys from seven to seventeen had to give each a bow and two arrows. It even set prices. A boy's yew bow, no more than a shilling. Yew was dear. The act orders bowyers outside London to make four bows of other wood for every one of yew. It also rules on yew staves brought into the realm from abroad. Where the Mary Rose's own yew grew, we found no published study to tell us. So what can a Tudor ship tell us about the medieval longbow? Very few medieval longbows survive. Robert Kaiser, in a thesis on the longbow, put it simply: because it was so common, few longbows survive. And the written sources do not describe one standard bow. Gerald of Wales travelled through Wales in 1188, and praised the archers of Gwent. At Abergavenny, he says, their arrows went through an oak door almost a palm thick. The arrows were left in the door, in memory. But their bows, he says, were not of yew. They were of wild elm, rough and unpolished, but stiff and strong. And his word is simply: bow. So how close were the bows of Agincourt to these? We do not know. The Mary Rose gives us one moment, 1545, that we can measure. And in that moment, the bows went down beside the ship's guns, which she had just fired. So, what do we really have? 172 yew bows, all lost on a single day. Thousands of arrows. Draw weights that are estimates, from damaged wood. And bones that may, or may not, show the archers. So that's the longbow of the Mary Rose. My reading: a mass-made bow of one piece of yew, and heavy to draw. How heavy is still argued. And it is a Tudor bow. It cannot tell us everything about Agincourt. How heavy were the Mary Rose bows really: nearer 110 pounds, or 160? And the royal archer, who may have grown up far from England: how do you think he came to serve on an English warship?
