The Lost Diary of the Great Pyramid

Hidden between the stones that sealed a desert cave lay the diary of a man who carried stone for the Great Pyramid. It is the oldest writing on papyrus ever found in Egypt. Its author, probably the inspector Merer himself, was there, most likely in the last years of the work. One entry reads: Day 27: “sets sail from She-Khufu, sails towards Akhet-Khufu, loaded with stone, spends the night at Akhet-Khufu.” Akhet-Khufu means the Horizon of Khufu: the Great Pyramid and its buildings. And not once does he say how the pyramid was built.

So what does a four and a half thousand year old logbook really settle, and what is still a mystery?

Found between the blocking stones

The Red Sea coast of Egypt. A dry valley called Wadi al-Jarf. In 1823, the British explorer John Gardner Wilkinson passed a small hill cut with long chambers. He went in, he wrote, and “found them to be catacombs”. They were not tombs. They were storerooms, most likely for boats. From 2011, a team led by the French Egyptologist Pierre Tallet dug there. It was a harbour of King Khufu, the builder of the Great Pyramid. Its ships sailed from here to the copper and turquoise mines of Sinai, and were probably stored in thirty galleries cut into the hills. In 2013, at the mouths of two galleries, the team found papyrus. Hundreds of fragments, some over 80 centimetres long, lying between the blocking stones. Tallet's guess: it was the crew's last mission, perhaps ended by the king's death, and they buried their records as they sealed the galleries.

Did you know? In 1823 the British explorer John Gardner Wilkinson went into the long chambers at Wadi al-Jarf and, he wrote, “found them to be catacombs”. They were not tombs. They were storerooms, most likely for boats.

The oldest writing on papyrus in Egypt

They are dated. One account names the year after the thirteenth cattle count of Khufu. The count is thought to have been held every second year, so this is year 26 or 27 of his reign, the latest year known for Khufu. That makes them, in the words of Tallet and his colleague Gregory Marouard, “the oldest inscribed papyri that have ever been discovered in Egypt”. Inscribed matters. An older papyrus came from the tomb of an official named Hemaka, in the First Dynasty. It is blank.

Did you know? An older papyrus than Merer's exists. It came from the tomb of an official named Hemaka, in the First Dynasty, and it is blank.

Who was Merer?

So who was the writer? Merer was an inspector, a middle ranking official. He led one section of a crew of boatmen with a magnificent name: the Escorts of the boat “The Uraeus of Khufu is its Prow”. The uraeus is the rearing cobra worn on a pharaoh's forehead. The crew's four sections were called the Big, the Asian, the Prosperous and the Small. Merer's was the Big. The archaeologist Mark Lehner said the papyri are “like Excel spreadsheets of the ancient world”.

Did you know? Merer's crew had four sections, called the Big, the Asian, the Prosperous and the Small. Merer's was the Big.

Ten days on the Nile

A normal day, in Tallet's translation. A phyle is Merer's section. Day 29: “Inspector Merer spends the day with his phyle hauling stones in Tura South; spends the night at Tura South.” Tura is the white limestone quarry across the Nile, about 17 kilometres from the pyramid. Its stone most probably went into the pyramid's outer casing, which has almost all disappeared. Ten days at Tura North, ten days at Tura South, and two or three round trips in every ten. How much stone is that? Tallet risks an estimate: some 30 blocks a boat, at least 200 a month for this crew. The casing needed tens of thousands. A limited number of small crews like Merer's, he reckons, would probably have been enough over the years.

The log also shows how they were fed. On the first day of a month, a man called Idjeru, director of six, sails to Heliopolis “to bring us food”. Three days later he is back with forty sacks of bread. Tallet works it out at nearly a ton: a month's bread for about forty men. And notice the word. Us.

Did you know? Idjeru, director of six, came back from Heliopolis with forty sacks of bread: nearly a ton, by Tallet's reckoning, a month's bread for about forty men.

The king's half-brother

Then, on day 24, Merer works alongside “the noble Ankhhaf, director of Ro-She Khufu”. Ankhhaf was Khufu's half-brother, and, Tallet writes, the vizier in charge of all the king's works. His bust is in the Museum of Fine Arts in Boston. So, at least in these last years, the king's own half-brother ran the project.

Did you know? The noble Ankhhaf, who works alongside Merer's crew on day 24, was Khufu's half-brother, and his bust is in the Museum of Fine Arts in Boston.

Water to the pyramids

But how does a boat full of stone reach a pyramid on a desert plateau? The log names the water: She-Khufu, the pool of Khufu, and its entrance, Ro-She Khufu. In an earlier month, Merer's crew works on the dyke at that entrance. Day 17: “lifting the piles of the dyke”. Tallet reads it as opening the basin that gives boats access to the plateau. That fits Mark Lehner's case, from drill cores at Giza: canals and basins at the foot of the plateau, raised by a Nile flood of about 7 metres, like a hydraulic lift. In 2022, pollen in cores from the Giza floodplain suggested a branch of the Nile standing high enough, in Khufu's time, to help bring stone by boat. In 2024, Eman Ghoneim's team traced a lost branch of the Nile along the pyramid field, about 64 kilometres, from satellite radar and cores. Tallet himself doubts the pool's entrance lay right at Giza: from there, the boats needed a day to reach the pyramid.

How many workers?

So the stone came by water. Who did the work, and how many? The Greek historian Herodotus came to Egypt about two thousand years after Khufu. He was told: “They worked in gangs of a hundred thousand men, each gang for three months.” His interpreter, he says, read him writing on the pyramid: how much was spent on “radishes and onions and garlic for the workmen”. 1,600 talents of silver. Modern estimates are far smaller. Mark Lehner's, as Tallet sums it up: at most 20,000 on the whole project, perhaps only 4,000 at the pyramid itself. Merer's men do not look like slaves. Tallet calls them highly qualified, fed by the state, and named with a word he translates as the Elite. The crew's scribe, Dedi, even added a small note in red ink between two columns: give equipment, or pieces of fabric, to Inspector Merer.

Did you know? Herodotus says his interpreter read him writing on the pyramid: how much was spent on “radishes and onions and garlic for the workmen”. Sixteen hundred talents of silver.

Did you know? The crew's scribe, Dedi, added a small note in red ink between two columns of the log: give equipment, or pieces of fabric, to Inspector Merer.

How did the stones go up?

That leaves the big question. How did the stones go up? The diary does not say. Tallet calls the building methods the object of “passionate and contradictory debates”, and stays out of them. Herodotus had an answer: “short wooden logs as levers”, lifting each block from step to step. Modern ideas: one long ramp, a ramp coiling around the sides, or one inside the pyramid. In 2018, a team recording ancient graffiti at Hatnub, an alabaster quarry, announced a ramp it had cleared. One of its directors, Roland Enmarch, had begun his career studying Egyptian poetry. The ramp climbs at 20 percent or more, between two staircases cut with post holes. A block on a sled, ropes round wooden posts, and men above and below hauling it up together. Tool marks and two inscriptions of Khufu date it, the excavators say, at least to his reign. A ramp that steep could be far shorter. Enmarch finds it reasonable to speculate the same method was used at the Great Pyramid. But it is a quarry, not the pyramid. And aliens? Enmarch: there are always those lacking in imagination who cannot accept a human explanation, and instead groundlessly evoke aliens or Atlanteans.

Did you know? Roland Enmarch, one of the directors of the mission that announced the Hatnub ramp in 2018, had begun his career studying Egyptian poetry.

What would settle it

So what do we really have? A section of about forty men, named, fed and dated. Boats of casing stone. Water at the foot of the plateau. The king's half-brother in charge. What we do not have: how the blocks were lifted, how many people worked, or how the chambers inside were built. What would settle more? A dig in the Tura quarries. Today they are a military zone, closed to digging, Tallet notes. In 1942 a block was found there, still on its wooden sled, with an inspection mark. Nobody recorded its size or its text.

Did you know? In 1942 a block was found in the Tura quarries, still on its wooden sled, with an inspection mark. Nobody recorded its size or its text.

Verdict

My verdict: the oldest inscribed papyri ever found in Egypt show how the stone travelled, by boat, across the river and its basins, and who moved it: organised, fed crews, under the king's half-brother. How the pyramid was raised, they do not tell us. On that, nobody knows yet.

Over to you

Herodotus said a hundred thousand men at a time. Merer's section was about forty. How many do you think it really took? If you could read one more page of Merer's diary, what would you want it to say?

Quick answers

What is the Diary of Merer?

The logbook kept, most probably, by an inspector named Merer, a middle ranking official who led one section of a crew of boatmen, the Escorts of the boat “The Uraeus of Khufu is its Prow”, late in the reign of King Khufu, the builder of the Great Pyramid. In 2013 a team led by the French Egyptologist Pierre Tallet found the papyri at Wadi al-Jarf, a harbour of Khufu on Egypt's Red Sea coast: hundreds of fragments, some over 80 centimetres long, lying between the blocking stones at the mouths of two galleries. One account names the year after the thirteenth cattle count of Khufu; the count is thought to have been held every second year, so this is year 26 or 27 of his reign, the latest year known for Khufu. Tallet's guess: it was the crew's last mission, perhaps ended by the king's death, and they buried their records as they sealed the galleries.

Are the Wadi al-Jarf papyri the oldest papyri ever found?

Not quite. They are, in the words of Tallet and his colleague Gregory Marouard, “the oldest inscribed papyri that have ever been discovered in Egypt”: the oldest writing on papyrus ever found in Egypt. Inscribed matters. An older papyrus came from the tomb of an official named Hemaka, in the First Dynasty. It is blank.

Does the Diary of Merer say how the Great Pyramid was built?

No. Not once does Merer say how the pyramid was built, and Tallet calls the building methods the object of “passionate and contradictory debates”. What the logbook shows is how the stone travelled and who moved it. Merer's section hauled stone at Tura, the white limestone quarry across the Nile, about 17 kilometres from the pyramid, and sailed it to Akhet-Khufu, the Horizon of Khufu: the Great Pyramid and its buildings. Ten days at Tura North, ten days at Tura South, and two or three round trips in every ten. Tura's stone most probably went into the pyramid's outer casing, which has almost all disappeared.

How did boats bring the stone to the Great Pyramid?

The log names the water: She-Khufu, the pool of Khufu, and its entrance, Ro-She Khufu. In an earlier month Merer's crew works on the dyke at that entrance, and Tallet reads it as opening the basin that gives boats access to the plateau. That fits Mark Lehner's case, from drill cores at Giza: canals and basins at the foot of the plateau, raised by a Nile flood of about 7 metres, like a hydraulic lift. In 2022 pollen in cores from the Giza floodplain suggested a branch of the Nile standing high enough, in Khufu's time, to help bring stone by boat, and in 2024 Eman Ghoneim's team traced a lost branch of the Nile along the pyramid field, about 64 kilometres, from satellite radar and cores. Tallet himself doubts the pool's entrance lay right at Giza: from there, the boats needed a day to reach the pyramid.

How many workers built the Great Pyramid?

Nobody knows. The Greek historian Herodotus, who came to Egypt about two thousand years after Khufu, was told: “They worked in gangs of a hundred thousand men, each gang for three months.” Modern estimates are far smaller. Mark Lehner's, as Tallet sums it up: at most 20,000 on the whole project, perhaps only 4,000 at the pyramid itself. Merer's own section was about forty men, and Tallet reckons a limited number of small crews like his would probably have been enough to bring the tens of thousands of casing blocks over the years. Merer's men do not look like slaves: Tallet calls them highly qualified, fed by the state, and named with a word he translates as the Elite.

Where it happened

  • Wadi al-Jarf (2555 B.C.): A harbour of King Khufu on the Red Sea coast (Gulf of Suez), with thirty galleries for boats; the papyri were found here in 2013 between the blocking stones of two galleries (Tallet and Marouard 2014). The year stands for the papyri, year 26 or 27 of Khufu, and is approximate. Coordinates approximate.
  • Giza: the Great Pyramid (Akhet-Khufu) (2555 B.C.): Akhet-Khufu, the Horizon of Khufu: the Great Pyramid and its buildings, where Merer's boats delivered the stone. The year is approximate (late in Khufu's reign).
  • Tura quarries (2555 B.C.): The white limestone quarries across the Nile, about 17 km from the pyramid (Sheisha et al. 2022), where Merer's section hauled stone at Tura North and Tura South, whose exact positions are not known. Today a military zone. Year approximate; coordinates approximate.
  • Heliopolis (2555 B.C.): Where Idjeru, director of six, sailed “to bring us food”, coming back three days later with forty sacks of bread (Papyrus B). Year approximate; coordinates approximate (modern Matariya).
  • Wadi Maghara, Sinai (2565 B.C.): The copper and turquoise mining region of Sinai, reached by Khufu's ships from Wadi al-Jarf (D1.5). The year stands for Khufu's reign and is approximate. Coordinates approximate.
  • Hatnub alabaster quarry (A.D. 2018): Where the Hatnub mission (Gourdon and Enmarch) announced in 2018 a ramp at 20 percent or more between two staircases with post holes, dated by tool marks and two inscriptions of Khufu at least to his reign. The year stands for the announcement. Coordinates approximate.

    Transcript

    Read the full narration

    The Great Pyramid. Inspector Merer's diary. Hidden between the stones that sealed a desert cave lay the diary of a man who carried stone for the Great Pyramid. It is the oldest writing on papyrus ever found in Egypt. Its author, probably the inspector Merer himself, was there, most likely in the last years of the work. One entry reads: Day 27: sets sail from She-Khufu, sails towards Akhet-Khufu, loaded with stone, spends the night at Akhet-Khufu. Akhet-Khufu means the Horizon of Khufu: the Great Pyramid and its buildings. And not once does he say how the pyramid was built. So what does a four and a half thousand year old logbook really settle, and what is still a mystery? The Red Sea coast of Egypt. A dry valley called Wadi al-Jarf. In 1823, the British explorer John Gardner Wilkinson passed a small hill cut with long chambers. He went in, he wrote, and found them to be catacombs. They were not tombs. They were storerooms, most likely for boats. From 2011, a team led by the French Egyptologist Pierre Tallet dug there. It was a harbour of King Khufu, the builder of the Great Pyramid. Its ships sailed from here to the copper and turquoise mines of Sinai, and were probably stored in thirty galleries cut into the hills. In 2013, at the mouths of two galleries, the team found papyrus. Hundreds of fragments, some over 80 centimetres long, lying between the blocking stones. Tallet's guess: it was the crew's last mission, perhaps ended by the king's death, and they buried their records as they sealed the galleries. They are dated. One account names the year after the thirteenth cattle count of Khufu. The count is thought to have been held every second year, so this is year 26 or 27 of his reign, the latest year known for Khufu. That makes them, in the words of Tallet and his colleague Gregory Marouard, the oldest inscribed papyri that have ever been discovered in Egypt. Inscribed matters. An older papyrus came from the tomb of an official named Hemaka, in the First Dynasty. It is blank. So who was the writer? Merer was an inspector, a middle ranking official. He led one section of a crew of boatmen with a magnificent name: the Escorts of the boat The Uraeus of Khufu is its Prow. The uraeus is the rearing cobra worn on a pharaoh's forehead. The crew's four sections were called the Big, the Asian, the Prosperous and the Small. Merer's was the Big. The archaeologist Mark Lehner said the papyri are like Excel spreadsheets of the ancient world. A normal day, in Tallet's translation. A phyle is Merer's section. Day 29: Inspector Merer spends the day with his phyle hauling stones in Tura South; spends the night at Tura South. Tura is the white limestone quarry across the Nile, about 17 kilometres from the pyramid. Its stone most probably went into the pyramid's outer casing, which has almost all disappeared. Ten days at Tura North, ten days at Tura South, and two or three round trips in every ten. How much stone is that? Tallet risks an estimate: some 30 blocks a boat, at least 200 a month for this crew. The casing needed tens of thousands. A limited number of small crews like Merer's, he reckons, would probably have been enough over the years. The log also shows how they were fed. On the first day of a month, a man called Idjeru, director of six, sails to Heliopolis to bring us food. Three days later he is back with forty sacks of bread. Tallet works it out at nearly a ton: a month's bread for about forty men. And notice the word. Us. Then, on day 24, Merer works alongside the noble Ankhhaf, director of Ro-She Khufu. Ankhhaf was Khufu's half-brother, and, Tallet writes, the vizier in charge of all the king's works. His bust is in the Museum of Fine Arts in Boston. So, at least in these last years, the king's own half-brother ran the project. If you are enjoying this type of content, please don't forget to subscribe. But how does a boat full of stone reach a pyramid on a desert plateau? The log names the water: She-Khufu, the pool of Khufu, and its entrance, Ro-She Khufu. In an earlier month, Merer's crew works on the dyke at that entrance. Day 17: lifting the piles of the dyke. Tallet reads it as opening the basin that gives boats access to the plateau. That fits Mark Lehner's case, from drill cores at Giza: canals and basins at the foot of the plateau, raised by a Nile flood of about 7 metres, like a hydraulic lift. In 2022, pollen in cores from the Giza floodplain suggested a branch of the Nile standing high enough, in Khufu's time, to help bring stone by boat. In 2024, Eman Ghoneim's team traced a lost branch of the Nile along the pyramid field, about 64 kilometres, from satellite radar and cores. Tallet himself doubts the pool's entrance lay right at Giza: from there, the boats needed a day to reach the pyramid. So the stone came by water. Who did the work, and how many? The Greek historian Herodotus came to Egypt about two thousand years after Khufu. He was told: They worked in gangs of a hundred thousand men, each gang for three months. His interpreter, he says, read him writing on the pyramid: how much was spent on radishes and onions and garlic for the workmen. 1,600 talents of silver. Modern estimates are far smaller. Mark Lehner's, as Tallet sums it up: at most 20,000 on the whole project, perhaps only 4,000 at the pyramid itself. Merer's men do not look like slaves. Tallet calls them highly qualified, fed by the state, and named with a word he translates as the Elite. The crew's scribe, Dedi, even added a small note in red ink between two columns: give equipment, or pieces of fabric, to Inspector Merer. That leaves the big question. How did the stones go up? The diary does not say. Tallet calls the building methods the object of passionate and contradictory debates, and stays out of them. Herodotus had an answer: short wooden logs as levers, lifting each block from step to step. Modern ideas: one long ramp, a ramp coiling around the sides, or one inside the pyramid. In 2018, a team recording ancient graffiti at Hatnub, an alabaster quarry, announced a ramp it had cleared. One of its directors, Roland Enmarch, had begun his career studying Egyptian poetry. The ramp climbs at 20 percent or more, between two staircases cut with post holes. A block on a sled, ropes round wooden posts, and men above and below hauling it up together. Tool marks and two inscriptions of Khufu date it, the excavators say, at least to his reign. A ramp that steep could be far shorter. Enmarch finds it reasonable to speculate the same method was used at the Great Pyramid. But it is a quarry, not the pyramid. And aliens? Enmarch: there are always those lacking in imagination who cannot accept a human explanation, and instead groundlessly evoke aliens or Atlanteans. So what do we really have? A section of about forty men, named, fed and dated. Boats of casing stone. Water at the foot of the plateau. The king's half-brother in charge. What we do not have: how the blocks were lifted, how many people worked, or how the chambers inside were built. What would settle more? A dig in the Tura quarries. Today they are a military zone, closed to digging, Tallet notes. In 1942 a block was found there, still on its wooden sled, with an inspection mark. Nobody recorded its size or its text. My verdict: the oldest inscribed papyri ever found in Egypt show how the stone travelled, by boat, across the river and its basins, and who moved it: organised, fed crews, under the king's half-brother. How the pyramid was raised, they do not tell us. On that, nobody knows yet. Herodotus said a hundred thousand men at a time. Merer's section was about forty. How many do you think it really took?
