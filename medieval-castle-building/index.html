How a Medieval Castle Was Built: "The Greatest Castle Never Built"

North Wales. 27 February 1296. Two men running a building site write to the king's treasury, the Exchequer, at Westminster. They are £500 short. The men's pay is very much in arrear. We are having the greatest difficulty in keeping them, they write, because they simply have nothing to live on. And after the letter is closed, they add one more line. “For God's sake be quick with the money for the works … otherwise everything done up till now will have been of no avail.”

The building site was a castle: Beaumaris, on the island of Anglesey. And the first name on the letter is Master James of St George, the man in charge of the king's castle works in Wales. Today: how a castle like this was built, what the paperwork says that the stones cannot, and who designed it. Because before it was a fortress, a castle was a payroll.

Why Beaumaris?

In 1294, the Welsh rose against English rule, led by Madog ap Llywelyn. Edward I, king of England, had conquered North Wales a decade earlier, and had begun the great castles of Conwy, Caernarfon and Harlech. He crushed the rising, and added one more castle, on Anglesey, beside the Menai Strait. The site's Norman French name, Beau Mareys, means beautiful marsh. By July 1295, when the king himself crossed over to Anglesey, the work had already begun.

Llanfaes, the town that had to go

A mile away stood a Welsh town, Llanfaes, a busy port. It had to go. As soon as Edward's troops arrived, says the Welsh heritage body Cadw, they started to pull it down. The townspeople petitioned the king. Their houses have been carried off to Beaumaris, they said, and nothing is left. Many were forcibly resettled in a new town, Newborough, about 20 kilometres away.

What the letter asks for

So where could so much money go in a week? The letter answers. 400 masons, both cutters and layers. 2,000 minor workmen. 200 quarrymen. 30 smiths. 100 carts, 60 waggons, and 30 boats bringing stone and sea coal. And for all of it, £250 a week, throughout the season. Careful, though. This is a request, written to get money. It says what they needed, not who was on site on a given day. For the first summer, historians who worked through the pay accounts give a similar picture. About 450 masons, 375 quarriers, and 1,800 other workmen.

Where the workers came from

Where did they all come from? Arnold Taylor, the leading authority on these castles, described a small nucleus of experts, probably including some from the Continent, and under it a veritable army. Quarriers and stone-cutters, masons, smiths and carpenters, plumbers and labourers, all drawn from every shire in England. The historian Jane Laughton notes craftsmen for the Welsh works coming from as far away as Cumberland, Norfolk and Sussex. Not all of them chose to come. In June 1295, the king ordered the justice of Chester to choose a hundred suitable masons. They were to come with their tools to Caernarfon, without delay. Their travel was paid. Historians call this impressment: craftsmen taken into the king's works by order.

Hewers, layers and winter work

On site, the masons were two trades. Hewers cut and shaped the stone. Layers set it in the wall. They were not paid one rate. Accounts from Beaumaris and Caernarfon, a generation later, show twenty-two different time rates. A hewer might earn 15 pence a week, or as much as 2 shillings and 9 pence. And they did not stop for the winter. Masons, quarrymen and minor workmen were kept on all through it, making mortar and breaking up stone for lime. Even the two men in charge were living in timber buildings inside the castle itself.

Less than a year in

And it rose fast. Less than a year in, some of it stood about 28 feet high, 8.5 metres. The lowest part, 20 feet. 10 of the outer towers were begun, and 4 of the inner. 4 gates were hung, and shut and locked every night. Each gateway was to have 3 portcullises. And at high tide, they promised, a 40-ton ship would be able to come fully laden right up to the castle gateway. So much have we been able to do, they wrote, in spite of all the Welshmen. And a few lines later: Welshmen are Welshmen, and you need to understand them properly. That tells you what the walls were for.

Did you know? The castle had its own harbour. In February 1296 its builders promised that at high tide a forty-ton ship could come fully laden right up to the castle gateway, and the dock is still there (the letter, TNA E 101/5/18 no. 11, translation A. J. Taylor; Cadw).

Holes in the walls: the scaffolding

How did the stone get up there? Look closely at these walls, and you see rows of small holes. They held the builders' scaffolding. At Beaumaris, says the official guide of 1961, some mark scaffolding fixed at an incline, for loads of stone to be wheeled or dragged up. At Harlech, on some towers, the holes climb in a spiral. Taylor called it helicoidal scaffolding, winding round the tower as it rose. And Taylor found the same thing in Savoy, in the Alps, but rarely anywhere else. Which brings us to the man in charge.

Did you know? At Harlech the putlog holes climb round some towers in a spiral. Arnold Taylor called it helicoidal scaffolding, and found the same thing in Savoy but rarely anywhere else (A. J. Taylor, Harlech Castle, Cadw guide).

Master James of St George

Master James came to Wales from Savoy, a state in the western Alps, in 1278. He had directed castles and other works for the counts of Savoy for at least sixteen years. His name most likely comes from a castle palace he built for Count Philip: Saint-Georges-d'Espéranche, south-east of Lyon. And Edward had seen it. In June 1273, Edward was there, while parts of it were still being built.

1950: the night train to Turin

We know all this because of one man's detective work. In 1950, Arnold Taylor, an inspector of ancient monuments, went looking for Master James in the Alps. In the archives at Turin, he felt at first that he had drawn a blank. Then, in a library in Lausanne, a clue. At 3.37 the next morning, he was on the Orient Express, back towards Turin. The next three and a half days, he wrote, were as productive of crucial sources as any I have ever spent. In the accounts of Savoy, he found Master James working under his father at Yverdon, and later being paid at Chillon.

Did you know? A night train helped solve the mystery of Master James. In September 1950 Arnold Taylor left Lausanne at 3.37 in the morning on the Orient Express, back to the archives in Turin, and found Master James in the accounts of Savoy (A. D. Saunders, Proceedings of the British Academy 138, 2006, quoting Taylor's diary).

Architect or manager?

So was James the architect of these castles? UNESCO thinks so. Its World Heritage listing calls him the king's chief architect, and the greatest military architect of the age. But in 1928, the castle scholar Douglas Simpson argued that James was not a master mason at all, but an administrator who looked after the money. Taylor's archive work settled that part: James was a mason. But even Taylor's own guidebook for Cadw says it is difficult to assess how far Caernarfon owes its form to its designer, and how far to the king and his circle. In 2003, the architectural historian Nicola Coldstream reopened the question. Her title says where she looked: architects, advisers and design. In 2020, Malcolm Hislop argued for James. Some of the castles, he says, are too alike inside not to share one mind. And James, the best paid of the known masters, fits. So the mason from Savoy ran the works. How much of the design was his, and how much the king's, is still argued.

The castle never finished

And Beaumaris was never finished. The letter had already mentioned the wars it feared, with France and Scotland. The money went to the wars. Work slowed, and then stopped. It picked up again for a few years after 1306. By the 1320s, says Cadw, it had petered out. The south gatehouse and the six great towers of the inner ward never reached their intended height. One gate was barely started before it was abandoned. Cadw calls Beaumaris the greatest castle never built.

What we really have

So, what do we really have? A castle you can walk around, and a letter that tells you what it took. Masons and quarrymen, carts and boats, £250 a week, and men with nothing to live on. What we do not have is a signature on the design. We do not know for certain who drew the plan.

Verdict

So that's how a castle like Beaumaris was built. Not by one genius alone, but by thousands of workers, some of them pressed, and a treasury that could not keep up.

Quick answers

How was a medieval castle like Beaumaris built?

Not by one genius alone, but by thousands of workers and a treasury that could not keep up. A letter of 27 February 1296 from the two men in charge asks for 400 masons, 2,000 minor workmen, 200 quarrymen, 30 smiths, 100 carts, 60 waggons and 30 boats bringing stone and sea coal, and £250 a week throughout the season. It is a request written to get money, not a count of who was on site. For the first summer, historians who worked through the pay accounts give about 450 masons, 375 quarriers and 1,800 other workmen.

Where did the castle builders come from?

Arnold Taylor described a small nucleus of experts, probably including some from the Continent, and under it a veritable army of quarriers, masons, smiths, carpenters, plumbers and labourers drawn from every shire in England. Not all of them chose to come: in June 1295 the king ordered the justice of Chester to choose a hundred suitable masons and send them with their tools to Caernarfon, their travel paid. Historians call this impressment.

What are the small holes in castle walls?

Rows of small holes, called putlog holes, held the builders' scaffolding. At Beaumaris, says the official guide of 1961, some mark scaffolding fixed at an incline, for loads of stone to be wheeled or dragged up. At Harlech, on some towers, the holes climb in a spiral: Arnold Taylor called it helicoidal scaffolding, and found the same thing in Savoy but rarely anywhere else.

Who designed Beaumaris Castle?

We do not know for certain. Master James of St George, a mason from Savoy, ran the king's castle works in Wales. UNESCO calls him Edward I's chief architect; in 1928 Douglas Simpson argued he was an administrator, until Taylor's archive work showed he was a mason. Taylor's own Cadw guide says it is difficult to assess how far Caernarfon owes its form to its designer and how far to the king and his circle. Nicola Coldstream reopened the question in 2003; Malcolm Hislop argued for James in 2020.

Why was Beaumaris Castle never finished?

The money went to the wars with France and Scotland. Work slowed and then stopped, picked up again for a few years after 1306, and by the 1320s, says Cadw, it had petered out. The south gatehouse and the six great towers of the inner ward never reached their intended height, and one gate was barely started. Cadw calls Beaumaris the greatest castle never built.

Where it happened

  • Beaumaris Castle (A.D. 1295): Begun 1295 for Edward I beside the Menai Strait; never finished (Cadw).
  • Llanfaes (A.D. 1295): The Welsh port a mile from the castle, pulled down; the point is the modern village, the medieval town's extent is not known to us.
  • Newborough, Anglesey (A.D. 1295): The new town where many people of Llanfaes were forcibly resettled (Cadw); the year of the move is not given in our sources.
  • Conwy (A.D. 1296): Where the letter of 27 February 1296 was written (Morris 1901).
  • Caernarfon Castle (A.D. 1295): In June 1295 a hundred masons chosen at Chester were ordered here (Calendar of Close Rolls).
  • Harlech Castle (A.D. 1283): Putlog holes climbing in a spiral: Taylor's helicoidal scaffolding.
  • Chester (A.D. 1295): The justice of Chester was ordered to choose a hundred suitable masons.
  • Saint-Georges-d'Espéranche (A.D. 1273): Count Philip of Savoy's castle palace, very likely built under Master James; Edward was there in June 1273 (Saunders).
  • Yverdon (A.D. 1266): Taylor found Master James working here under his father, Master John, in the accounts of Savoy; before the Chillon payments, exact year not in our sources (Saunders).
  • Chillon (A.D. 1266): Payments to Master James here in 1266 to 1267 (Saunders).
  • Turin (A.D. 1950): The archives where Taylor searched the accounts of Savoy in 1950.

    How a Medieval Castle Was Built. The greatest castle never built. North Wales. 27 February 1296. Two men running a building site write to the king's treasury, the Exchequer, at Westminster. They are £500 short. The men's pay is very much in arrear. We are having the greatest difficulty in keeping them, they write, because they simply have nothing to live on. And after the letter is closed, they add one more line. “For God's sake be quick with the money for the works … otherwise everything done up till now will have been of no avail.” The building site was a castle: Beaumaris, on the island of Anglesey. And the first name on the letter is Master James of St George, the man in charge of the king's castle works in Wales. Today: how a castle like this was built, what the paperwork says that the stones cannot, and who designed it. Because before it was a fortress, a castle was a payroll. In 1294, the Welsh rose against English rule, led by Madog ap Llywelyn. Edward the First, king of England, had conquered North Wales a decade earlier, and had begun the great castles of Conwy, Caernarfon and Harlech. He crushed the rising, and added one more castle, on Anglesey, beside the Menai Strait. The site's Norman French name, Beau Mareys, means beautiful marsh. By July 1295, when the king himself crossed over to Anglesey, the work had already begun. A mile away stood a Welsh town, Llanfaes, a busy port. It had to go. As soon as Edward's troops arrived, says the Welsh heritage body Cadw, they started to pull it down. The townspeople petitioned the king. Their houses have been carried off to Beaumaris, they said, and nothing is left. Many were forcibly resettled in a new town, Newborough, about 20 kilometres away. So where could so much money go in a week? The letter answers. 400 masons, both cutters and layers. 2,000 minor workmen. 200 quarrymen. 30 smiths. 100 carts, 60 waggons, and 30 boats bringing stone and sea coal. And for all of it, £250 a week, throughout the season. Careful, though. This is a request, written to get money. It says what they needed, not who was on site on a given day. For the first summer, historians who worked through the pay accounts give a similar picture. About 450 masons, 375 quarriers, and 1,800 other workmen. Where did they all come from? Arnold Taylor, the leading authority on these castles, described a small nucleus of experts, probably including some from the Continent, and under it a veritable army. Quarriers and stone-cutters, masons, smiths and carpenters, plumbers and labourers, all drawn from every shire in England. The historian Jane Laughton notes craftsmen for the Welsh works coming from as far away as Cumberland, Norfolk and Sussex. Not all of them chose to come. In June 1295, the king ordered the justice of Chester to choose a hundred suitable masons. They were to come with their tools to Caernarfon, without delay. Their travel was paid. Historians call this impressment: craftsmen taken into the king's works by order. On site, the masons were two trades. Hewers cut and shaped the stone. Layers set it in the wall. They were not paid one rate. Accounts from Beaumaris and Caernarfon, a generation later, show twenty-two different time rates. A hewer might earn 15 pence a week, or as much as 2 shillings and 9 pence. And they did not stop for the winter. Masons, quarrymen and minor workmen were kept on all through it, making mortar and breaking up stone for lime. Even the two men in charge were living in timber buildings inside the castle itself. And it rose fast. Less than a year in, some of it stood about 28 feet high, 8.5 metres. The lowest part, 20 feet. 10 of the outer towers were begun, and 4 of the inner. 4 gates were hung, and shut and locked every night. Each gateway was to have 3 portcullises. And at high tide, they promised, a 40-ton ship would be able to come fully laden right up to the castle gateway. So much have we been able to do, they wrote, in spite of all the Welshmen. And a few lines later: Welshmen are Welshmen, and you need to understand them properly. That tells you what the walls were for. If you are enjoying this type of content, please don't forget to subscribe. How did the stone get up there? Look closely at these walls, and you see rows of small holes. They held the builders' scaffolding. At Beaumaris, says the official guide of 1961, some mark scaffolding fixed at an incline, for loads of stone to be wheeled or dragged up. At Harlech, on some towers, the holes climb in a spiral. Taylor called it helicoidal scaffolding, winding round the tower as it rose. And Taylor found the same thing in Savoy, in the Alps, but rarely anywhere else. Which brings us to the man in charge. Master James came to Wales from Savoy, a state in the western Alps, in 1278. He had directed castles and other works for the counts of Savoy for at least sixteen years. His name most likely comes from a castle palace he built for Count Philip: Saint-Georges-d'Espéranche, south-east of Lyon. And Edward had seen it. In June 1273, Edward was there, while parts of it were still being built. We know all this because of one man's detective work. In 1950, Arnold Taylor, an inspector of ancient monuments, went looking for Master James in the Alps. In the archives at Turin, he felt at first that he had drawn a blank. Then, in a library in Lausanne, a clue. At 3.37 the next morning, he was on the Orient Express, back towards Turin. The next three and a half days, he wrote, were as productive of crucial sources as any I have ever spent. In the accounts of Savoy, he found Master James working under his father at Yverdon, and later being paid at Chillon. So was James the architect of these castles? UNESCO thinks so. Its World Heritage listing calls him the king's chief architect, and the greatest military architect of the age. But in 1928, the castle scholar Douglas Simpson argued that James was not a master mason at all, but an administrator who looked after the money. Taylor's archive work settled that part: James was a mason. But even Taylor's own guidebook for Cadw says it is difficult to assess how far Caernarfon owes its form to its designer, and how far to the king and his circle. In 2003, the architectural historian Nicola Coldstream reopened the question. Her title says where she looked: architects, advisers and design. In 2020, Malcolm Hislop argued for James. Some of the castles, he says, are too alike inside not to share one mind. And James, the best paid of the known masters, fits. So the mason from Savoy ran the works. How much of the design was his, and how much the king's, is still argued. And Beaumaris was never finished. The letter had already mentioned the wars it feared, with France and Scotland. The money went to the wars. Work slowed, and then stopped. It picked up again for a few years after 1306. By the 1320s, says Cadw, it had petered out. The south gatehouse and the six great towers of the inner ward never reached their intended height. One gate was barely started before it was abandoned. Cadw calls Beaumaris the greatest castle never built. So, what do we really have? A castle you can walk around, and a letter that tells you what it took. Masons and quarrymen, carts and boats, £250 a week, and men with nothing to live on. What we do not have is a signature on the design. We do not know for certain who drew the plan. So that's how a castle like Beaumaris was built. Not by one genius alone, but by thousands of workers, some of them pressed, and a treasury that could not keep up. Two questions for you. Was Master James the architect, or the man who made the king's plan happen? And which detail stays with you: the ship at the gate, the houses from Llanfaes, or the men with nothing to live on?
