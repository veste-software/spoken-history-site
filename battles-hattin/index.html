Hattin, 1187: The Battle That Lost the True Cross

"Be quiet; we shall not have beaten them until that tent falls!" A sultan says that to his son, on a battlefield in Galilee, on the 4th of July, 1187. Twice, the Christian king had charged down from a hill. Twice, the son, al-Afdal, had cried out that they had won. As his father spoke, the tent fell. Saladin dismounted, bowed to the ground in thanks to God, and wept for joy. The historian Ibn al-Athir was told this as al-Afdal's memory of his first battle.

Did you know? In the Arabic text of Ibn al-Athir and its French translation of 1872, al-Afdal also remembers his father as the Franks charged: he had changed colour, and he was holding his beard in his hand (Recueil des historiens des croisades, Historiens orientaux I, 1872, pp. 685 to 686).

That tent was the king of Jerusalem's. When it fell, his kingdom had lost its army, its king, and the relic they carried into battle: the True Cross. How did an army camped beside good springs end up dying of thirst on a dry hill? And who was to blame? The sources, Christian and Muslim, blame different men.

Reynald and the caravan

It began with a caravan. Probably early in 1187, Reynald of Chatillon, lord of the castle of Kerak, beyond the Dead Sea, attacked a caravan coming from Egypt, during a truce. They pleaded the truce. Baha al-Din, later in Saladin's service, gives his answer: "Ask your Muhammad to deliver you." Saladin swore to kill him with his own hand. On the 2nd of July, he stormed the town of Tiberias, on the Sea of Galilee. Only the citadel held out. Inside were the wife of Raymond, count of Tripoli, and her children. Ibn al-Athir says Saladin besieged Tiberias for one reason: to make the Franks leave their position and fight.

Did you know? Baha al-Din, who tells Reynald's reply to the caravan, was not there. "This is what I have been told by many persons, for I was not present." He entered Saladin's service only in June 1188 (tr. C. R. Conder, 1897, pp. 43 and 126).

The camp at the springs of Sepphoris

That position was the springs of Sepphoris, about thirty kilometres to the west. A Latin writer of the time counts 1,200 knights, and 18,000 foot soldiers or more. The Old French chronicle of Ernoul says many were paid from the English king's treasure, and carried banners with his arms. In 2021, archaeologists reported a crusader camp at those springs, where Frankish armies gathered more than once. Most of the metal they found was horseshoe nails, many of them broken. The archaeologist Rafael Lewis reads them as farriers at work, shoeing warhorses before battle. And from Jerusalem they had brought the True Cross, believed to hold a piece of the cross of Christ.

The night council

On the evening of the 2nd of July, King Guy called his barons. Raymond, whose wife was trapped in that citadel, told the king to let the town fall. In our rendering of Ernoul: "Tiberias is mine, and my wife and my goods are in it, and no one will lose as much as I, if it is lost." "Between here and Tiberias there is no water, except one little spring ... That is nothing for an army." The Master of the Templars, Gerard of Ridefort, said there was still some wolf's hair in that advice. The barons sided with Raymond. But near midnight, when the king had eaten, the Master came to him. "Sire, do you believe this traitor ... ? ... have it cried through the army that all arm ... and follow the banner of the Holy Cross." The king did not dare refuse him, says Ernoul. The Master had made him king. But another version of the chronicle has Raymond himself urge the king to go. Who said what that night, we cannot know.

Did you know? In the base manuscript of Ernoul printed in 1871, the Templar Master says there is still some bear's hair in Raymond's advice. Other manuscripts have a wolf's hair, and the Latin Libellus has the wolf too, so we followed the wolf (ed. Mas Latrie, 1871, p. 160 and variants; ed. Stevenson, 1875, p. 222).

The march into the dry hills

On Friday, the 3rd of July, the army left the springs. Saladin's men shot at them all day. By the middle of the afternoon, says Ernoul, they were only halfway to Tiberias. Near the village of Turan they probably passed one small spring. In 1989 it was measured for the historian Benjamin Kedar: 180 litres an hour. One spring at Sepphoris, measured in 1949, gave 108,000. In the Latin account, Raymond begs the king to push on to the water. The king orders the tents pitched. Seeing the tents go up, Raymond says, in our rendering: "Alas! alas! Lord God, the war is over, we are handed over to death, and the land is destroyed." Ernoul tells it the other way round. There, it was Raymond who advised the halt.

Thirst, fire and smoke

They camped, says the Latin writer, at a dry halting place, where more blood was shed that night than water. Ernoul: not a man, nor horse, nor beast drank all night. Saladin's men set fire to the dry grass. When, the sources disagree. Ibn al-Athir writes that the wind carried the heat and smoke onto the Franks.

The battle at the Horns of Hattin

The foot soldiers broke, and ran up a hill. The king and the bishops sent to ask them to defend the Cross. Their answer, in our rendering of the Latin: We are not coming, because we are dead with thirst and cannot fight. Raymond charged. Saladin's men opened their ranks, let him through, and closed again behind him. He did not turn back. Tiberias, with his wife inside, was two leagues away. He rode to Tyre. Traitor, or the only man who saw it coming? Baha al-Din calls him the most intelligent man of his race, who saw disaster coming and saved himself. The bishop of Acre, who carried the Cross, was mortally wounded, and the Cross was taken. Imad al-Din, Saladin's secretary, who rode with the army, wrote, in our rendering: "The capture of this cross is more important in their eyes than that of their king." The king's last knights held a hill near Hattin, around his tent. They charged, and charged again. Then, Ibn al-Athir writes, they dismounted, and sat down on the ground. And the tent fell.

Did you know? Imad al-Din describes the captured Cross: covered with a casing of fine gold, set with pearls and jewels, and kept ready for days of danger and for their feasts (in Abu Shama, Recueil des historiens des croisades, Historiens orientaux IV, 1898, p. 274; our rendering).

In Saladin's tent

Saladin had King Guy and Reynald brought to his tent. Baha al-Din, who heard it from others, says the king was given a bowl of sherbet made with iced rose water. Guy drank, and passed the bowl to Reynald. Saladin said to the interpreter: Tell the King that it is he, and not I, who is giving this man to drink. By Arab custom, Baha al-Din explains, a captive who had drunk from his captor was spared. Saladin had given Reynald nothing. He reminded Reynald of his words to the caravan, and struck him with his sword. His men finished him off. To the frightened king, he said: It is not the wont of kings to kill kings; but that man had transgressed all bounds. Imad al-Din saw the prisoners tied in thirties or forties to a single rope, each string led by one horseman. Both Imad al-Din and Ibn al-Athir say Saladin paid fifty dinars for each captured Templar and Hospitaller, and had them beheaded. Terricus, a Templar who escaped, wrote soon after of two hundred and thirty brothers beheaded, as we verily believe.

Did you know? Abu Shama, born some fifteen years after Hattin, was told of a poor ascetic with the army who exchanged his share, one prisoner, for a pair of sandals, so that people would say the Christian captives were so many and so cheap that one was sold for sandals. A story told a generation later, with no informant named (Recueil des historiens des croisades, Historiens orientaux IV, 1898, p. 289; our rendering).

What became of the True Cross?

And the Cross? Ernoul tells of a Templar who came, years later, to the lord of Acre. He said he had buried the Cross with his own hands, during the rout. They dug for three nights, and found nothing. But Baha al-Din, by then at Saladin's side, writes that in 1191 the Cross was shown to the envoys of Richard the Lionheart. They prostrated themselves on the ground till their faces were covered with dust. Saladin kept it. What became of it after that, we do not know.

Did you know? Saladin refused the Cross to the Byzantine ambassador in 1192, and, Baha al-Din adds, "it is said that the King of the Georgians offered two hundred thousand gold pieces to obtain possession of the cross, and that his proposal was refused" (tr. C. R. Conder, 1897, pp. 334 to 335).

What the ground still holds

About a year later, Ibn al-Athir crossed the field. He saw the land all covered with their bones. Where the march crossed the watershed, Rafael Lewis reports broken weapons, armour and dress fittings: signs of close fighting. On the southern summit of the Horns of Hattin, Zvi Gal excavated what is left of Saladin's Dome of Victory. A few layers of stone.

Did you know? Ibn al-Athir saw the bones from a distance, "lying in heaps or scattered around", and those, he says, were what was left after all the rest had been carried away by storms or by the wild beasts of the hills and valleys (tr. E. J. Costello, in F. Gabrieli, Arab Historians of the Crusades, 1969, p. 125).

Who was to blame?

On the 2nd of October, 1187, Jerusalem surrendered to Saladin. So who was to blame? The Latin writer blames the king. Ernoul blames the Templar Master, and then Raymond. An English chronicler calls Raymond the designer of this treachery. Rafael Lewis is more careful. Whether the trap was chance or Saladin's tactical genius is not known. But the land played into Saladin's hands.

Over to you

Raymond of Tripoli: a traitor, or the one man at the council who was right? And the Cross: do you believe the Templar who said he buried it, or the envoys who knelt in front of it?

Quick answers

What happened at the Battle of Hattin in 1187?

On the 4th of July 1187, in Galilee, the kingdom of Jerusalem lost its army, its king and the relic it carried into battle, the True Cross. The day before, the Franks had left the springs of Sepphoris and marched into dry hills; they camped, says a Latin writer of the time, at a dry halting place, and Ernoul writes that not a man, nor horse, nor beast drank all night. Saladin's men set fire to the dry grass. The foot soldiers broke and ran up a hill, Raymond of Tripoli charged through Saladin's ranks and rode to Tyre, the Cross was taken, and the king's last knights held a hill near Hattin around his tent until it fell. On the 2nd of October 1187, Jerusalem surrendered to Saladin.

Why did the crusaders leave the springs of Sepphoris and march to Hattin?

The sources disagree. Ibn al-Athir says Saladin besieged Tiberias for one reason: to make the Franks leave their position and fight. In Ernoul's Old French chronicle, Raymond of Tripoli, whose wife was trapped in the citadel of Tiberias, told King Guy at the night council of the 2nd of July to let the town fall: between here and Tiberias there is no water, except one little spring. The barons sided with Raymond, but near midnight the Master of the Templars, Gerard of Ridefort, came to the king: Sire, do you believe this traitor? The king did not dare refuse him. But another version of the chronicle has Raymond himself urge the king to go. Who said what that night, we cannot know.

How much water was there on the march to Hattin?

Near the village of Turan the army probably passed one small spring. In 1989 it was measured for the historian Benjamin Kedar: 180 litres an hour. One spring at Sepphoris, measured in 1949, gave 108,000. These are modern measurements, not figures from 1187. In the Latin account, Raymond begged the king to push on to the water, and the king ordered the tents pitched; Ernoul tells it the other way round, with Raymond advising the halt.

What happened to the True Cross after the Battle of Hattin?

The bishop of Acre, who carried it, was mortally wounded, and the Cross was taken. Imad al-Din, Saladin's secretary, who rode with the army, wrote that its capture was more important in the Franks' eyes than that of their king. Ernoul tells of a Templar who came years later and said he had buried it with his own hands during the rout; they dug for three nights and found nothing. But Baha al-Din, by then at Saladin's side, writes that in 1191 the Cross was shown in Saladin's camp to the envoys of Richard the Lionheart, who prostrated themselves on the ground. Saladin kept it. What became of it after that, we do not know.

Who was to blame for the defeat at Hattin?

The sources, Christian and Muslim, blame different men. The Latin writer of the Libellus blames the king. Ernoul blames the Templar Master, and then Raymond of Tripoli. An English chronicler, Roger of Howden, calls Raymond the designer of this treachery, and Baha al-Din calls Raymond the most intelligent man of his race, who saw disaster coming and saved himself. The archaeologist Rafael Lewis is more careful: whether the trap was chance or Saladin's tactical genius is not known, but the land played into Saladin's hands.

Where it happened

  • Horns of Hattin, above the Sea of Galilee (A.D. 1187): The double summit near the village of Hattin where the kingdom of Jerusalem lost its army, its king and the True Cross on 4 July 1187. The last knights held 'a hill near Hattin' around the king's tent (Ibn al-Athir); exactly where the lines stood is not known. Saladin's Dome of Victory stood on the southern summit.
  • Springs of Sepphoris (Zippori) (A.D. 1187): The Frankish camp before the march; in 2021 archaeologists reported a crusader camp at the springs, where Frankish armies gathered more than once. The army left on Friday, 3 July 1187.
  • Tiberias, on the Sea of Galilee (A.D. 1187): Stormed by Saladin on 2 July 1187; only the citadel held out, with the wife of Raymond of Tripoli and her children inside.
  • Turan (A.D. 1187): The village near the one small spring the army passed on 3 July; measured in 1989 for Benjamin Kedar at 180 litres an hour.
  • Kerak castle, beyond the Dead Sea (A.D. 1187): Castle of Reynald of Chatillon, who attacked a caravan coming from Egypt during a truce, probably early in 1187. Where the caravan was attacked is not known.
  • Tyre (A.D. 1187): Where Raymond of Tripoli rode after his charge through Saladin's ranks, says Ernoul.
  • Acre (A.D. 1191): Its bishop carried the True Cross at Hattin and was mortally wounded. Years later, says Ernoul, a Templar came to the lord of Acre and said he had buried the Cross; in 1191 two of Richard the Lionheart's officers came out from Acre to Saladin's camp, where the Cross was shown to them (Baha al-Din).
  • Jerusalem (A.D. 1187): The True Cross was brought from here to the army; the city surrendered to Saladin on 2 October 1187.

What we did NOT use, and why

  • The film *Kingdom of Heaven* (2005) and games: not sources.
  • Matthew Paris's drawing of Saladin taking the Cross (England, about sixty years later): not evidence of the event; if shown at all, only

    • labelled as a later English picture.

  • Conder's own footnotes in the 1897 Baha al-Din (dates in brackets, "killed soon after by the Assassins"): his, not Baha al-Din's, and partly wrong.
  • Roger of Howden's army sizes ("eight hundred thousand men or more"): impossible.
  • The claim that Reynald attacked a caravan carrying Saladin's sister: not found in the sources we read.
  • Gabrieli's "five hundred" knights with the king: the Arabic and the 1872 French say about 150; not used.
  • Gabrieli's "fires of Hell" in Reynald's retort: not in the Arabic ("fire is not harmed by much firewood"); not used.
  • The name of Raymond's wife (Eschiva): found only on Wikipedia in our reading; the script says "the wife of Raymond".

    Transcript

    Read the full narration

    Hattin. Where the True Cross was lost. "Be quiet; we shall not have beaten them until that tent falls!" A sultan says that to his son, on a battlefield in Galilee, on the 4th of July, 1187. Twice, the Christian king had charged down from a hill. Twice, the son, al Afdal, had cried out that they had won. As his father spoke, the tent fell. Saladin dismounted, bowed to the ground in thanks to God, and wept for joy. The historian Ibn al Athir was told this as al Afdal's memory of his first battle. That tent was the king of Jerusalem's. When it fell, his kingdom had lost its army, its king, and the relic they carried into battle: the True Cross. How did an army camped beside good springs end up dying of thirst on a dry hill? And who was to blame? The sources, Christian and Muslim, blame different men. It began with a caravan. Probably early in 1187, Reynald of Chatillon, lord of the castle of Kerak, beyond the Dead Sea, attacked a caravan coming from Egypt, during a truce. They pleaded the truce. Baha al Din, later in Saladin's service, gives his answer: "Ask your Muhammad to deliver you." Saladin swore to kill him with his own hand. On the 2nd of July, he stormed the town of Tiberias, on the Sea of Galilee. Only the citadel held out. Inside were the wife of Raymond, count of Tripoli, and her children. Ibn al Athir says Saladin besieged Tiberias for one reason: to make the Franks leave their position and fight. That position was the springs of Sepphoris, about thirty kilometres to the west. A Latin writer of the time counts 1,200 knights, and 18,000 foot soldiers or more. The Old French chronicle of Ernoul says many were paid from the English king's treasure, and carried banners with his arms. In 2021, archaeologists reported a crusader camp at those springs, where Frankish armies gathered more than once. Most of the metal they found was horseshoe nails, many of them broken. The archaeologist Rafael Lewis reads them as farriers at work, shoeing warhorses before battle. And from Jerusalem they had brought the True Cross, believed to hold a piece of the cross of Christ. On the evening of the 2nd of July, King Guy called his barons. Raymond, whose wife was trapped in that citadel, told the king to let the town fall. In our rendering of Ernoul: "Tiberias is mine, and my wife and my goods are in it, and no one will lose as much as I, if it is lost." "Between here and Tiberias there is no water, except one little spring ... That is nothing for an army." The Master of the Templars, Gerard of Ridefort, said there was still some wolf's hair in that advice. The barons sided with Raymond. But near midnight, when the king had eaten, the Master came to him. "Sire, do you believe this traitor ... ? ... have it cried through the army that all arm ... and follow the banner of the Holy Cross." The king did not dare refuse him, says Ernoul. The Master had made him king. But another version of the chronicle has Raymond himself urge the king to go. Who said what that night, we cannot know. On Friday, the 3rd of July, the army left the springs. Saladin's men shot at them all day. By the middle of the afternoon, says Ernoul, they were only halfway to Tiberias. Near the village of Turan they probably passed one small spring. In 1989 it was measured for the historian Benjamin Kedar: 180 litres an hour. One spring at Sepphoris, measured in 1949, gave 108,000. In the Latin account, Raymond begs the king to push on to the water. The king orders the tents pitched. Seeing the tents go up, Raymond says, in our rendering: "Alas! alas! Lord God, the war is over, we are handed over to death, and the land is destroyed." Ernoul tells it the other way round. There, it was Raymond who advised the halt. They camped, says the Latin writer, at a dry halting place, where more blood was shed that night than water. Ernoul: not a man, nor horse, nor beast drank all night. Saladin's men set fire to the dry grass. When, the sources disagree. Ibn al Athir writes that the wind carried the heat and smoke onto the Franks. The foot soldiers broke, and ran up a hill. The king and the bishops sent to ask them to defend the Cross. Their answer, in our rendering of the Latin: We are not coming, because we are dead with thirst and cannot fight. Raymond charged. Saladin's men opened their ranks, let him through, and closed again behind him. He did not turn back. Tiberias, with his wife inside, was two leagues away. He rode to Tyre. Traitor, or the only man who saw it coming? Baha al Din calls him the most intelligent man of his race, who saw disaster coming and saved himself. The bishop of Acre, who carried the Cross, was mortally wounded, and the Cross was taken. Imad al Din, Saladin's secretary, who rode with the army, wrote, in our rendering: "The capture of this cross is more important in their eyes than that of their king." The king's last knights held a hill near Hattin, around his tent. They charged, and charged again. Then, Ibn al Athir writes, they dismounted, and sat down on the ground. And the tent fell. Baha al Din, who heard it from others, says the king was given a bowl of sherbet made with iced rose water. Guy drank, and passed the bowl to Reynald. Saladin said to the interpreter: Tell the King that it is he, and not I, who is giving this man to drink. By Arab custom, Baha al Din explains, a captive who had drunk from his captor was spared. Saladin had given Reynald nothing. He reminded Reynald of his words to the caravan, and struck him with his sword. His men finished him off. To the frightened king, he said: It is not the wont of kings to kill kings; but that man had transgressed all bounds. Imad al Din saw the prisoners tied in thirties or forties to a single rope, each string led by one horseman. Both Imad al Din and Ibn al Athir say Saladin paid fifty dinars for each captured Templar and Hospitaller, and had them beheaded. Terricus, a Templar who escaped, wrote soon after of two hundred and thirty brothers beheaded, as we verily believe. And the Cross? Ernoul tells of a Templar who came, years later, to the lord of Acre. He said he had buried the Cross with his own hands, during the rout. They dug for three nights, and found nothing. But Baha al Din, by then at Saladin's side, writes that in 1191 the Cross was shown to the envoys of Richard the Lionheart. They prostrated themselves on the ground till their faces were covered with dust. Saladin kept it. What became of it after that, we do not know. About a year later, Ibn al Athir crossed the field. He saw the land all covered with their bones. Where the march crossed the watershed, Rafael Lewis reports broken weapons, armour and dress fittings: signs of close fighting. On the southern summit of the Horns of Hattin, Zvi Gal excavated what is left of Saladin's Dome of Victory. A few layers of stone. On the 2nd of October, 1187, Jerusalem surrendered to Saladin. So who was to blame? The Latin writer blames the king. Ernoul blames the Templar Master, and then Raymond. An English chronicler calls Raymond the designer of this treachery. Rafael Lewis is more careful. Whether the trap was chance or Saladin's tactical genius is not known. But the land played into Saladin's hands. Two questions for you. Raymond of Tripoli: a traitor, or the one man at the council who was right? And the Cross: do you believe the Templar who said he buried it, or the envoys who knelt in front of it?
