The Mystery of Rome's Lost Legion

Around A.D. 108, the Ninth Legion put its name on a great stone at York. It is the last dated trace of that legion anywhere in Britain. About fourteen years later, another legion had come to Britain in its place, and no ancient writer we have tells us what became of the Ninth. A famous novel says it marched into Scotland and never came back. But its tiles turned up in the Netherlands. So did it die in Britain, far away in the East, or did it die at all?

We will look at the stone, the old texts, the finds on the Rhine, the wars in the East, and the argument that still runs today.

An unlucky legion

To see why the silence matters, start with the legion itself. A legion was a big unit of Roman citizen soldiers, with its own number, its own name, and an eagle as its emblem. The Ninth was called Hispana, the Spanish legion, and it had bad luck in Britain. During Boudica's revolt, its commander, Petillius Cerialis, marched to the rescue of a Roman town. Tacitus writes that the Britons routed the legion and slaughtered the infantry to a man. Cerialis escaped with the cavalry. Rome filled the gaps with 2,000 legionaries sent over from Germany. Years later, far north in Scotland, under the governor Agricola, the Caledonians picked the Ninth for a night attack. In Tacitus's words, they “attacked by night the ninth Legion, as being the weakest, and cutting down the sentries … broke into the camp”. Agricola's men came up behind the attackers at daybreak, and saved it. By then the Ninth had its own fortress, at York, built between A.D. 71 and 74.

Did you know? After Boudica's revolt, Rome filled the gaps in the Ninth with two thousand legionaries sent over from Germany (Tacitus, Annals 14.38).

The York inscription

And that fortress is where its trail in Britain ends. In 1854, a great inscribed slab came up from 7 metres below King's Square in York, a few metres from where a Roman gate had stood. It names the emperor Trajan, with all his titles, and it ends: “by the agency of the Ninth Legion Hispana”. Trajan's titles date it to A.D. 107 or 108, when the fortress was being rebuilt in stone. The legion's stamped tiles turn up across the north, at York, Malton, Aldborough and Carlisle. But after this stone, not one dated record of the Ninth has been found in Britain.

Did you know? The York slab came up in 1854 from seven metres below King's Square, a few metres from where a gate of the Roman fortress had stood. It is the last dated record of the Ninth in Britain.

The case for Britain

For a long time, the answer seemed obvious. In 1885, the great German historian Theodor Mommsen took that York stone as his evidence, and drew a dark conclusion. “Under Hadrian”, he wrote, “a severe disaster occurred here, to all appearance a sudden attack on the camp of Eburacum [York], and the annihilation of the legion stationed there”. His chief proof was a silence. The Ninth disappears, and the Sixth Legion takes its place. But he also had two old lines. Around the year 162, the orator Fronto wrote to the emperor Marcus Aurelius about Rome's old defeats. “Again under the rule of your grandfather Hadrian what a number of soldiers were killed by the Jews, what a number by the Britons!” And the Historia Augusta, a collection of emperors' lives that is not always to be trusted, says that when Hadrian came to power, the Britons “could not be kept under Roman sway”. Then, in 1997, at the fort of Vindolanda near Hadrian's Wall, excavators found part of a tombstone. It names Titus Annius, a centurion of the First Cohort of Tungrians, “killed in war”. He was not in the Ninth, and the stone does not say which war. The historian Anthony Birley linked it to the fighting in Britain when Hadrian came to power, and the stone's editors call that likely.

Did you know? A tombstone found at Vindolanda in 1997 names a centurion, Titus Annius, "killed in war". He was not in the Ninth, and the stone does not say which war; the historian Anthony Birley linked it to the fighting in Britain when Hadrian came to power.

The novel and the Silchester eagle

Then came the story that everyone knows. In 1954, Rosemary Sutcliff's novel, The Eagle of the Ninth, gave the legion its famous ending, lost in the north with its eagle. In 2011, the film The Eagle told it again. Sutcliff's spark was a real bird. In 1866, the Reverend Joyce, digging at the Roman town of Silchester, found a bronze eagle with its wings missing. He imagined a standard bearer at a last stand, who wrenched off its wings and hid it in the wooden ceiling. But real legion eagles were made of silver or gold. In 1957, the archaeologist George Boon suggested it more likely came from a statue of Jupiter or of an emperor. That view has held ever since. The eagle behind the legend was probably never carried by a legion at all.

Tiles in the Netherlands

And the legion itself may not have died in Britain either. Since the 1960s, archaeologists have published tiles of the Ninth from the Roman fortress at Nijmegen, on the Rhine frontier. A bronze object with the legion's name, now in the museum at Nijmegen, is dated to about A.D. 125. Then there are its officers. One of them, Lucius Aemilius Karus, later governed the province of Arabia. The historian Jona Lendering says he cannot have served in the Ninth before A.D. 122, and if he is right, the Ninth outlived the York stone by years. That same year, the Sixth Legion arrived in Britain from the Rhine, and Hadrian came to start his wall. At Newcastle, the Sixth set up two altars on the new bridge over the Tyne. One reads: To Neptune, the Sixth Legion. The other: To Ocean. Both were found in the river, at the Swing Bridge, in 1875 and 1903. One legion left the Rhine for Britain. Did the other go the opposite way? Lendering asks just that: did they trade places?

Did you know? The legion that came to Britain in 122, the Sixth, set up two altars on the new bridge over the Tyne at Newcastle, one to Neptune and one to Ocean. Both were found in the river at the Swing Bridge, in 1875 and 1903.

Lost in the East?

If the Ninth did leave Britain, where did it end? Some scholars have looked east. In A.D. 132, Judaea rose against Rome, in the war of Bar Kokhba. The historian Cassius Dio, a Roman senator who wrote in the next century, says, in the summary of his work that survives, that many Romans perished in that war. So many that Hadrian, writing to the Senate, left out the usual opening line of an emperor: “I and the legions are in health.” Dio names no legion in that war. About thirty years later, a Parthian king trapped a Roman legion at Elegeia, in Armenia, and, Dio says, destroyed “the whole force, leaders and all”. That is the disaster Fronto's letter was written to soften. Again, Dio does not say which legion it was. And Dio himself lists the legions that still existed in his own day. The Ninth is not among them.

Did you know? After the heavy losses of the war in Judaea, Cassius Dio says, Hadrian left out the usual opening line of an emperor's letter to the Senate: "I and the legions are in health." Dio names no legion in that war.

Did you know? Cassius Dio lists the legions that still existed in his own day, and the Ninth is not among them.

The argument today

So the argument has swung back towards Britain. In 2021, the archaeologist Nick Hodgson went through the evidence again, in the journal Britannia. He concluded that only a detachment of the Ninth went abroad, from about A.D. 105 to 120, and that nothing proves the legion existed after the early 120s. He points instead to war in Britain, heavy Roman losses, and a pause in the building of the Wall, probably in A.D. 122 or soon after. Most likely, he argues, the Ninth was defeated and disbanded in those events. In 2025, Erik Graafstal, in the same journal, asked whether a major crisis broke out after Hadrian's visit, and whether Rome hushed up a shameful episode. The archaeologist Miles Russell has even asked whether an ambush like that is what drew the border between England and Scotland. Not everyone agrees: for others, the tiles, the bronze and the officers' careers keep the eastern endings alive.

What would settle it

So here is what we really have: a dated stone at York, tiles in the Netherlands, a few officers, two old lines about Hadrian's wars, and a legion missing from Dio's list. What would settle it is one more stone: a building inscription with an emperor's date, or a soldier's tombstone that names the Ninth after the 120s, in Britain or in the East. No part of this needs a curse or ghosts in the mist. It needs one more piece of evidence, and nobody has found it yet.

Verdict

My verdict: Britain, in the early 120s, is the strongest case we have, but not a proven one. Where its men died, or whether they died at all, nobody knows.

Quick answers

What happened to the Ninth Legion?

Nobody knows for certain. Around A.D. 108 the Ninth Legion put its name on a great stone at York, the last dated trace of that legion anywhere in Britain, and no ancient writer we have tells us what became of it. In 2021 the archaeologist Nick Hodgson concluded that nothing proves the legion existed after the early 120s and argued that it was most likely defeated and disbanded in a war in Britain. Not everyone agrees: for others, the tiles, the bronze and the officers' careers keep the eastern endings alive.

Was the Ninth Legion destroyed in Scotland?

That is the famous ending of Rosemary Sutcliff's novel The Eagle of the Ninth (1954), told again in the film The Eagle in 2011: the legion lost in the north with its eagle. No ancient writer we have tells it. In 1885 Theodor Mommsen thought the legion was annihilated in a sudden attack on the camp at York, but his chief proof was a silence: the Ninth disappears from the record, and the Sixth Legion takes its place.

What is the last record of the Ninth Legion in Britain?

A great inscribed slab found in 1854, seven metres below King's Square in York (RIB 665). It names the emperor Trajan with all his titles and ends: by the agency of the Ninth Legion Hispana. Trajan's titles date it to A.D. 107 or 108, when the fortress was being rebuilt in stone. After this stone, not one dated record of the Ninth has been found in Britain.

Did the Ninth Legion leave Britain?

Perhaps, or part of it. Since the 1960s archaeologists have published tiles of the Ninth from the Roman fortress at Nijmegen, on the Rhine frontier, and a bronze object with the legion's name, now in the museum at Nijmegen, is dated to about A.D. 125. The historian Jona Lendering asks whether the Ninth and the Sixth traded places. Nick Hodgson concluded that only a detachment of the Ninth went abroad, from about A.D. 105 to 120.

Was the Silchester eagle the Ninth Legion's eagle?

Probably not. The Reverend Joyce found the wingless bronze eagle at Silchester in 1866 and imagined a standard bearer at a last stand who hid it; it was Sutcliff's spark. But real legion eagles were made of silver or gold, and in 1957 the archaeologist George Boon suggested it more likely came from a statue of Jupiter or of an emperor. That view has held ever since.

Where it happened

  • York (Eboracum), England (A.D. 108): The Ninth's fortress, built A.D. 71 to 74 (City of York HER). The slab RIB 665, dated A.D. 107/108 by Trajan's titles, came up in 1854 from seven metres below King's Square; the last dated record of the Ninth in Britain.
  • Nijmegen, Netherlands (A.D. 125): Tiles of the Ninth from the Roman fortress, published since the 1960s; a bronze inscribed LEG HISP IX from Ewijk nearby, dated about A.D. 125, is in Museum Het Valkhof.
  • Vindolanda, near Hadrian's Wall, England (A.D. 1997): The tombstone of Titus Annius, centurion of the First Cohort of Tungrians, "killed in war" (RIB 3364), was found here in 1997. The stone does not say which war.
  • Silchester (Calleva Atrebatum), England (A.D. 1866): The Reverend J. G. Joyce found the wingless bronze eagle here in 1866; it inspired The Eagle of the Ninth. George Boon (1957) thought it more likely came from a statue of Jupiter or of an emperor.
  • Newcastle upon Tyne, the Swing Bridge, England (A.D. 122): The Sixth Legion's altars to Neptune and to Ocean (RIB 1319 and 1320) from the bridge over the Tyne were found in the river at the Swing Bridge in 1875 and 1903.
  • Elegeia, Armenia (site uncertain) (A.D. 162): Where, Dio says, a Parthian king destroyed a Roman legion, "the whole force, leaders and all", about thirty years after the war in Judaea. The site is not securely located: the point is only a marker on the Erzurum plain.

About fourteen years later, another legion had come to Britain in its place, and no ancient writer we have tells us what became of the Ninth. A famous novel says it marched into Scotland and never came back. But its tiles turned up in the Netherlands. So did it die in Britain, far away in the East, or did it die at all? We will look at the stone, the old texts, the finds on the Rhine, the wars in the East, and the argument that still runs today. To see why the silence matters, start with the legion itself. A legion was a big unit of Roman citizen soldiers, with its own number, its own name, and an eagle as its emblem. The Ninth was called Hispana, the Spanish legion, and it had bad luck in Britain. During Boudica's revolt, its commander, Petillius Cerialis, marched to the rescue of a Roman town. Tacitus writes that the Britons routed the legion and slaughtered the infantry to a man. Cerialis escaped with the cavalry. Rome filled the gaps with 2,000 legionaries sent over from Germany. Years later, far north in Scotland, under the governor Agricola, the Caledonians picked the Ninth for a night attack. In Tacitus's words, they attacked by night the ninth Legion, as being the weakest, and cutting down the sentries ... they broke into the camp. It names the emperor Trajan, with all his titles, and it ends: by the agency of the Ninth Legion Hispana. Trajan's titles date it to A.D. 107 or 108, when the fortress was being rebuilt in stone. The legion's stamped tiles turn up across the north, at York, Malton, Aldborough and Carlisle. But after this stone, not one dated record of the Ninth has been found in Britain. For a long time, the answer seemed obvious. In 1885, the great German historian Theodor Mommsen took that York stone as his evidence, and drew a dark conclusion. Under Hadrian, he wrote, a severe disaster occurred here, to all appearance a sudden attack on the camp of Eburacum [York], and the annihilation of the legion stationed there. His chief proof was a silence. The Ninth disappears, and the Sixth Legion takes its place. But he also had two old lines. Around the year 162, the orator Fronto wrote to the emperor Marcus Aurelius about Rome's old defeats. Under the rule of your grandfather Hadrian, what a number of soldiers were killed by the Jews, what a number by the Britons! And the Historia Augusta, a collection of emperors' lives that is not always to be trusted, says that when Hadrian came to power, the Britons could not be kept under Roman sway. Then, in 1997, at the fort of Vindolanda near Hadrian's Wall, excavators found part of a tombstone. It names Titus Annius, a centurion of the First Cohort of Tungrians, killed in war. He was not in the Ninth, and the stone does not say which war. The historian Anthony Birley linked it to the fighting in Britain when Hadrian came to power, and the stone's editors call that likely. Then came the story that everyone knows. In 1954, Rosemary Sutcliff's novel, The Eagle of the Ninth, gave the legion its famous ending, lost in the north with its eagle. In 2011, the film The Eagle told it again. Sutcliff's spark was a real bird. In 1866, the Reverend Joyce, digging at the Roman town of Silchester, found a bronze eagle with its wings missing. He imagined a standard bearer at a last stand, who wrenched off its wings and hid it in the wooden ceiling. But real legion eagles were made of silver or gold. In 1957, the archaeologist George Boon suggested it more likely came from a statue of Jupiter or of an emperor. That view has held ever since. The eagle behind the legend was probably never carried by a legion at all. If you are enjoying this type of content, please don't forget to subscribe. And the legion itself may not have died in Britain either. Since the nineteen sixties, archaeologists have published tiles of the Ninth from the Roman fortress at Nijmegen, on the Rhine frontier. A bronze object with the legion's name, now in the museum at Nijmegen, is dated to about A.D. 125. Then there are its officers. One of them, Lucius Aemilius Karus, later governed the province of Arabia. The historian Jona Lendering says he cannot have served in the Ninth before A.D. 122, and if he is right, the Ninth outlived the York stone by years. That same year, the Sixth Legion arrived in Britain from the Rhine, and Hadrian came to start his wall. At Newcastle, the Sixth set up two altars on the new bridge over the Tyne. One reads: To Neptune, the Sixth Legion. The other: To Ocean. Both were found in the river, at the Swing Bridge, in 1875 and 1903. One legion left the Rhine for Britain. Did the other go the opposite way? Lendering asks just that: did they trade places? If the Ninth did leave Britain, where did it end? Some scholars have looked east. In A.D. 132, Judaea rose against Rome, in the war of Bar Kokhba. The historian Cassius Dio, a Roman senator who wrote in the next century, says, in the summary of his work that survives, that many Romans perished in that war. So many that Hadrian, writing to the Senate, left out the usual opening line of an emperor: I and the legions are in health. Dio names no legion in that war. About thirty years later, a Parthian king trapped a Roman legion at Elegeia, in Armenia, and, Dio says, destroyed the whole force, leaders and all. That is the disaster Fronto's letter was written to soften. Again, Dio does not say which legion it was. And Dio himself lists the legions that still existed in his own day. The Ninth is not among them. So the argument has swung back towards Britain. In 2021, the archaeologist Nick Hodgson went through the evidence again, in the journal Britannia. He concluded that only a detachment of the Ninth went abroad, from about A.D. 105 to 120, and that nothing proves the legion existed after the early 120s. He points instead to war in Britain, heavy Roman losses, and a pause in the building of the Wall, probably in A.D. 122 or soon after. Most likely, he argues, the Ninth was defeated and disbanded in those events. In 2025, Erik Graafstal, in the same journal, asked whether a major crisis broke out after Hadrian's visit, and whether Rome hushed up a shameful episode. The archaeologist Miles Russell has even asked whether an ambush like that is what drew the border between England and Scotland. Not everyone agrees: for others, the tiles, the bronze and the officers' careers keep the eastern endings alive. So here is what we really have: a dated stone at York, tiles in the Netherlands, a few officers, two old lines about Hadrian's wars, and a legion missing from Dio's list. What would settle it is one more stone: a building inscription with an emperor's date, or a soldier's tombstone that names the Ninth after the 120s, in Britain or in the East. No part of this needs a curse or ghosts in the mist. It needs one more piece of evidence, and nobody has found it yet. My verdict: Britain, in the early 120s, is the strongest case we have, but not a proven one. Where its men died, or whether they died at all, nobody knows. If Rome lost a whole legion, do you think it would have kept quiet about it? And which ending do you find more believable: an ambush in the north of Britain, or a lost war in the East?
