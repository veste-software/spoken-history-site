Mongol Invasion of Japan: What Sank Kublai Khan's Fleet?

One of the most famous pictures of the Mongol invasion of Japan shows a bomb, bursting beside a samurai's bleeding horse. The historian Thomas Conlan has examined the original scroll. He argues that the bomb was painted in later, with poorer ink. But the real bombs are on the seabed. Off the island of Takashima, they have been raised from Kublai Khan's lost fleet: ceramic balls, packed with scrap iron and gunpowder. Their bottoms were made flat, notes the archaeologist Randall Sasaki, so they would not roll around on deck.

Did you know? The ceramic bombs raised off Takashima had flat bottoms. The archaeologist Randall Sasaki notes that they were made that way so they would not roll around on deck (Sasaki, The Origin of the Lost Fleet of the Mongol Empire, MA thesis, Texas A&M University, 2008).

So what really sank Kublai Khan's fleet? The story says a divine wind, a kamikaze, saved Japan twice. Let's check the texts, the scroll, and the wrecks.

Kublai's letter to Japan

It begins with a letter. In 1266, Kublai sent envoys with a letter to the King of Japan. He asked for friendly contact. And he ended, in our rendering: as for going so far as to use weapons, who would like that? Let the King consider it. No answer came. Envoys went again and again, for years. Turned away at the island of Tsushima, one party seized two Japanese men. Kublai sent them home. Still no reply.

1274: one samurai at Hakata Bay

So in 1274, a fleet sailed from Korea. The official Yuan history counts 900 ships and 15,000 soldiers. The History of Goryeo, Korea's official history, gives more: 25,000 Mongol and Chinese troops, 8,000 Koreans, and 6,700 sailors. Both were compiled long after the events, the Korean one in 1451. The fleet struck the islands of Tsushima and Iki, and landed at Hakata Bay, in Kyushu. Among the warriors waiting there was Takezaki Suenaga. His land dispute was unsettled, he says, so he had only five horsemen. His retainer begged him to wait for a witness. Suenaga answered: the way of the bow and arrow is to do what is worthy of reward. Charge! His horse was shot, and he was wounded. Another warrior's charge saved him, and the two agreed to be each other's witness. That charge is the scene in his scroll with the bomb.

Was there a storm in 1274?

And then the fleet was gone. By the morning of the 21st, says a shrine text written much later, not one ship was left. Why? Here the sources split. The Yuan history has no storm. In our rendering: the army was in disorder, and its arrows were used up. They only plundered the borderlands, and returned. The Korean history says the commanders argued. The Mongol general would not drive tired troops against an enemy that grew every day. So they sailed for home. Then, it says, a great storm of wind and rain came in the night. Ships struck the rocks, and many were wrecked. In Kyoto, a courtier heard that a contrary wind had blown them home. But his own daily weather notes, says the meteorologist Mizukoshi Mitsuharu, show a strong wind, then heavy frost: the winter monsoon, not typhoon weather. The historian Hattori Hideo still argues for a typhoon, after seven days in Japan. Kakehi Masahiro thinks it was a reconnaissance from the start. So, for 1274: perhaps a storm on the way home. But in the Korean history, it comes after the decision to leave.

Did you know? The same Kyoto courtier who heard that a contrary wind had blown the fleet home also kept daily weather notes. They record a strong wind, then heavy frost: the winter monsoon, says the meteorologist Mizukoshi Mitsuharu, not typhoon weather (Tenki 56, 2009).

A reward, and a wall

And Suenaga? He had fought, and been seen. But no reward came. So he sold his horse and saddle to pay for the journey, and set out for Kamakura, the seat of the shogunate. Not one relative saw him off. Officials ignored him. At last he spoke to Adachi Yasumori, who heard appeals, and staked his own head on his story. He got his reward: land. Years later he wrote that more than a hundred men were praised, but only he received a grant, and a horse. His scrolls record exactly that: deeds, witnesses, reward. Meanwhile, from 1276, warriors of every province of Kyushu built walls along the bays. Along Hakata Bay, Fukuoka City puts their total length at 20 kilometres. Parts can still be seen in the city today.

Did you know? Suenaga sold his horse and saddle to pay for his journey to Kamakura. Years later he wrote that more than a hundred men were praised, but only he received a grant, and a horse (Suenaga's text, in Thomas Conlan's translation).

1281: two fleets

In 1281, Kublai sent two fleets: one from Korea, one from south China. Before they left, he told his generals, in our rendering: there is one more thing that truly worries me. I fear you will not get along. The Yuan history says 100,000 men. The Korean history gives 10,000 Korean soldiers and 15,000 sailors, plus more than 100,000 troops from the south. The southern ships: 3,500 in one passage, 9,000 in another. Conlan thinks the invasions were much smaller than is commonly assumed. The real numbers, we do not know. The southern fleet sailed about six weeks after the eastern one. At Hakata Bay, the wall kept the eastern army off the beach. Samurai attacked the invaders' ships from small boats. Suenaga talked his way onto one without his helmet. So he tied two shin guards together, and wore them on his head. Disease broke out in the army. The Korean history counts over 3,000 dead. The generals complained of rotting ships and failing food.

Did you know? Suenaga joined the attack on the invaders' ships without his helmet, so he tied two shin guards together and wore them on his head, and his scroll shows it (Suenaga's text and Conlan's guided tour of the scrolls, Princeton).

The typhoon at Takashima

At last the southern fleet reached Imari Bay, by the island of Takashima. On the first day of the eighth month, by the Chinese calendar, says one survivor, the wind broke the ships. The Korean history says the southern troops all drowned. The bodies came in with the tide, and choked the bay, so that you could walk across it. And the generals? Four days later, the survivor says, they picked the soundest ships for themselves, and left more than a hundred thousand men behind. Japanese warriors hunted them down. The men from south China were spared, and enslaved. Mongols, Koreans and northern Chinese were killed. On Takashima, place names still seem to recall it: Head Cape, Blood Bay, Hell Valley. But the archaeologist Ikeda Yoshifumi reads the Korean history as saying most of the eastern army got home.

Did you know? On Takashima, place names still seem to recall the battle: Head Cape, Blood Bay, Hell Valley (Mochida Joji, interview with Ikeda Yoshifumi, nippon.com, 2024).

The wrecks on the seabed

Now the seabed. Fishermen at Takashima had long pulled up jars and weapons in their nets. But shipworms eat exposed wood. Only wood buried in the mud survives. So Ikeda and his divers probed the mud with iron rods, spot after spot. Shell crunches, he says. Pottery clicks. Wood gives. In 2011, they confirmed a ship, about 27 metres long. In 2015, a second, about 20 metres. Four wooden anchors were found side by side, all pointing south. Ikeda thinks a huge force, like a typhoon, dragged them north and tore them from their ships. Were the lost ships badly built? In 2008, Sasaki found that many timbers came from small boats of the Yangtze River, not built for long sea voyages. It is tempting, he wrote, to say most of the lost ships were river craft. But he called his results tentative. The two wrecks found since have V-shaped or rounded hulls with bulkheads, and are identified as ships of the southern fleet. The question is open. A third wreck was confirmed in 2024. A board from it, perhaps nailed up inside, carries a Yuan date: 1275 or 1276. And in October 2026, near what may be its kitchen, divers found five dice, each about a centimetre across, and 29 small tiles. Ikeda thinks the men who worked there played games.

Did you know? In October 2026 divers found five dice, each about a centimetre across, and 29 small tiles in the third wreck off Takashima, near what may be its kitchen. What the tiles are made of is still to be examined (Matsuura City Board of Education, reported by the Nagasaki Shimbun, 29 October 2026).

Who got the credit?

So who got the credit? The warriors had paid for the war themselves, and there was no conquered land to hand out. But the court gave thanks to the gods. A rumour said a wind god at Ise had sent the gusts. In 1293, the court raised his shrine in rank. Other shrines were raised too. And slowly, the belief spread that a divine wind would protect the country.

Verdict

So what sank Kublai Khan's fleet? In 1281, a real storm, striking an invasion that had been held off the beach for weeks, by a wall, and by men in small boats. In 1274, perhaps a storm on the way home. And the bomb in the picture may be a later addition. But the bombs on the seabed are real. If the southern fleet had arrived on time, would the wall still have held?

Quick answers

What sank Kublai Khan's fleet?

In 1281, a real storm. On the first day of the eighth month, by the Chinese calendar, says one survivor, the wind broke the ships at Imari Bay, by the island of Takashima. But it struck an invasion that had been held off the beach for weeks, by a wall along Hakata Bay and by samurai attacking from small boats, and disease had broken out in the army. In 1274 there was perhaps a storm on the way home, but in the Korean history it comes after the decision to leave.

Was there a storm in 1274?

Here the sources split. The official Yuan history has no storm: in our rendering, the army was in disorder, its arrows were used up, and it returned. The History of Goryeo, Korea's official history, says the commanders argued and sailed for home, and then a great storm of wind and rain came in the night, and many ships were wrecked on the rocks. A courtier in Kyoto heard that a contrary wind had blown them home, but his own daily weather notes, says the meteorologist Mizukoshi Mitsuharu, show a strong wind, then heavy frost: the winter monsoon, not typhoon weather. The historian Hattori Hideo still argues for a typhoon; Kakehi Masahiro thinks it was a reconnaissance from the start.

Did the Mongol fleet carry bombs?

Yes. Off the island of Takashima, bombs have been raised from Kublai Khan's lost fleet: ceramic balls, packed with scrap iron and gunpowder. Their bottoms were made flat, notes the archaeologist Randall Sasaki, so they would not roll around on deck. The famous picture of a bomb bursting beside Takezaki Suenaga's horse in 1274 is another matter: the historian Thomas Conlan, who examined the original scroll, argues that the bomb was painted in later, with poorer ink.

How big was the Mongol invasion fleet?

We do not know. For 1274 the official Yuan history counts 900 ships and 15,000 soldiers; the History of Goryeo gives 25,000 Mongol and Chinese troops, 8,000 Koreans and 6,700 sailors. For 1281 the Yuan history says 100,000 men; the Korean history gives 10,000 Korean soldiers and 15,000 sailors, plus more than 100,000 troops from the south, and for the southern ships 3,500 in one passage and 9,000 in another. Both histories were compiled long after the events, and the historian Thomas Conlan thinks the invasions were much smaller than is commonly assumed.

Where were the wrecks of Kublai Khan's fleet found?

On the seabed off the island of Takashima. Shipworms eat exposed wood, so only wood buried in the mud survives, and the archaeologist Ikeda Yoshifumi and his divers probed the mud with iron rods. They confirmed a ship about 27 metres long in 2011, a second of about 20 metres in 2015, and a third in 2024, with a board that carries a Yuan date, 1275 or 1276. The first two have V-shaped or rounded hulls with bulkheads and are identified as ships of the southern fleet. In October 2026 divers found five dice and 29 small tiles in the third.

Where it happened

  • Hakata Bay, Fukuoka (A.D. 1274): Where the fleet of 1274 landed and Takezaki Suenaga made his charge; in 1281 the wall along the bay kept the eastern army off the beach.
  • Takashima, Imari Bay (A.D. 1281): The southern fleet reached Imari Bay, by the island of Takashima, where the wind broke the ships. Three wrecks were confirmed on the seabed site off the island in 2011, 2015 and 2024; the exact positions are not shown.
  • Imazu, Fukuoka (A.D. 1276): One of the places where parts of the walls built from 1276 can still be seen today. Fukuoka City puts the total length along Hakata Bay at 20 kilometres.
  • Tsushima (A.D. 1274): Envoys were turned away here, and one party seized two Japanese men; in 1274 the fleet struck the island.
  • Iki (A.D. 1274): Struck by the fleet in 1274, on the way to Hakata Bay.
  • Kamakura (A.D. 1275): The seat of the shogunate, where Suenaga appealed to Adachi Yasumori and got his reward.
  • Kyoto (A.D. 1274): Where a courtier heard that a contrary wind had blown the fleet home; his own daily weather notes point to the winter monsoon, says Mizukoshi Mitsuharu.
  • Inner Shrine, Ise (A.D. 1293): A rumour said a wind god at Ise had sent the gusts; in 1293 the court raised his shrine in rank (Kawai 2021).

    The Mongol Invasion. A bomb painted in later?. One of the most famous pictures of the Mongol invasion of Japan shows a bomb, bursting beside a samurai's bleeding horse. The historian Thomas Conlan has examined the original scroll. He argues that the bomb was painted in later, with poorer ink. But the real bombs are on the seabed. Off the island of Takashima, they have been raised from Kublai Khan's lost fleet: ceramic balls, packed with scrap iron and gunpowder. Their bottoms were made flat, notes the archaeologist Randall Sasaki, so they would not roll around on deck. So what really sank Kublai Khan's fleet? The story says a divine wind, a kamikaze, saved Japan twice. Let's check the texts, the scroll, and the wrecks. It begins with a letter. In 1266, Kublai sent envoys with a letter to the King of Japan. He asked for friendly contact. And he ended, in our rendering: as for going so far as to use weapons, who would like that? Let the King consider it. No answer came. Envoys went again and again, for years. Turned away at the island of Tsushima, one party seized two Japanese men. Kublai sent them home. Still no reply. So in 1274, a fleet sailed from Korea. The official Yuan history counts 900 ships and 15,000 soldiers. The History of Goryeo, Korea's official history, gives more: 25,000 Mongol and Chinese troops, 8,000 Koreans, and 6,700 sailors. Both were compiled long after the events, the Korean one in 1451. The fleet struck the islands of Tsushima and Iki, and landed at Hakata Bay, in Kyushu. Among the warriors waiting there was Takezaki Suenaga. His land dispute was unsettled, he says, so he had only five horsemen. His retainer begged him to wait for a witness. Suenaga answered: the way of the bow and arrow is to do what is worthy of reward. Charge! His horse was shot, and he was wounded. Another warrior's charge saved him, and the two agreed to be each other's witness. That charge is the scene in his scroll with the bomb. And then the fleet was gone. By the morning of the 21st, says a shrine text written much later, not one ship was left. Why? Here the sources split. The Yuan history has no storm. In our rendering: the army was in disorder, and its arrows were used up. They only plundered the borderlands, and returned. The Korean history says the commanders argued. The Mongol general would not drive tired troops against an enemy that grew every day. So they sailed for home. Then, it says, a great storm of wind and rain came in the night. Ships struck the rocks, and many were wrecked. In Kyoto, a courtier heard that a contrary wind had blown them home. But his own daily weather notes, says the meteorologist Mizukoshi Mitsuharu, show a strong wind, then heavy frost: the winter monsoon, not typhoon weather. The historian Hattori Hideo still argues for a typhoon, after seven days in Japan. Kakehi Masahiro thinks it was a reconnaissance from the start. So, for 1274: perhaps a storm on the way home. But in the Korean history, it comes after the decision to leave. If you are enjoying this type of content, please don't forget to subscribe. And Suenaga? He had fought, and been seen. But no reward came. So he sold his horse and saddle to pay for the journey, and set out for Kamakura, the seat of the shogunate. Not one relative saw him off. Officials ignored him. At last he spoke to Adachi Yasumori, who heard appeals, and staked his own head on his story. He got his reward: land. Years later he wrote that more than a hundred men were praised, but only he received a grant, and a horse. His scrolls record exactly that: deeds, witnesses, reward. Meanwhile, from 1276, warriors of every province of Kyushu built walls along the bays. Along Hakata Bay, Fukuoka City puts their total length at 20 kilometres. Parts can still be seen in the city today. In 1281, Kublai sent two fleets: one from Korea, one from south China. Before they left, he told his generals, in our rendering: there is one more thing that truly worries me. I fear you will not get along. The Yuan history says 100,000 men. The Korean history gives 10,000 Korean soldiers and 15,000 sailors, plus more than 100,000 troops from the south. The southern ships: 3,500 in one passage, 9,000 in another. Conlan thinks the invasions were much smaller than is commonly assumed. The real numbers, we do not know. The southern fleet sailed about six weeks after the eastern one. At Hakata Bay, the wall kept the eastern army off the beach. Samurai attacked the invaders' ships from small boats. Suenaga talked his way onto one without his helmet. So he tied two shin guards together, and wore them on his head. Disease broke out in the army. The Korean history counts over 3,000 dead. The generals complained of rotting ships and failing food. At last the southern fleet reached Imari Bay, by the island of Takashima. On the first day of the eighth month, by the Chinese calendar, says one survivor, the wind broke the ships. The Korean history says the southern troops all drowned. The bodies came in with the tide, and choked the bay, so that you could walk across it. And the generals? Four days later, the survivor says, they picked the soundest ships for themselves, and left more than a hundred thousand men behind. Japanese warriors hunted them down. The men from south China were spared, and enslaved. Mongols, Koreans and northern Chinese were killed. On Takashima, place names still seem to recall it: Head Cape, Blood Bay, Hell Valley. But the archaeologist Ikeda Yoshifumi reads the Korean history as saying most of the eastern army got home. Now the seabed. Fishermen at Takashima had long pulled up jars and weapons in their nets. But shipworms eat exposed wood. Only wood buried in the mud survives. So Ikeda and his divers probed the mud with iron rods, spot after spot. Shell crunches, he says. Pottery clicks. Wood gives. In 2011, they confirmed a ship, about 27 metres long. In 2015, a second, about 20 metres. Four wooden anchors were found side by side, all pointing south. Ikeda thinks a huge force, like a typhoon, dragged them north and tore them from their ships. Were the lost ships badly built? In 2008, Sasaki found that many timbers came from small boats of the Yangtze River, not built for long sea voyages. It is tempting, he wrote, to say most of the lost ships were river craft. But he called his results tentative. The two wrecks found since have V-shaped or rounded hulls with bulkheads, and are identified as ships of the southern fleet. The question is open. A third wreck was confirmed in 2024. A board from it, perhaps nailed up inside, carries a Yuan date: 1275 or 1276. And in October 2026, near what may be its kitchen, divers found five dice, each about a centimetre across, and 29 small tiles. Ikeda thinks the men who worked there played games. So who got the credit? The warriors had paid for the war themselves, and there was no conquered land to hand out. But the court gave thanks to the gods. A rumour said a wind god at Ise had sent the gusts. In 1293, the court raised his shrine in rank. Other shrines were raised too. And slowly, the belief spread that a divine wind would protect the country. So what sank Kublai Khan's fleet? In 1281, a real storm, striking an invasion that had been held off the beach for weeks, by a wall, and by men in small boats. In 1274, perhaps a storm on the way home. And the bomb in the picture may be a later addition. But the bombs on the seabed are real. If the southern fleet had arrived on time, would the wall still have held?
