A bronze warship ram was on display in a dentist's office in Sicily. In 2001, an archaeologist, Sebastiano Tusa, heard about it. The authorities seized it. Fishermen had pulled it up near the Egadi Islands. So a team went looking on the seabed. By the end of the 2024 season, they had counted 27 rams. For the first time, the excavators write, an ancient naval battle site had been discovered. The battle that ended Rome's first war with Carthage. And a writer tells us Rome's fleet that day was paid for by its leading citizens, to be repaid if all went well. So does the seabed agree with him?

Off western Sicily, 241 B.C.

A fleet paid for by citizens

To find out, we need his story first, then the bronze on the seabed, and then the names cast into it. Rome and Carthage had been fighting over Sicily for more than twenty years. Our main witness is the Greek historian Polybius, born a generation or more after the battle. He names the two best-known earlier writers, Philinus, who took Carthage's side, and Fabius, who took Rome's. They seem to me, he says, to have been much in the case of lovers. By his account, Rome had stayed off the sea for nearly five years, after storms and defeats. There were no funds in the public treasury for this purpose, he writes. So the leading citizens paid. One, two, or three of them, according to their means, undertook to provide a quinquereme fully equipped on the understanding that they would be repaid if all went well. Two hundred warships, built on a promise.

The blockade runner

And all of them, Polybius says, were copies of one ship. A few years before the battle, a leading Carthaginian, Hannibal, called the Rhodian, ran Rome's blockade of the harbour at Lilybaeum, in daylight, in full view. On the way out, he stopped, oars still out, as if to challenge them. No one came out. With one ship, Polybius writes, he successfully defied the whole Roman fleet. Then the Romans caught a fine Carthaginian ship on a bank they had built in the shallows, gave it a select crew, and ran him down. His ship became the model for the new Roman fleet.

Did you know? Rome had copied an enemy ship before. Polybius says that when Rome built its first fleet, in the same war, a Carthaginian decked ship ran aground and fell into Roman hands: “This ship they now used as a model, and built their whole fleet on its pattern.” The rowers trained “on rowers' benches on dry land”. (Histories 1.20.15 and 1.21.2, tr. W. R. Paton, 1922)

The morning of the battle

Now the copied ships had to fight. Carthage sent a fleet under a commander called Hanno, loaded with grain and supplies for its army on the mountain of Eryx. His plan: land the cargo first, take on the best mercenaries and their general, Hamilcar Barca, and only then fight. The Roman commander, Gaius Lutatius Catulus, had drilled his crews every day. He waited off the islands. At dawn, Polybius says, the wind was blowing for the enemy, and the sea was heavy and rough. At first he hesitated much what to do. Then he reckoned: fight now, against heavily laden ships, or later, against lightened ships, the pick of the army, and Hamilcar himself. He attacked. The Carthaginians lowered their masts and came on, cheering each other. But their ships, being loaded, Polybius writes, were not in a serviceable condition for battle. Fifty sunk, he says, and seventy captured with their crews. The rest got away when the wind turned. A later Roman summary gives the day: the tenth of March.

Did you know? A very late writer, Orosius, in the fifth century A.D., says the two fleets lay all night at the islands “with their anchors almost interwoven” (Histories against the Pagans 4.10.6, our rendering of “intertextis propemodum anchoris”).

How many ships sank?

But the numbers do not agree. Another Greek historian, Diodorus, gives 117 Carthaginian ships lost. And 80 Roman ships lost, 30 of them completely. He even keeps Philinus' own figure for the prisoners: 6,000. Polybius says very nearly 10,000. A late Roman summary, by Eutropius, has 125 sunk, and says: never was a battle fought at sea with such forces. The excavators think Polybius follows Fabius and plays down the Roman losses.

Finding the battlefield

That is the story the writers tell. Now the seabed. The survey began in 2005. The finds lie 70 to 90 metres down, in a search area that Manhattan could fit inside 4.5 times. The first three years, by the excavators' account, were largely fruitless. The survey found its first ram in 2008. Then the rams came: some with the wood of the ship they had hit still stuck in their fins. Helmets. By 2019, more than 900 supply jars. One helmet, decorated with a griffin and given a silvery finish, stands 28 centimetres tall, larger, the excavators note, than any helmet of its type in the catalogue they compared it with. Tusa did not see the end of it. In March 2019, he died in a plane crash, on his way to a UNESCO meeting.

Names stamped in bronze

And the rams talk. Several carry a Latin stamp, cast into the bronze. Lucius Quinctius, son of Gaius, quaestor, approved this ram. A quaestor was a junior Roman official, often handling money. On some, a winged figure, probably Victory, holds up a wreath. On others, a helmet, one of them with three feathers. In 2025, a newly cleaned ram gave another quaestor, probably Servius Sulpicius, son of Gaius. And the same year, encrusted lumps from the site went through a CT scanner at a radiology practice in Trapani. Inside: swords, spears and javelins. The find began in a dentist's office. It goes on at a radiologist's.

Did you know? The ram in our photographs, Egadi 6, has its own stamp: “Gaius Papirius, son of Tiberius, (and) Marcus Publicius, son of Lucius, quaestors, approved (this ram)”, in J. Prag's translation (I.Sicily ISic004367). The Latin spells the names in an old form, Paperios and Populicios.

Roman rams on the losing side?

Now the test. Rome's victory was lopsided, so most of what lies here, the excavators reason, should come from Carthage's fleet. Yet by 2019, of 23 rams, 11 carried Roman stamps or symbols, and only two Punic inscriptions. So why do so many Roman rams lie where Carthage's fleet went down? The historian Jonathan Prag calls one explanation attractive: eight years earlier, at Drepana, Carthage had captured 93 Roman ships. Perhaps Carthage sent them back to sea in its emergency fleet. The historian William Murray argues that a number of these rams came from such ships. Behind one Latin-stamped ram, divers found two Carthaginian coins. And ballast stones in the jars came from Pantelleria and North Africa, which points to Carthage. But Rome's own losses are unclear, the excavators warn, so pinning any single ram to one side is difficult. Some may come from Roman ships that sank. And one more thing does not fit. By 2019, all 23 rams looked the same size, 84 centimetres to a metre long. The excavators write that the ships appear to correspond with triremes, rather than the quinqueremes that Polybius states. Either Polybius made the fleets too uniform, they suggest, or bigger ships are still down there.

Did you know? A ram with a Punic inscription, Egadi 3, had its letters cut into the wax layer of the mould before the bronze was cast (RPM Nautical Foundation). What they say is disputed: very different translations have been proposed.

The price of peace

Whoever's ships they were, the battle ended the war. Carthage asked for peace, and Polybius gives the first draft. The Carthaginians to evacuate the whole of Sicily. The Carthaginians to pay to the Romans by instalments in twenty years 2,200 Euboean talents. The Roman people refused it. Their commissioners halved the time and added a thousand talents. Polybius called it the longest, most unintermittent, and greatest war we know of. Rome, he says, lost about seven hundred warships in it, counting shipwrecks, and Carthage about five hundred. Were the citizens who paid for the fleet ever repaid? In the passages we read, Polybius does not say.

Two men, one triumph

Back in Rome, the victory started a quarrel of its own. A praetor, Quintus Valerius Falto, claimed a triumph too, and bet that the fleet had been beaten under his command. A later Roman collector of famous deeds, Valerius Maximus, gives his argument: the consul had lain lame in a litter in that battle. Other late writers say Catulus had been wounded earlier. One says in the thigh, at Drepana. A judge, Atilius Calatinus, asked Falto two things. If you two had disagreed, whose order would have counted? And whose omens? The consul's, said Falto, both times. Then the judge ruled for Catulus, before Catulus had said a word. And yet the list of triumphs later carved in stone in Rome shows both men. Catulus on the fourth of October. Falto two days later.

Did you know? A Roman summary of Livy notes for these years: “two praetors were then created for the first time” (Periocha 19, our rendering). Falto, the man who wanted a triumph of his own, was a praetor.

Verdict

So, does the seabed agree with Polybius? On the place, yes. A battle at the Egadi Islands, ships rammed and sunk, a fleet carrying cargo. The jars and the helmets are there. On the ships, not quite: they look smaller than he says, and many carry Roman stamps. On the numbers, the seabed cannot settle it, at least not yet.

Over to you

Most of the readable rams on the seabed carry Roman marks, yet Rome won. Captured Roman ships sailing for Carthage, or another answer? Falto or Catulus: who deserved the triumph, the man who fought or the man in command?

Where it happened

  • Battle site off Levanzo, Egadi Islands (241 B.C.): Where the rams were found, 70 to 90 metres down, west of Levanzo; the survey area was about 270 square kilometres. The point is placed from one ram's published position (Egadi 6, about 7 km west of Levanzo), not from a survey map.
  • Levanzo (241 B.C.): One of the Egadi Islands; the finds lie off its west side.
  • Favignana (241 B.C.): The largest of the Egadi Islands. Polybius says Catulus sailed to Aegusa, off Lilybaeum, before the battle; we have no source in hand that identifies Aegusa with a modern island.
  • Lilybaeum (Marsala) (250 B.C.): The Carthaginian harbour Rome blockaded, where Hannibal the Rhodian ran the blockade in full view, a few years before the battle.
  • Drepana (Trapani) (249 B.C.): Where Carthage captured ninety-three Roman ships in 249 B.C., Polybius says; some scholars think those ships fought again for Carthage in 241. One late writer says Catulus was wounded in the thigh at Drepana.
  • Mount Eryx (Erice) (241 B.C.): The mountain where Hamilcar Barca's army was; Hanno's fleet was bringing it grain and supplies.
  • Pantelleria (241 B.C.): Ballast stones in the jars found at the battle site came from Pantelleria and North Africa, which points to Carthage.
  • Carthage (241 B.C.): Rome's enemy in the First Punic War; it sent Hanno's fleet and then asked for peace.
  • Capitoline Museums, Rome (the Fasti) (241 B.C.): The list of triumphs, carved in stone under Augustus, shows a naval triumph for Catulus on 4 October and for Falto two days later.

It goes on at a radiologist's. Now the test. Rome's victory was lopsided, so most of what lies here, the excavators reason, should come from Carthage's fleet. Yet by 2019, of 23 rams, 11 carried Roman stamps or symbols, and only two Punic inscriptions. So why do so many Roman rams lie where Carthage's fleet went down? The historian Jonathan Prag calls one explanation attractive: eight years earlier, at Drepana, Carthage had captured 93 Roman ships. Perhaps Carthage sent them back to sea in its emergency fleet. The historian William Murray argues that a number of these rams came from such ships. Behind one Latin-stamped ram, divers found two Carthaginian coins. And ballast stones in the jars came from Pantelleria and North Africa, which points to Carthage. But Rome's own losses are unclear, the excavators warn, so pinning any single ram to one side is difficult. Some may come from Roman ships that sank. And one more thing does not fit. By 2019, all 23 rams looked the same size, 84 centimetres to a metre long. The excavators write that the ships appear to correspond with triremes, rather than the quinqueremes that Polybius states. Either Polybius made the fleets too uniform, they suggest, or bigger ships are still down there. Whoever's ships they were, the battle ended the war. Carthage asked for peace, and Polybius gives the first draft. The Carthaginians to evacuate the whole of Sicily. The Carthaginians to pay to the Romans by instalments in twenty years 2,200 Euboean talents. The Roman people refused it. Their commissioners halved the time and added a thousand talents. Polybius called it the longest, most unintermittent, and greatest war we know of. Rome, he says, lost about seven hundred warships in it, counting shipwrecks, and Carthage about five hundred. Were the citizens who paid for the fleet ever repaid? In the passages we read, Polybius does not say. Back in Rome, the victory started a quarrel of its own. A praetor, Quintus Valerius Falto, claimed a triumph too, and bet that the fleet had been beaten under his command. A later Roman collector of famous deeds, Valerius Maximus, gives his argument: the consul had lain lame in a litter in that battle. Other late writers say Catulus had been wounded earlier. One says in the thigh, at Drepana. A judge, Atilius Calatinus, asked Falto two things. If you two had disagreed, whose order would have counted? And whose omens? The consul's, said Falto, both times. Then the judge ruled for Catulus, before Catulus had said a word. And yet the list of triumphs later carved in stone in Rome shows both men. Catulus on the fourth of October. Falto two days later. So, does the seabed agree with Polybius? On the place, yes. A battle at the Egadi Islands, ships rammed and sunk, a fleet carrying cargo. The jars and the helmets are there. On the ships, not quite: they look smaller than he says, and many carry Roman stamps. On the numbers, the seabed cannot settle it, at least not yet. Most of the readable rams on the seabed carry Roman marks, yet Rome won. Captured Roman ships sailing for Carthage, or another answer?
