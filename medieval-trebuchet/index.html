The Trebuchet: How It Really Worked

Stirling, Scotland. 20 July 1304. After three months of siege, the castle surrenders to Edward I, king of England, without conditions. But the king gives an order. A letter written at Stirling that day passes it on. None of his people are to enter, “till it is struck with the Warwolf”. And those inside are to defend themselves from the said wolf “as they best can”.

The Warwolf was the king's newest siege engine. Almost certainly, a trebuchet. And the trebuchet is usually drawn as one machine. There were really two. One was pulled by a crew on ropes. The other was swung by a falling weight. Today: how each worked, what the sources say, and what nobody knows.

After Rome: a beam and a crew

Our Roman onager drew its power from twisted rope. The machine that took over after Rome had no spring at all. A long beam turns on an axle. A sling hangs from the long end, and ropes from the short end. A crew pulls the ropes down hard, all at once. The long end whips up, and the sling throws the stone. We call it a traction trebuchet. Medieval writers called it a petrary, or a mangonel. Not a torsion catapult: a beam, pulled by people.

From China to Thessalonica

The idea came from China. A Chinese military manual of the eleventh century lists the crews. The largest: 250 men pulling, and 2 more to aim and release. By the late 500s, the machine had reached the eastern Mediterranean. At Thessalonica, in 586 or 597, scholars still argue which, a bishop describes the Avars' machines. “…lengths of wood, like the beams of a large house, and these had slings … the rocks were large, the shots frequent …”

A note on the source: the historian Whitby (as reported by S. McCotter, 2003) points out that the author of the Miracles of St Demetrius may be describing the machines of his own time and assuming they worked the same way half a century earlier. So the bishop's description shows what the machines were like when he wrote, and only probably what the Avars used.

Toulouse, 1218

Toulouse, 1218. The crusader Simon de Montfort is besieging the city. A poem written on the Toulouse side tells what happened. Inside the town stood a stone thrower that a carpenter had built. And it was pulled by ladies, and girls, and married women. The stone came straight to where it was needed, and struck the count on his steel helmet. He fell to the ground, dead, bloody and black. A chronicler on the crusaders' side, Peter of Vaux-de-Cernay, says the stone came from a mangonel. One machine, two names.

Did you know? The stone that killed Simon de Montfort at Toulouse in 1218 came, says the Toulouse poem, from a stone thrower that a carpenter had built and that was pulled by ladies, girls and married women ("E tiravan la donas e tozas e molhers", Canso, line 8450, ed. P. Meyer, 1875). The crusaders' chronicler, Peter of Vaux-de-Cernay, calls it a mangonel.

The counterweight: who invented it?

The second kind replaced the crew with a weight. Winch the long end down, let go, and a falling box swings the beam. Who invented it, and when? Here the experts disagree. The earliest known drawing of a counterweight machine is in a treatise written for Saladin, by al-Tarsusi. The historian Michael Fulton dates it to about 1180, and puts the machine's arrival in the last two decades of that century. Paul Chevedden argues for an older story, with an Islamic hybrid machine first, and then a Byzantine invention. Fulton, and the historian Peter Purton, say the hybrid never existed. On the date, as Purton puts it, this debate will continue.

Villard's chest of earth

Around 1230, a Frenchman called Villard de Honnecourt drew the base of one in his book of drawings. “If you desire to make the strong engine which is called a trebuchet,” he wrote on the page, “pay attention …” “The counter-poise is very heavy. For it is a chest full of earth, which is two great toises (twelve feet) long, and nine feet broad, and twelve feet deep.” The physicist Erik Horsdal reckons a chest that size suggests a weight of up to about 30 tonnes. The other page he points to was removed from the book centuries ago. So every complete Villard trebuchet, ours included, is a reconstruction.

How the throw works

So how does the throw work? Capstans winch the arm down and lift the weight. The stone sits in the sling, under the beam. Release the catch. The weight drops, the arm swings up, and the sling whips round behind it. The sling's free end is a loop over a metal prong at the tip. At the right moment it flies off, and the stone is free. Too early, and it flies backwards. The Danish builders report that Napoleon III's test machine fired its first shot 70 metres behind it. And a box hung on a hinge, free to swing, throws farther than one fixed solid to the arm.

What the replicas show

How far could they throw? Medieval sources give few reliable figures, so the best numbers come from replicas. In 1989, a Danish museum built a 12 tonne counterweight trebuchet. With 2 tonnes in the box and a 15 kilo ball, its longest shot was 168 metres. Shots landed within 2 or 3 metres of a straight line. A crew of 10 or 12 fired about once every 10 minutes. Its trigger, the builders admit, is not from any medieval source. Small rope machines built today throw stones of 2 to 4 kilos about 80 metres. Quick and light, against slow and heavy.

Back to China

And then the counterweight went east. In 1271, Kublai Khan sent west, to a Mongol prince, for trebuchet makers. At Xiangyang, the official history says, one engineer's machine threw a stone of about 95 kilos. The sound shook heaven and earth. The Song commander was afraid, and surrendered the city. Chinese writers called it the Muslim trebuchet.

A machine in a hundred carts

Machines this big travelled to a siege in pieces, by cart. Early 1291. The Mamluk army is gathering machines for the siege of Acre. The officer Abu'l-Fida and his comrades collect a great machine from Krak des Chevaliers. It is a load for 100 carts. “I received as my share the load of one cart, for I was then an emir of ten.” (our translation) Rain and snow. Oxen dying of cold. A ride of eight days took them a month.

Did you know? Abu'l-Fida, later known as a historian, helped haul a great siege machine to Acre in 1291. It filled a hundred carts, and his own share, as "an emir of ten", was one cart; rain, snow and oxen dying of cold turned a ride of eight days into a month (Abu'l-Fida, Annales, Recueil des historiens des croisades, Historiens orientaux I, pp. 163 to 164; our translation from the French).

Building the Warwolf

At Stirling, the engines needed weights. The king told his son to get as much lead as he could for the siege, from the churches around Perth and Dunblane. Except over the altars. A receipt at Stirling names what lead was for: the weights of the king's engines. Then they built the new one. The pay roll lists 5 master carpenters, 50 carpenters and 4 pages, making a “War wolf”. The king even paid for an oriel, a window, for the queen and her ladies, to watch the assault. And the man rewarded for his labour on the Warwolf was the queen's valet, Thomas of Greenfield. £40. Some websites say Edward's castle builder, Master James of St George, built it. No record we read says so.

Did you know? At Stirling in 1304 the king paid for an oriel "for the queen, the countesses of Gloucester and Hereford and other ladies and damsels, for watching the assault on the said castle" (Wardrobe book, in Bain, Calendar of Documents relating to Scotland, vol. IV, p. 466; our translation from the Latin).

The siege of Stirling

The siege had begun in April. The chronicler Pierre de Langtoft counts thirteen great engines. Walter of Guisborough says the king's machines threw stones of 100, 200 or 300 pounds, and knocked down the tops of the walls. When the commander, William Oliphant, gave up, says a chronicle kept at Westminster, the garrison walked out barefoot, with ropes around their necks. Edward put none of them to death.

How many were inside? The sources disagree. The chronicler Pierre de Langtoft names two knights, twenty gentlemen besides pages and a porter, a friar, a monk and thirteen gentlewomen with their laundress; his editor Wright takes these to be only the people of rank. The surrender instrument names the governor, Sir William Oliphant, and 25 of his garrison (Bain II no. 1562). Walter of Guisborough says the prisoners were more than 140.

Did it break the walls?

So did the Warwolf break Stirling? The sources do not agree. Langtoft names a “terrible engine” of the king's, “Ludgar”, probably the same machine. “At its stroke”, he says, it “broke down the entire wall”. The Westminster chronicle says it cost less than the king's failed battering ram, and did more harm. But the letter shows the castle had already surrendered when the king insisted on his shot. Weapon, or demonstration? We cannot be sure of the order of events. And could any trebuchet breach a stone wall? Fulton argues even the biggest could not breach stone walls, though he grants they threatened the thicker walls below. Purton doubts that carved stones of over a hundred kilos did no serious harm. Some stones survive. Outside Kenilworth Castle, archaeologists found eight shots from the siege of 1266, from 1 kilo to 105, probably thrown by trebuchets.

What we really know

So what do we know about the Warwolf? Its name, its builders, and the king's order. What we do not know is how big it was. No medieval source we have found gives its height, its weight, its range, or its stones. The giant figures you find online come with no source. We leave them out.

Verdict

So that's how the trebuchet really worked. First a beam pulled by people. Then a beam swung by a falling weight. Gravity, used as a weapon. And a king who kept a surrendered castle waiting for his wolf.

Over to you

Stirling had already surrendered, and Edward still made it wait for the Warwolf. Was that a test, a show, or a punishment? The women pulling the ropes at Toulouse, the officer with one cartload of a giant machine, or the queen's window at Stirling: which detail stays with you?

Quick answers

How did a trebuchet work?

There were really two machines. In the first, a long beam turns on an axle, with a sling at the long end and ropes at the short end; a crew pulls the ropes down hard, all at once, the long end whips up and the sling throws the stone. In the second, a weight replaced the crew: capstans winch the arm down and lift the weight, the catch is released, the weight drops, the arm swings up and the sling whips round behind it. The sling's free end is a loop over a metal prong at the tip; at the right moment it flies off, and the stone is free.

Who invented the counterweight trebuchet?

Here the experts disagree. The earliest known drawing of a counterweight machine is in a treatise written for Saladin by al-Tarsusi. The historian Michael Fulton dates it to about 1180 and puts the machine's arrival in the last two decades of that century. Paul Chevedden argues for an older story, with an Islamic hybrid machine first and then a Byzantine invention. Fulton and the historian Peter Purton say the hybrid never existed. On the date, as Purton puts it, this debate will continue.

How far could a trebuchet throw?

Medieval sources give few reliable figures, so the best numbers come from replicas. In 1989 the Medieval Centre in Denmark built a 12 tonne counterweight trebuchet. With 2 tonnes in the box and a 15 kilo ball, its longest shot was 168 metres, and shots landed within 2 or 3 metres of a straight line. A crew of 10 or 12 fired about once every 10 minutes. Small rope machines built today throw stones of 2 to 4 kilos about 80 metres.

What was the Warwolf?

The Warwolf was Edward I's newest siege engine at the siege of Stirling Castle in 1304, almost certainly a trebuchet. A pay roll lists 5 master carpenters, 50 carpenters and 4 pages making it. On 20 July 1304 the castle surrendered without conditions, but a letter written that day says none of the king's people were to enter till it was struck with the Warwolf. What we do not know is how big it was: no medieval source we have found gives its height, its weight, its range or its stones.

Could a trebuchet break a castle wall?

The sources do not agree. The chronicler Pierre de Langtoft says that a terrible new engine of the king's, which he calls Ludgar and which was probably the Warwolf, broke down the entire wall at Stirling at its stroke, and the Westminster chronicle says it cost less than the king's failed battering ram and did more harm. The historian Michael Fulton argues even the biggest machines mostly destroyed battlements, while threatening the thicker walls below; Peter Purton doubts that carved stones of over a hundred kilos did no serious harm. Outside Kenilworth Castle, archaeologists found eight shots from the siege of 1266, from 1 kilo to 105, probably thrown by trebuchets.

Where it happened

  • Stirling Castle (A.D. 1304): Besieged by Edward I from April 1304; surrendered without conditions on 20 July 1304, and the king's order was that none of his people enter till it was struck with the Warwolf (Bain II no. 1560).
  • Perth (A.D. 1304): The king told his son to get lead for the siege from the churches around Perth and Dunblane, except over the altars (Bain II no. 1504).
  • Dunblane Cathedral (A.D. 1304): The churches around Dunblane were to give lead for the siege of Stirling (Bain II no. 1504); which churches is not recorded in our sources.
  • Toulouse (A.D. 1218): Simon de Montfort was killed besieging the city in 1218 by a stone from a machine pulled by women, says the Toulouse poem (Canso, lines 8448 to 8455).
  • Thessaloniki (A.D. 586): The Avars' stone throwers, described by Bishop John in the Miracles of St Demetrius; the siege is dated 586 or 597, scholars disagree.
  • Krak des Chevaliers (A.D. 1291): Abu'l-Fida's 'fortress of the Kurds', where a great machine, a load for a hundred carts, was collected for the siege of Acre (standard identification).
  • Acre (A.D. 1291): The Mamluk army gathered siege machines for Acre in early 1291 (Abu'l-Fida).
  • Xiangyang (A.D. 1273): A counterweight machine built by an engineer from the west threw a stone of about 95 kilos; the Song commander surrendered the city (Yuan shi, juan 203).
  • Kenilworth Castle (A.D. 1266): Eight shots from the siege of 1266, 1 to 105 kg, found outside the walls; probably thrown by trebuchets (English Heritage).
  • Medieval Centre, Nykøbing Falster (A.D. 1989): Where a 12 tonne counterweight trebuchet was reconstructed in 1989; longest shot 168 m with a 15 kg ball (Hansen 1992).

    Transcript

    Read the full narration

    The Trebuchet. Ropes, weights and the Warwolf. Stirling, Scotland. 20 July 1304. After three months of siege, the castle surrenders to Edward the First, king of England, without conditions. But the king gives an order. A letter written at Stirling that day passes it on. None of his people are to enter, “till it is struck with the Warwolf”. And those inside are to defend themselves from the said wolf “as they best can”. The Warwolf was the king's newest siege engine. Almost certainly, a trebuchet. And the trebuchet is usually drawn as one machine. There were really two. One was pulled by a crew on ropes. The other was swung by a falling weight. Today: how each worked, what the sources say, and what nobody knows. Our Roman onager drew its power from twisted rope. The machine that took over after Rome had no spring at all. A long beam turns on an axle. A sling hangs from the long end, and ropes from the short end. A crew pulls the ropes down hard, all at once. The long end whips up, and the sling throws the stone. We call it a traction trebuchet. Medieval writers called it a petrary, or a mangonel. Not a torsion catapult: a beam, pulled by people. The idea came from China. A Chinese military manual of the eleventh century lists the crews. The largest: 250 men pulling, and 2 more to aim and release. By the late 500s, the machine had reached the eastern Mediterranean. At Thessalonica, in 586 or 597, scholars still argue which, a bishop describes the Avars' machines. “…lengths of wood, like the beams of a large house, and these had slings … the rocks were large, the shots frequent …” Toulouse, 1218. The crusader Simon de Montfort is besieging the city. A poem written on the Toulouse side tells what happened. Inside the town stood a stone thrower that a carpenter had built. And it was pulled by ladies, and girls, and married women. The stone came straight to where it was needed, and struck the count on his steel helmet. He fell to the ground, dead, bloody and black. A chronicler on the crusaders' side, Peter of Vaux-de-Cernay, says the stone came from a mangonel. One machine, two names. The second kind replaced the crew with a weight. Winch the long end down, let go, and a falling box swings the beam. Who invented it, and when? Here the experts disagree. The earliest known drawing of a counterweight machine is in a treatise written for Saladin, by al-Tarsusi. The historian Michael Fulton dates it to about 1180, and puts the machine's arrival in the last two decades of that century. Paul Chevedden argues for an older story, with an Islamic hybrid machine first, and then a Byzantine invention. Fulton, and the historian Peter Purton, say the hybrid never existed. On the date, as Purton puts it, this debate will continue. Around 1230, a Frenchman called Villard de Honnecourt drew the base of one in his book of drawings. “If you desire to make the strong engine which is called a trebuchet,” he wrote on the page, “pay attention …” “The counter-poise is very heavy. For it is a chest full of earth, which is two great toises (twelve feet) long, and nine feet broad, and twelve feet deep.” The physicist Erik Horsdal reckons a chest that size suggests a weight of up to about 30 tonnes. The other page he points to was removed from the book centuries ago. So every complete Villard trebuchet, ours included, is a reconstruction. So how does the throw work? Capstans winch the arm down and lift the weight. The stone sits in the sling, under the beam. Release the catch. The weight drops, the arm swings up, and the sling whips round behind it. The sling's free end is a loop over a metal prong at the tip. At the right moment it flies off, and the stone is free. Too early, and it flies backwards. The Danish builders report that Napoleon III's test machine fired its first shot 70 metres behind it. And a box hung on a hinge, free to swing, throws farther than one fixed solid to the arm. How far could they throw? Medieval sources give few reliable figures, so the best numbers come from replicas. In 1989, a Danish museum built a 12 tonne counterweight trebuchet. With 2 tonnes in the box and a 15 kilo ball, its longest shot was 168 metres. Shots landed within 2 or 3 metres of a straight line. A crew of 10 or 12 fired about once every 10 minutes. Its trigger, the builders admit, is not from any medieval source. Small rope machines built today throw stones of 2 to 4 kilos about 80 metres. Quick and light, against slow and heavy. If you are enjoying this type of content, please don't forget to subscribe. And then the counterweight went east. In 1271, Kublai Khan sent west, to a Mongol prince, for trebuchet makers. At Xiangyang, the official history says, one engineer's machine threw a stone of about 95 kilos. The sound shook heaven and earth. The Song commander was afraid, and surrendered the city. Chinese writers called it the Muslim trebuchet. Machines this big travelled to a siege in pieces, by cart. Early 1291. The Mamluk army is gathering machines for the siege of Acre. The officer Abu'l-Fida and his comrades collect a great machine from Krak des Chevaliers. It is a load for 100 carts. “I received as my share the load of one cart, for I was then an emir of ten.” (our translation) Rain and snow. Oxen dying of cold. A ride of eight days took them a month. At Stirling, the engines needed weights. The king told his son to get as much lead as he could for the siege, from the churches around Perth and Dunblane. Except over the altars. A receipt at Stirling names what lead was for: the weights of the king's engines. Then they built the new one. The pay roll lists 5 master carpenters, 50 carpenters and 4 pages, making a “War wolf”. The king even paid for an oriel, a window, for the queen and her ladies, to watch the assault. And the man rewarded for his labour on the Warwolf was the queen's valet, Thomas of Greenfield. £40. Some websites say Edward's castle builder, Master James of St George, built it. No record we read says so. The siege had begun in April. The chronicler Pierre de Langtoft counts thirteen great engines. Walter of Guisborough says the king's machines threw stones of 100, 200 or 300 pounds, and knocked down the tops of the walls. When the commander, William Oliphant, gave up, says a chronicle kept at Westminster, the garrison walked out barefoot, with ropes around their necks. Edward put none of them to death. So did the Warwolf break Stirling? The sources do not agree. Langtoft names a “terrible engine” of the king's, “Ludgar”, probably the same machine. “At its stroke”, he says, it “broke down the entire wall”. The Westminster chronicle says it cost less than the king's failed battering ram, and did more harm. But the letter shows the castle had already surrendered when the king insisted on his shot. Weapon, or demonstration? We cannot be sure of the order of events. And could any trebuchet breach a stone wall? Fulton argues even the biggest could not breach stone walls, though he grants they threatened the thicker walls below. Purton doubts that carved stones of over a hundred kilos did no serious harm. Some stones survive. Outside Kenilworth Castle, archaeologists found eight shots from the siege of 1266, from 1 kilo to 105, probably thrown by trebuchets. So what do we know about the Warwolf? Its name, its builders, and the king's order. What we do not know is how big it was. No medieval source we have found gives its height, its weight, its range, or its stones. The giant figures you find online come with no source. We leave them out. So that's how the trebuchet really worked. First a beam pulled by people. Then a beam swung by a falling weight. Gravity, used as a weapon. And a king who kept a surrendered castle waiting for his wolf. Two questions for you. Stirling had already surrendered. Was the Warwolf's shot a test, a show, or a punishment? And which detail stays with you: the women on the ropes at Toulouse, the officer with one cartload, or the queen's window?
