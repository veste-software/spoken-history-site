Dyrrachium, on the Adriatic coast, 48 B.C. Caesar and Pompey are fighting a civil war. Pompey's men storm one of Caesar's small forts. When it is over, says Caesar, not one man inside is unwounded. Four centurions of a single cohort have lost their eyes. His men count the arrows shot into the fort. About 30,000, Caesar writes. Suetonius, writing later, makes it 130,000. And they bring him the shield of a centurion named Scaeva. It has 120 holes in it.

The scutum. The big shield of the Roman foot soldier. Today we're asking what it was made of, what has been dug up, and what the texts really say. Because the answer is a lot thinner than you might think.

Polybius: boards, glue, linen, calfskin

The earliest detailed description we have comes from Polybius, a Greek historian who lived in Rome in the second century B.C. His shield is curved. Two and a half feet wide and four feet long. In Roman feet, that's about 74 centimetres by 118. It is made, he says, of a double layer of boards, glued. Then covered, first with linen, then with calfskin. Iron edging on the top and bottom rims. And an iron boss in the middle, to turn aside stones and heavy missiles. Paton's old English translation leaves out a detail. The Greek names the glue. Taurokolla. Glue made from bulls' hides. And one puzzle. He says the shield is a palm thick at the rim. About seven centimetres. No shield in this video comes close. We do not know exactly what he meant.

Did you know? Polybius names the glue of the Roman shield: taurokolla, glue made from bulls' hides. The best-known English translation leaves it out (Polybius 6.23.3; Liddell, Scott and Jones).

Where did it come from? Two stories

Where did it come from? The ancient writers disagree. Livy says the Romans once carried round shields. Then, after the soldiers began to be paid, they made the long scutum instead. The Greek writer Athenaeus says the Romans learned the long shield from the Samnites, their enemies in Italy. And Plutarch says the general Camillus had the long shields rimmed with bronze, because wood alone could not stop the swords of the Gauls. All three wrote centuries later. They give us traditions, not a date.

Pliny's shield wood

So what wood? Pliny the Elder says the best shield woods come from trees of wet ground. Cut into them, he says, and the wood closes up over the wound, so the iron finds it harder to get through. Vine, chaste tree, willow, lime, birch, elder and poplar. The lightest, he says, are chaste tree and willow. A shield that heals its own wounds. That is Pliny's claim, not a tested fact.

Did you know? Pliny the Elder says the best shield woods close up over a cut, so that the iron finds it harder to get through: vine, chaste tree, willow, lime, birch, elder and poplar. That is Pliny's claim, not a tested fact (Natural History 16.209).

What survives: the metal

Now the finds. Wood, glue, linen and skin rot away. What usually survives is the metal. But the metal can tell us about the wood. In a grave at Nijmegen, in the Netherlands, from about the year 90, all that was left of the shield was its boss, two bronze rivets and an iron grip. The rivets had gone through the board, and their ends were bent back. The gap between head and end shows the board was 4 to 5 millimetres thick. The wood stuck to them was alder. Annelies Koster, of the museum in Nijmegen, thinks the dead man was a cavalryman. So this is not a legionary's scutum. But it shows how thin a shield could be.

A whole shield from Egypt

For a whole shield, we have to go to Egypt. At Harit, in the Fayum, a whole shield was found. The German archaeologist Wolfgang Kimmig examined it in Cairo and published it in 1940. It is 128 centimetres long. Its board is three layers of thin wooden strips, each 2 to 3 millimetres thick at the rim. A wooden spine runs down the middle, with a wooden boss. Both faces were covered in felt. No metal part bigger than a nail. But whose shield was it? Kimmig called it Celtic. The historian Sean Manning points out that it has never been scientifically dated. Who made it, we do not know. But its size is close to Polybius's numbers. And on a Roman relief that the Louvre dates to the second century B.C., legionaries hold long oval shields with a spine down the middle.

Dura-Europos: a painted scutum

Dura-Europos, a town with a Roman garrison, on the Euphrates, in Syria. Probably the year 256. A Persian army is besieging the town. Its engineers dig a mine under one of the towers, Tower 19. The defenders dig a countermine to meet them. The town falls. And in that tower, nearly seventeen centuries later, the excavators find a painted shield.

It came out flattened, in thirteen pieces, with its boss missing. It is just over a metre tall, and about 83 centimetres wide, measured around the curve. An exhibition about Dura called it the best-preserved example of the classic legionary shield.

How it was built

So what is it made of? The excavators' report from 1936 tells us. Strips of plane wood, 3 to 8 centimetres wide, and 1.5 to 2 millimetres thick. Three layers. The front and back layers run across the shield. The middle layer runs from top to bottom. All glued together. Five millimetres of wood in all. In the report's words: resembling modern plywood. Remember the first episode? A replica bolt-shooter punched through a shield boss backed with 18 millimetres of plywood. More than three times this shield's wood. And plane is not on Pliny's list of shield woods. The report noticed that too.

On the back, a frame of thin wooden strips. The cross strip in the middle was thickened into a handle, lashed with rawhide, behind the hole where the boss sat. Front and back were covered in thin red-dyed leather, kid or parchment. Over the front went fine linen, to paint on. The edges were bound with strips of leather, stitched through tiny holes in the wood. Leather, not the iron edging Polybius describes. And it was painted. An eagle, two winged Victories, and a lion. Was the lion a legion's badge? The report suggests a few that might fit. Whose shield it was, we do not know.

Names on shields

Shields did carry names, though. Vegetius, writing at the end of the fourth century A.D. or later, says that each soldier's name was once written on his shield, with his cohort and his century. One real example: a bronze shield boss, said to have been dredged from the mouth of the Tyne, in north-east England, before 1877. Its letters say: the Eighth Legion Augusta, the century of Julius Magnus, Junius Dubitatus. A soldier's name, on his own shield, more than two centuries before Vegetius wrote. How it ended up in the river, we do not know.

Did you know? A bronze shield boss said to have been dredged from the mouth of the Tyne carries its owner's name: Junius Dubitatus, of the century of Julius Magnus, Eighth Legion Augusta (Tyne and Wear HER 928).

How heavy? A dispute

So how heavy was a scutum? Here the experts disagree. A standard handbook puts it at about ten kilos, citing replicas built by Peter Connolly and Marcus Junkelmann. Sean Manning thinks that is too heavy. Replicas, he argues, tend to be too thick at the edges, with heavy leather and too much metal. And Kimmig called the Harit shield astonishingly light. Manning estimates six to seven kilos. A team that scanned shield remains from Kalkriese, in Germany, leans to the lightest replicas, at about five. Vegetius adds one detail. In the old days, he says, recruits trained with wicker shields that weighed twice as much as a real one. In 1936, the Dura report said nothing is known of the scutum's weight. We found no original weighed since.

Did you know? Vegetius, a late writer quoting older books, says recruits once trained with wicker shields twice as heavy as a real one (Vegetius 1.11).

Covers, and the rain

And the shield had an enemy we tend to forget. Water. In 57 B.C., Caesar says, the Nervii attacked so suddenly that his men had no time to pull the covers off their shields. Roman shield covers have been dug up. One from Caerleon, in Wales, is goatskin, with a drawstring in the hem. And in the forests of Germany, in the year 9, Varus's army was caught in heavy rain. Cassius Dio, writing two centuries later, says the soldiers could not use their shields. They were thoroughly soaked. The leather expert Carol van Driel-Murray suspects the glued boards warped too.

What we really have

So, what do we really have? One painted scutum from Syria, nearly whole. One undated shield from Egypt. Metal parts and covers from across the empire. And texts that only partly fit them. What we don't have: its real weight, a date for when it came in, and what Polybius meant by a palm.

Two questions for you: Scaeva's shield came back with 120 holes. Proof of a great shield, or of a great story? And ten kilos or six: which side of the weight dispute do you find more convincing, and why?

Quick answers

What was the Roman scutum made of?

Polybius, in the second century B.C., says a double layer of boards, glued with taurokolla, glue made from bulls' hides, then covered first with linen, then with calfskin, with iron edging on the top and bottom rims and an iron boss. The Dura-Europos shield, according to the excavators' report of 1936, is strips of plane wood 1.5 to 2 millimetres thick in three layers glued crosswise, five millimetres of wood in all, covered in thin red-dyed leather, kid or parchment, with fine linen over the front for the painting, and edges bound with leather.

How thick was a Roman shield?

The Dura board is five millimetres of wood. The rivets from a grave at Nijmegen show a board 4 to 5 millimetres thick. The Harit shield's three layers of strips are each 2 to 3 millimetres thick at the rim, as Wolfgang Kimmig reported. Polybius says the shield is a palm thick at the rim, about seven centimetres; no shield in the video comes close, and we do not know exactly what he meant.

How heavy was a Roman scutum?

The experts disagree. A standard handbook puts it at about ten kilos, citing replicas by Peter Connolly and Marcus Junkelmann. The historian Sean Manning estimates six to seven kilos, and a team that scanned shield remains from Kalkriese leans to the lightest replicas, at about five. In 1936 the Dura report said nothing is known of the scutum's weight, and we found no original weighed since.

Where was the best-preserved legionary scutum found?

In Tower 19 at Dura-Europos, a town with a Roman garrison on the Euphrates in Syria, which fell to a Persian siege, probably in the year 256. It came out flattened, in thirteen pieces, with its boss missing. An exhibition about Dura called it the best-preserved example of the classic legionary shield. It is now in the Yale University Art Gallery.

Where did the scutum come from?

The ancient writers disagree. Livy says the Romans once carried round shields and made the long scutum after the soldiers began to be paid; Athenaeus says they learned the long shield from the Samnites; Plutarch says Camillus had the long shields rimmed with bronze against the swords of the Gauls. All three wrote centuries later: they give traditions, not a date.

Where it happened

  • Dura-Europos, Syria (A.D. 256): Town with a Roman garrison on the Euphrates, besieged and taken by the Persians, probably in 256; the painted scutum was found in Tower 19 in the 1932-33 season (Yale University Art Gallery 1933.715).
  • Dyrrachium (Durres, Albania) (48 B.C.): Caesar against Pompey, 48 B.C.; the centurion Scaeva's shield with 120 holes (Caesar, Civil War 3.53). The fort's exact site is not known.
  • Nijmegen, Netherlands (A.D. 92): A grave of about A.D. 90 to 95 with a shield boss, two rivets and an iron grip; the rivets give a board 4 to 5 mm thick (Koster 2010).
  • Harit (Kasr el-Harit), Fayum, Egypt : Find place of a whole shield published by Wolfgang Kimmig in 1940; never scientifically dated, maker unknown. Pin approximate.
  • Mouth of the Tyne, north-east England (A.D. 125): A bronze shield boss of Junius Dubitatus, Eighth Legion Augusta, said to have been dredged up before 1877; the record dates it to Hadrian's reign (A.D. 117 to 138). Now in the British Museum.
  • Caerleon, Wales (A.D. 100): Legionary fortress; a fragmentary goatskin shield cover, probably first or early second century (van Driel-Murray 1988).
  • Kalkriese, Germany : Early imperial shield remains scanned by a modern team (Schroeder, Petri and Zerbe 2022, as reported by S. Manning).

    Korrespondenzblatt 52 (2022) 91-107 (CT measurements of the early imperial shields from Kalkriese) "point to reconstructions of the Kasr el Harit shield which

    weigh 5.28 to 7.84 kg and suspect that the lower end of that range is more correct for the Augustan period"; a 2024 replica by Sergio Escudero weighs 4.8 kg.

    **Secondary only**: Kimmig, Bishop and Coulston, Junkelmann and Schroeder et al. are known to us through Manning. The narration attributes each point.

    Transcript

    Read the full narration

    The Roman Scutum. What Rome's shield was really made of. Dyrrachium, on the Adriatic coast, 48 B.C. Caesar and Pompey are fighting a civil war. Pompey's men storm one of Caesar's small forts. When it is over, says Caesar, not one man inside is unwounded. Four centurions of a single cohort have lost their eyes. His men count the arrows shot into the fort. About 30,000, Caesar writes. Suetonius, writing later, makes it 130,000. And they bring him the shield of a centurion named Scaeva. It has 120 holes in it. The scutum. The big shield of the Roman foot soldier. Today we're asking what it was made of, what has been dug up, and what the texts really say. Because the answer is a lot thinner than you might think. The earliest detailed description we have comes from Polybius, a Greek historian who lived in Rome in the second century B.C. His shield is curved. Two and a half feet wide and four feet long. In Roman feet, that's about 74 centimetres by 118. It is made, he says, of a double layer of boards, glued. Then covered, first with linen, then with calfskin. Iron edging on the top and bottom rims. And an iron boss in the middle, to turn aside stones and heavy missiles. Paton's old English translation leaves out a detail. The Greek names the glue. Taurokolla. Glue made from bulls' hides. And one puzzle. He says the shield is a palm thick at the rim. About seven centimetres. No shield in this video comes close. We do not know exactly what he meant. Where did it come from? The ancient writers disagree. Livy says the Romans once carried round shields. Then, after the soldiers began to be paid, they made the long scutum instead. The Greek writer Athenaeus says the Romans learned the long shield from the Samnites, their enemies in Italy. And Plutarch says the general Camillus had the long shields rimmed with bronze, because wood alone could not stop the swords of the Gauls. All three wrote centuries later. They give us traditions, not a date. So what wood? Pliny the Elder says the best shield woods come from trees of wet ground. Cut into them, he says, and the wood closes up over the wound, so the iron finds it harder to get through. Vine, chaste tree, willow, lime, birch, elder and poplar. The lightest, he says, are chaste tree and willow. A shield that heals its own wounds. That is Pliny's claim, not a tested fact. Now the finds. Wood, glue, linen and skin rot away. What usually survives is the metal. But the metal can tell us about the wood. In a grave at Nijmegen, in the Netherlands, from about the year 90, all that was left of the shield was its boss, two bronze rivets and an iron grip. The rivets had gone through the board, and their ends were bent back. The gap between head and end shows the board was 4 to 5 millimetres thick. The wood stuck to them was alder. Annelies Koster, of the museum in Nijmegen, thinks the dead man was a cavalryman. So this is not a legionary's scutum. But it shows how thin a shield could be. For a whole shield, we have to go to Egypt. At Harit, in the Fayum, a whole shield was found. The German archaeologist Wolfgang Kimmig examined it in Cairo and published it in 1940. It is 128 centimetres long. Its board is three layers of thin wooden strips, each 2 to 3 millimetres thick at the rim. A wooden spine runs down the middle, with a wooden boss. Both faces were covered in felt. No metal part bigger than a nail. But whose shield was it? Kimmig called it Celtic. The historian Sean Manning points out that it has never been scientifically dated. Who made it, we do not know. But its size is close to Polybius's numbers. And on a Roman relief that the Louvre dates to the second century B.C., legionaries hold long oval shields with a spine down the middle. Dura-Europos, a town with a Roman garrison, on the Euphrates, in Syria. Probably the year 256. A Persian army is besieging the town. Its engineers dig a mine under one of the towers, Tower 19. The defenders dig a countermine to meet them. The town falls. And in that tower, nearly seventeen centuries later, the excavators find a painted shield. It came out flattened, in thirteen pieces, with its boss missing. It is just over a metre tall, and about 83 centimetres wide, measured around the curve. An exhibition about Dura called it the best-preserved example of the classic legionary shield. If you are enjoying this type of content, please don't forget to subscribe. So what is it made of? The excavators' report from 1936 tells us. Strips of plane wood, 3 to 8 centimetres wide, and 1.5 to 2 millimetres thick. Three layers. The front and back layers run across the shield. The middle layer runs from top to bottom. All glued together. Five millimetres of wood in all. In the report's words: resembling modern plywood. Remember the first episode? A replica bolt-shooter punched through a shield boss backed with 18 millimetres of plywood. More than three times this shield's wood. And plane is not on Pliny's list of shield woods. The report noticed that too. On the back, a frame of thin wooden strips. The cross strip in the middle was thickened into a handle, lashed with rawhide, behind the hole where the boss sat. Front and back were covered in thin red-dyed leather, kid or parchment. Over the front went fine linen, to paint on. The edges were bound with strips of leather, stitched through tiny holes in the wood. Leather, not the iron edging Polybius describes. And it was painted. An eagle, two winged Victories, and a lion. Was the lion a legion's badge? The report suggests a few that might fit. Whose shield it was, we do not know. Shields did carry names, though. Vegetius, writing at the end of the fourth century A.D. or later, says that each soldier's name was once written on his shield, with his cohort and his century. One real example: a bronze shield boss, said to have been dredged from the mouth of the Tyne, in north-east England, before 1877. Its letters say: the Eighth Legion Augusta, the century of Julius Magnus, Junius Dubitatus. A soldier's name, on his own shield, more than two centuries before Vegetius wrote. How it ended up in the river, we do not know. So how heavy was a scutum? Here the experts disagree. A standard handbook puts it at about ten kilos, citing replicas built by Peter Connolly and Marcus Junkelmann. Sean Manning thinks that is too heavy. Replicas, he argues, tend to be too thick at the edges, with heavy leather and too much metal. And Kimmig called the Harit shield astonishingly light. Manning estimates six to seven kilos. A team that scanned shield remains from Kalkriese, in Germany, leans to the lightest replicas, at about five. Vegetius adds one detail. In the old days, he says, recruits trained with wicker shields that weighed twice as much as a real one. In 1936, the Dura report said nothing is known of the scutum's weight. We found no original weighed since. And the shield had an enemy we tend to forget. Water. In 57 B.C., Caesar says, the Nervii attacked so suddenly that his men had no time to pull the covers off their shields. Roman shield covers have been dug up. One from Caerleon, in Wales, is goatskin, with a drawstring in the hem. And in the forests of Germany, in the year 9, Varus's army was caught in heavy rain. Cassius Dio, writing two centuries later, says the soldiers could not use their shields. They were thoroughly soaked. The leather expert Carol van Driel-Murray suspects the glued boards warped too. So, what do we really have? One painted scutum from Syria, nearly whole. One undated shield from Egypt. Metal parts and covers from across the empire. And texts that only partly fit them. What we don't have: its real weight, a date for when it came in, and what Polybius meant by a palm. So that's the scutum. Not a slab of oak. Thin strips of wood, glued crosswise, wrapped in skin and linen, and painted. Two questions for you. Scaeva's shield came back with 120 holes. Proof of a great shield, or of a great story?
