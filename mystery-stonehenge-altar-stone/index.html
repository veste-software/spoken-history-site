The Mystery of Stonehenge's Altar Stone

In 2024, tiny mineral grains traced the stone at the heart of Stonehenge to northeast Scotland. At least 750 kilometres away. More than 460 miles. If that is right, people moved a six tonne block almost the length of Britain, with no wheels, and no boat we know of that could carry it. Then, in November 2026, geologists went looking for the exact spot. Nothing they examined matched. So where did it come from? How did it travel? And why would anyone bother?

Stonehenge, on Salisbury Plain, in southern England. The people who built it left no writing at all. Everything we know comes from the stones, the digs and the laboratory. So: the stone, the old writers, the lab work, and three ways it could have travelled.

The stone under the fallen arch

First, the stone itself. The Altar Stone is a slab of pale green sandstone, 4.9 metres long, a metre wide, and half a metre thick. It weighs about 6 tonnes. It lies flat near the centre, broken, pinned under two fallen stones of the Great Trilithon, the tallest arch of the monument. Only three small patches of it can be seen. And nobody is allowed to take a sample. Its name probably goes back to the architect Inigo Jones. In 1620, King James the First, staying nearby, commanded him to find out what he could about Stonehenge. Jones measured a stone in the middle, sixteen foot long, and wrote: “whether it might be an Altar or no, I leave to the judgement of others.” He decided it was a Roman temple. Stonehenge is far older than Rome. The name stuck. What the stone was for, the newest study says plainly, is unknown.

Did you know? Nobody is allowed to take a sample of the Altar Stone, and only three small patches of it can be seen. The scientists who traced it worked on chips picked up in old digs, one of them collected in 1844 by a Mr Brown of Amesbury, from the stone's underside.

What the old writers saw

Jones was not the first to be baffled. In the 1130s, the chronicler Henry of Huntingdon counted Stonehenge among “four things in England which are very remarkable”. “No one”, he wrote, “has been able to discover by what mechanism such vast masses of stone were elevated, nor for what purpose they were designed.” In the same century, Geoffrey of Monmouth gave an answer. Merlin did it. In his story the stones stood in Ireland, and the king laughed at the plan to fetch them. “How is it possible”, he asks, “to remove such vast stones from so distant a country, as if Britain was not furnished with stones fit for the work?” Merlin took them down with ease, and had them carried to the ships. A legend, written thousands of years too late. But the king's question is ours. In 1654, the diarist John Evelyn tried to knock a piece off the monument. “The Stone is so exceeding hard”, he wrote, “that all my strength with a hammer could not breake a fragment.” Fourteen years later, Samuel Pepys hired saddle horses, “very dear”, and paid a shepherd woman fourpence for leading them. He found the stones, he wrote, “as prodigious as any tales I ever heard of them. God knows what their use was!”

Did you know? In 1654 the diarist John Evelyn took a hammer to the stones: “all my strength with a hammer could not breake a fragment”.

Did you know? On his visit in 1668 Samuel Pepys hired saddle horses, “very dear”, and paid a shepherd woman fourpence for leading them.

Tracing the other stones

Science has since traced the other stones. Most of the giant sarsens came from West Woods, about 25 kilometres north. Many of the smaller bluestones were traced to the Preseli Hills in west Wales, about 225 kilometres away. At two outcrops there, Craig Rhos-y-felin and Carn Goedog, archaeologists found the quarries, and at one, stone wedges still in the cracks. Mike Parker Pearson's team argued that some of them first stood in a circle in Wales, at Waun Mawn. Timothy Darvill calls that “unsupported by the current evidence”.

From Wales to Scotland

And the Altar Stone? For almost a century, it was Welsh too. In 1923 a geologist placed it in west Wales. Later work tried one Welsh sandstone after another. By 2023, a Welsh source looked very unlikely. With no sample allowed, scientists turned to chips picked up in old digs. One was collected in 1844 by a Mr Brown of Amesbury, from the stone's underside. A doctoral student at Curtin University in Australia, Anthony Clarke, and his colleagues dated the tiny grains inside two such fragments. Each grain carries the age of the rock it wore away from. Most of the zircons were over a billion years old. The pattern matched one place best: the Old Red Sandstone of the Orcadian Basin, in northeast Scotland. Their paper in Nature said the stone came at least 750 kilometres, and probably by sea. Weeks later, another study tested the obvious suspects, the standing stones of Orkney's own circles at Stenness and Brodgar. No match.

Did you know? Most of the zircon grains dated from the Altar Stone's chips were over a billion years old (Clarke et al., Nature 2024).

November 2026: no match

Then the Scottish trail went cold. On 5 November 2026, a team led by the geologist Duncan Pirrie published what he called “the most comprehensive geological investigation of the Altar Stone ever undertaken”. They went beyond the grains: the clay, the cement, a barium mineral called baryte, and chrome spinel, a mineral scarce in the rocks of the Orcadian Basin. Many outcrops in the Orcadian Basin, they concluded, could not have been the source. Pirrie said: “So far, none of the locations examined have the same set of characteristics as the Altar Stone.” Three of the scientists behind the Nature paper signed this one too. They do not throw out the grains. Those still point to the ancient crust of the far north. Clarke's team, in a study that summer, had found its closest grain match in Caithness, on the Scottish mainland. But the new study's clay results, as reported, count against Caithness. So the exact source is, in the new study's words, “as yet unknown”.

Did you know? Three of the scientists behind the 2024 Nature paper that traced the stone to Scotland also signed the 2026 study that found no examined outcrop to match (Bevins, Pearce and Ixer).

Ice, sea or land?

That makes the journey harder to judge. Three ways have been argued. First, ice. Could glaciers have carried it south? The Nature team said the ice of the far north flowed the other way, northward. Their summer study modelled the old ice sheets. From Caithness, one narrow path led southeast, toward Dogger Bank, land now under the North Sea. But the sea drowned Dogger Bank before the stone likely reached Stonehenge. And people would still have had to move it some 400 kilometres. Second, the sea. The Nature team thought a boat most likely. But no boat is known from that age. The oldest known in Britain, at Ferriby on the Humber, were about 15 metres long. The earliest dates from between about 2030 and 1780 B.C. Parker Pearson's team doubt such boats could carry six tonnes. In 2001, an attempt to carry a much smaller bluestone in a hide-covered boat failed. Third, land: a heavy wooden sledge on rails, laid and lifted again and again. Parker Pearson's team guess about eight months from northern Scotland. Ice, sea or land. Nobody knows which.

Did you know? In 2001 an attempt to carry a bluestone, much smaller than the Altar Stone, in a hide-covered boat failed (Parker Pearson et al. 2024).

Why bring it at all?

And why bring it at all? Even the scientists who traced it asked: “Why transport a drab greenish-grey six-tonne sandstone block over 650km from Scotland?” Parker Pearson points to a kind of stone circle that, in Britain, is found only in northeast Scotland, with a great slab laid flat between its two tallest stones. At Stonehenge, the Altar Stone lies at the foot of the two tallest uprights, under the arch that frames the midwinter sunset. And houses at Durrington Walls, near Stonehenge, have floor plans unusually like those at Skara Brae, in Orkney. So his team suggest the stone was a gift from the far north, to seal some kind of alliance. It is an idea, not a finding. And it leans on the very source the newest study now questions.

Did you know? A kind of stone circle that, in Britain, is found only in northeast Scotland, lays a great slab flat between its two tallest stones, and at Stonehenge the Altar Stone lies at the foot of the two tallest uprights (Parker Pearson et al. 2024).

What would settle it

So what do we really have? A six tonne stone, not local, not Welsh, with grains from the ancient north. And no quarry. What would settle it? An outcrop that matches the stone in every test, and beside it a quarry, with wedges and an empty niche, like the ones in Wales. No part of this needs wizards, giants or visitors from space. It needs people, working together over hundreds of kilometres.

Verdict

My verdict: the Altar Stone most likely came from somewhere far to the north, and people moved it for much of the way. Scotland is still the best guess, not a sure one. Nobody has found the place.

Over to you

Would you have risked six tonnes on the sea, or dragged it for eight months over land? Geoffrey's king laughed at fetching stones from so far. Why do you think anyone did it?

Where it happened

  • Stonehenge (2500 B.C.): Where the Altar Stone lies, near the centre, under two fallen stones of the Great Trilithon. When it arrived is uncertain: possibly during the second construction phase, around 2620 to 2480 B.C. (Clarke et al., Nature 2024); the year is approximate.
  • West Woods, near Marlborough (A.D. 2020): Where most of the giant sarsens came from, about 25 km north of Stonehenge (Nash et al. 2020, as cited by Clarke et al. 2024 and Clarke, Veness et al. 2026). The year stands for that study. Coordinates approximate.
  • Craig Rhos-y-felin, Preseli Hills (3000 B.C.): One of the two bluestone quarries in west Wales, about 225 km from Stonehenge, with stone wedges still in the cracks (Parker Pearson et al. 2024). The year is approximate: quarrying "centuries before and around 3000 bc" (same paper). Coordinates approximate.
  • Carn Goedog, Preseli Hills (3000 B.C.): The other bluestone quarry in west Wales (Parker Pearson et al. 2024). The year is approximate, as for Craig Rhos-y-felin. Coordinates approximate.
  • Stones of Stenness, Orkney (A.D. 2024): One of Orkney's own stone circles, tested as the Altar Stone's source in 2024: no match (Bevins et al. 2024, via Aberystwyth University, 5 September 2024). The year stands for the test. Coordinates approximate.
  • Ring of Brodgar, Orkney (A.D. 2024): Tested with the Stones of Stenness in 2024: no match (Bevins et al. 2024). The year stands for the test. Coordinates approximate.
  • Caithness (A.D. 2026): The county on the Scottish mainland whose sandstones gave the closest zircon match in Clarke, Veness et al. 2026 (J. Quaternary Science). No site is pinned: the exact source is, in Pirrie et al. 2026's words, as yet unknown. A point in the county area, not a find spot.
  • North Ferriby, on the Humber (2030 B.C.): Where the oldest known boats in Britain were found, about 15 m long; the earliest dates from between about 2030 and 1780 B.C. (Parker Pearson et al. 2024). No boat is known from the Altar Stone's age. Coordinates approximate.
  • Durrington Walls (A.D. 2024): The settlement near Stonehenge whose houses have floor plans unusually like those at Skara Brae (Parker Pearson et al. 2024). The year stands for that comparison; the houses' own dates are not in our sources. Coordinates approximate.
  • Skara Brae, Orkney (A.D. 2024): Neolithic houses by the Bay of Skaill, compared with Durrington Walls by Parker Pearson et al. 2024. The year stands for that comparison. Coordinates approximate.

No one, he wrote, has been able to discover by what mechanism such vast masses of stone were elevated, nor for what purpose they were designed. In the same century, Geoffrey of Monmouth gave an answer. Merlin did it. In his story the stones stood in Ireland, and the king laughed at the plan to fetch them. How is it possible, he asks, to remove such vast stones from so distant a country, as if Britain was not furnished with stones fit for the work? Merlin took them down with ease, and had them carried to the ships. A legend, written thousands of years too late. But the king's question is ours. In 1654, the diarist John Evelyn tried to knock a piece off the monument. The Stone is so exceeding hard, he wrote, that all my strength with a hammer could not breake a fragment. Fourteen years later, Samuel Pepys hired saddle horses, very dear, and paid a shepherd woman fourpence for leading them. He found the stones, he wrote, as prodigious as any tales I ever heard of them. God knows what their use was! Science has since traced the other stones. Most of the giant sarsens came from West Woods, about 25 kilometres north. Many of the smaller bluestones were traced to the Preseli Hills in west Wales, about 225 kilometres away. At two outcrops there, Craig Rhos-y-felin and Carn Goedog, archaeologists found the quarries, and at one, stone wedges still in the cracks. Mike Parker Pearson's team argued that some of them first stood in a circle in Wales, at Waun Mawn. Timothy Darvill calls that unsupported by the current evidence. And the Altar Stone? For almost a century, it was Welsh too. In 1923 a geologist placed it in west Wales. Later work tried one Welsh sandstone after another. By 2023, a Welsh source looked very unlikely. With no sample allowed, scientists turned to chips picked up in old digs. One was collected in 1844 by a Mr Brown of Amesbury, from the stone's underside. A doctoral student at Curtin University in Australia, Anthony Clarke, and his colleagues dated the tiny grains inside two such fragments. Each grain carries the age of the rock it wore away from. Most of the zircons were over a billion years old. The pattern matched one place best: the Old Red Sandstone of the Orcadian Basin, in northeast Scotland. Their paper in Nature said the stone came at least 750 kilometres, and probably by sea. Weeks later, another study tested the obvious suspects, the standing stones of Orkney's own circles at Stenness and Brodgar. No match. On 5 November 2026, a team led by the geologist Duncan Pirrie published what he called the most comprehensive geological investigation of the Altar Stone ever undertaken. They went beyond the grains: the clay, the cement, a barium mineral called baryte, and chrome spinel, a mineral scarce in the rocks of the Orcadian Basin. Many outcrops in the Orcadian Basin, they concluded, could not have been the source. Pirrie said: So far, none of the locations examined have the same set of characteristics as the Altar Stone. Three of the scientists behind the Nature paper signed this one too. They do not throw out the grains. Those still point to the ancient crust of the far north. Clarke's team, in a study that summer, had found its closest grain match in Caithness, on the Scottish mainland. But the new study's clay results, as reported, count against Caithness. So the exact source is, in the new study's words, as yet unknown. That makes the journey harder to judge. Three ways have been argued. First, ice. Could glaciers have carried it south? The Nature team said the ice of the far north flowed the other way, northward. Their summer study modelled the old ice sheets. From Caithness, one narrow path led southeast, toward Dogger Bank, land now under the North Sea. But the sea drowned Dogger Bank before the stone likely reached Stonehenge. And people would still have had to move it some 400 kilometres. Second, the sea. The Nature team thought a boat most likely. But no boat is known from that age. The oldest known in Britain, at Ferriby on the Humber, were about 15 metres long. The earliest dates from between about 2030 and 1780 B.C. Parker Pearson's team doubt such boats could carry six tonnes. In 2001, an attempt to carry a much smaller bluestone in a hide-covered boat failed. Third, land: a heavy wooden sledge on rails, laid and lifted again and again. Parker Pearson's team guess about eight months from northern Scotland. Ice, sea or land. Nobody knows which. And why bring it at all? Even the scientists who traced it asked: Why transport a drab greenish-grey six-tonne sandstone block over 650km from Scotland? Parker Pearson points to a kind of stone circle that, in Britain, is found only in northeast Scotland, with a great slab laid flat between its two tallest stones. At Stonehenge, the Altar Stone lies at the foot of the two tallest uprights, under the arch that frames the midwinter sunset. And houses at Durrington Walls, near Stonehenge, have floor plans unusually like those at Skara Brae, in Orkney. So his team suggest the stone was a gift from the far north, to seal some kind of alliance. It is an idea, not a finding. And it leans on the very source the newest study now questions. So what do we really have? A six tonne stone, not local, not Welsh, with grains from the ancient north. And no quarry. What would settle it? An outcrop that matches the stone in every test, and beside it a quarry, with wedges and an empty niche, like the ones in Wales. No part of this needs wizards, giants or visitors from space. It needs people, working together over hundreds of kilometres. My verdict: the Altar Stone most likely came from somewhere far to the north, and people moved it for much of the way. Scotland is still the best guess, not a sure one. Nobody has found the place. Would you have risked six tonnes on the sea, or dragged it for eight months over land?
