This cannon weighs about 17 tonnes. And it unscrews into two pieces. January 1868. On an open beach at the Dardanelles, the crew of a British warship does exactly that. They bring jacks of ten tons, and bars made to fit sockets cast in the bronze. It takes a force of nearly 40 tons to turn it. First the shorter half goes aboard. That afternoon, the longer half.

The gun was cast in 1464, for Mehmed II, the sultan who had taken Constantinople eleven years before. It is not the gun that fired on Constantinople. Of that one, as far as we know, only words written at the time survive. But this is a giant of the same kind, made for the same sultan. And you can stand next to it today. What the gun tells us, what the writers of 1453 say, and where the numbers break down.

What the gun is

It is at Fort Nelson, in Hampshire, a museum of the Royal Armouries. Two halves of bronze. The front one, the chase, took the stone ball. The back one, the breech, held the gunpowder. Each weighs between 8 and 9 tonnes. And by the museum's figures, the heavier half is the short one, the breech. The bore is 25 inches across, about 64 centimetres. Its granite balls weigh about 300 kilos each. The museum keeps five of them. Inside the bore, the museum notes score marks from the stone shot.

The screw and the sockets

Now the strange part. A screw thread on the breech turns into a thread inside the chase. At both ends of each half, sixteen bronze bars make sixteen sockets, for the levers that turn it. The British general John Lefroy studied the gun in 1868. He gives the screws as 23 inches across. He could only suppose that the threads were first cut in wood, then applied to the clay moulds. Why two pieces? The museum's catalogue suggests easier casting, or easier transport. It was cast muzzle down. The waste metal at the breech was hacked off with axes, probably while still hot. Lefroy could still see the axe marks.

The inscription and the maker's name

On the muzzle, raised in the bronze, is an inscription. “Help O God. The Sultan Muhammad Khan, son of Murad. The work of Munir Ali in the month of Rejeb. In the year 868.” That is the Islamic year. In our calendar, 1464. But whose name is it? The script is hard to read. The Ottoman Grand Master of Artillery read Munir. The orientalist Redhouse first read Muner, then leaned towards Minbir, a word that means pulpit. A third, Efflatoun Pasha, said it could not be Munir. Perhaps Mener. One story text in the museum's own collection record credits Urban, the founder of 1453. The bronze names someone else.

Orban, the founder of 1453

So who was Urban? The Byzantine historian Doukas tells of a famous Hungarian founder of bronze guns. Historians call him Urban, or Orban. He offered his skill to the emperor in Constantinople, who granted him a poor daily wage. And then, Doukas says, nobody paid even that. So he went to the sultan, who paid him so well that a quarter of it, from the emperor, would have kept him in the city. Could he cast a gun to beat those walls? Even the walls of Babylon, the founder answered. How far it would throw, he did not know. In three months it was cast. The test was at Adrianople. Before the test, the whole town was warned, so that no pregnant woman would lose her child from fright. The stone flew about a mile, Doukas says, and buried itself a fathom deep. Moving it took 60 oxen and 30 wagons. 200 men walked alongside to keep it balanced. 50 carpenters and 200 labourers went ahead, building bridges. It took February and March to drag it to a place five miles from the city.

Did you know? Before the great gun was tested at Adrianople, Doukas says, the whole town was warned, so that nobody would be struck dumb with fright and no pregnant woman would lose her child. The stone flew about a mile and buried itself a fathom deep (Doukas, chapter 35, Bonn edition, our translation from the Latin).

How the great guns were loaded and fired

Kritovoulos, a Greek in the sultan's service, describes how such a gun worked. For one great gun, he reports, 1,500 talents of bronze and tin. Bellows blew for three days and three nights. Its powder chamber, behind the bore, was a third as wide. In the Dardanelles Gun, the chamber is 9 inches across, the bore 25. To fire it, they packed the chamber with powder, and hammered in a plug of strong wood with iron bars. Then the stone ball. Then beams under the gun, weighed down with heavy stones, so the kick would not spoil the aim. Fire went in through a short hole at the back. And the powder, he adds, often bursts the bronze itself.

How big was the gun of 1453?

How big was the great gun? Here the numbers stop adding up. Kritovoulos makes the core of the mould 40 spans long, and 12 spans around. A gun that size, Lefroy wrote, would weigh over 100 tons, “beyond the bounds of credibility”. The bronze reported, on his rough reckoning, makes 32 at most. A writer of the time, Leonard of Chios, measured a ball from one of the siege guns with his own hands. “Eleven of my palms” around, he wrote (our translation). So how big Orban's gun really was, we do not know.

Did the guns take Constantinople?

Did the guns take the city? Kritovoulos says they shook the walls to pieces, and the sultan gave up on mining, because the guns did everything. But he also tells how the defenders hung bales of wool over the walls, to soften the blows. And how they built a rampart of timber and earth in the gaps. The stone balls buried themselves in it. The guns opened the walls. Men still had to climb through.

Guarding the Dardanelles

Later, giant guns like this one guarded the Dardanelles, the strait on the way to Constantinople. Around 1740, the traveller Richard Pococke saw a brass gun there, “twenty feet long, but in two parts”. A man, he wrote, may very well sit in it. Lefroy had no doubt it was this gun. The guns of its castle answered a ship's salute with real stone balls. Where they fell, Pococke says, the lands opposite commonly paid no rent. In his memoirs, Baron de Tott claims he fired a giant gun made in two screwed pieces, with 330 pounds of powder. Lefroy was sure it was this gun. But the chamber, he wrote, does not hold half that.

Did you know? Around 1740 Richard Pococke saw at the Dardanelles a brass gun "twenty feet long, but in two parts", so wide that "a man may very well sit in it"; Lefroy had no doubt it was this gun. The castle's guns answered a ship's salute with real stone balls, and the lands opposite, Pococke says, commonly paid no rent (Pococke 1745, vol. II, part II, pp. 103 to 104; Lefroy 1868, p. 262).

1807: stone shot against the Royal Navy

March 1807. Sir John Duckworth's British squadron forces the strait. The forts answer with stone balls. One nearly cuts the mainmast of the Windsor Castle in two. Another punches such a hole in the Active that her captain sees two of his crew put their heads through it at the same moment. The Royal Armouries' catalogue says this gun and its companions damaged six ships. Which gun fired which shot, no record we read says. But this gun, Lefroy noted, bore the mark of one shot that had hit it.

Did you know? In March 1807 a stone shot made such a hole in H.M.S. Active that her captain saw two of his crew put their heads through it at the same moment (Lefroy 1868, p. 272). Which gun fired it, no record we read says.

From the Sultan to Queen Victoria

How did it reach England? The talks began in 1857, says the museum, when the wrong gun was sent by mistake. Then Sultan Abdülaziz gave this one to Queen Victoria. Lefroy dates the gift to 1866; it was shipped in 1868. Lefroy counted eighteen of these old giants left, this one included, and three more just broken up. Only two dated from Mehmed's reign. One had just been broken up. This is the other. He knew of no other guns still in use after four centuries. Then Woolwich, then the Tower of London, and today Fort Nelson.

Did you know? The talks that brought this gun to England began in 1857, when the wrong gun, an eighteenth-century piece, was sent to England by mistake (Royal Armouries catalogue note, 2020).

What we really know

What do we know? Its date, its sultan, a maker's name read four ways, its weight, its screw. What we do not know is how closely it follows the guns of 1453. No source we read says it copies them. Lefroy thought the design went back to giant bombards from Flanders, like Dulle Griet at Ghent, whose chamber, he says, also screws on.

Verdict

So that is the Dardanelles Gun. Not the gun that broke Constantinople, but a giant of that sultan's reign you can still stand beside. 17 tonnes of bronze, made to unscrew, and still guarding the strait 400 years later.

Over to you

The gun names its maker, but its readers in 1868 could not agree on the name: Munir, Muner, Minbir or Mener. Why would a founder sign a gun at all? Sixty oxen and hundreds of men, two months on the road, for one gun. Was a giant bombard worth it, or would ten smaller guns have done more?

Quick answers

What is the Dardanelles Gun?

A bronze bombard cast in 1464 for the Ottoman sultan Mehmed the Second, eleven years after he took Constantinople. It is made in two halves: the chase, which took the stone ball, and the breech, which held the gunpowder, joined by a screw thread. The Royal Armouries' record gives the halves as 8,941 and 8,128 kilograms, about seventeen tonnes in all, and the bore as 25 inches across, about 64 centimetres. It is at Fort Nelson in Hampshire, a museum of the Royal Armouries.

Why does the Dardanelles Gun unscrew into two pieces?

The museum's catalogue suggests the two pieces may have made casting easier, or transport. A screw thread on the breech turns into a thread inside the chase, and at both ends of each half sixteen bronze bars make sockets for the levers that turn it. In January 1868 the crew of a British warship unscrewed it on an open beach at the Dardanelles with ten-ton jacks and bars made to fit the sockets; the ship's log, quoted by John Lefroy, says a force of nearly 40 tons was used.

Is the Dardanelles Gun the cannon that fired on Constantinople in 1453?

No. It was cast in 1464. Of the gun that fired on Constantinople, as far as we know, only words written at the time survive. The Dardanelles Gun is a giant of the same kind, made for the same sultan, but how closely it follows the guns of 1453 we do not know: no source we read says it copies them. One story text in the museum's own collection record credits Urban, the founder of 1453; the bronze names someone else.

Who made the Dardanelles Gun?

The inscription on the muzzle, in the museum's translation, reads: "The work of Munir Ali in the month of Rejeb. In the year 868", the Islamic year, 1464 in our calendar. But the script is hard to read. The Ottoman Grand Master of Artillery read Munir; the orientalist Redhouse first read Muner, then leaned towards Minbir, a word that means pulpit; Efflatoun Pasha said it could not be Munir, perhaps Mener.

How big was the great cannon of 1453?

We do not know. Kritovoulos makes the core of the mould forty spans long and twelve spans around. A gun that size, Lefroy wrote in 1868, would weigh over a hundred tons, beyond the bounds of credibility, while the bronze Kritovoulos reports makes, on Lefroy's rough reckoning, thirty-two tons at most. Leonard of Chios, a writer of the time, measured a ball from one of the siege guns with his own hands: eleven of his palms around.

Where it happened

  • Fort Nelson, Royal Armouries (A.D. 1868): Where the gun is today (Royal Armouries, Gallery 57). No year for its move here is given in our sources; the year marks its arrival in England.
  • Chanak (Çanakkale) (A.D. 1868): The open beach at Chanak where the crew of H.M.S. Terrible unscrewed the gun in January 1868 (log quoted by Lefroy); exact spot unknown.
  • Kilid Bahr (Kilitbahir) (A.D. 1868): The fort on the European shore; the gun stood in one or other of the forts at Chanak or Kilid Bahr (Royal Armouries). Year: Lefroy's list of the great guns at the straits, January 1868.
  • Constantinople (Istanbul), the land walls (A.D. 1453): Taken by Mehmed II in 1453; as far as we know, the great guns of the siege survive only in texts (Doukas, Kritovoulos, Leonard of Chios).
  • Adrianople (Edirne) (A.D. 1453): Where Doukas places the test shot of the great gun, in the winter before the siege.
  • Woolwich, the Rotunda (A.D. 1868): The Museum of Artillery, where Lefroy studied the gun in 1868.
  • Tower of London (A.D. 1930): Placed on the south side of the White Tower in 1930 (Royal Armouries, ffoulkes diary).
  • Ghent, Dulle Griet (A.D. 1868): The Flemish bombard Lefroy compared in 1868; year is his comparison, not the bombard's date.

And by the museum's figures, the heavier half is the short one, the breech. The bore is 25 inches across, about 64 centimetres. Its granite balls weigh about 300 kilos each. The museum keeps five of them. Inside the bore, the museum notes score marks from the stone shot. Now the strange part. A screw thread on the breech turns into a thread inside the chase. At both ends of each half, sixteen bronze bars make sixteen sockets, for the levers that turn it. The British general John Lefroy studied the gun in 1868. He gives the screws as 23 inches across. He could only suppose that the threads were first cut in wood, then applied to the clay moulds. Why two pieces? The museum's catalogue suggests easier casting, or easier transport. It was cast muzzle down. The waste metal at the breech was hacked off with axes, probably while still hot. Lefroy could still see the axe marks. On the muzzle, raised in the bronze, is an inscription. “Help O God. The Sultan Muhammad Khan, son of Murad. The work of Munir Ali in the month of Rejeb. In the year 868.” That is the Islamic year. In our calendar, 1464. But whose name is it? The script is hard to read. The Ottoman Grand Master of Artillery read Munir. The orientalist Redhouse first read Muner, then leaned towards Minbir, a word that means pulpit. A third, Efflatoun Pasha, said it could not be Munir. Perhaps Mener. One story text in the museum's own collection record credits Urban, the founder of 1453. The bronze names someone else. So who was Urban? The Byzantine historian Doukas tells of a famous Hungarian founder of bronze guns. Historians call him Urban, or Orban. He offered his skill to the emperor in Constantinople, who granted him a poor daily wage. And then, Doukas says, nobody paid even that. So he went to the sultan, who paid him so well that a quarter of it, from the emperor, would have kept him in the city. Could he cast a gun to beat those walls? Even the walls of Babylon, the founder answered. How far it would throw, he did not know. In three months it was cast. The test was at Adrianople. Before the test, the whole town was warned, so that no pregnant woman would lose her child from fright. The stone flew about a mile, Doukas says, and buried itself a fathom deep. Moving it took 60 oxen and 30 wagons. 200 men walked alongside to keep it balanced. 50 carpenters and 200 labourers went ahead, building bridges. It took February and March to drag it to a place five miles from the city. Kritovoulos, a Greek in the sultan's service, describes how such a gun worked. For one great gun, he reports, 1,500 talents of bronze and tin. Bellows blew for three days and three nights. Its powder chamber, behind the bore, was a third as wide. In the Dardanelles Gun, the chamber is 9 inches across, the bore 25. To fire it, they packed the chamber with powder, and hammered in a plug of strong wood with iron bars. Then the stone ball. Then beams under the gun, weighed down with heavy stones, so the kick would not spoil the aim. Fire went in through a short hole at the back. And the powder, he adds, often bursts the bronze itself. How big was the great gun? Here the numbers stop adding up. Kritovoulos makes the core of the mould 40 spans long, and 12 spans around. A gun that size, Lefroy wrote, would weigh over 100 tons, “beyond the bounds of credibility”. The bronze reported, on his rough reckoning, makes 32 at most. A writer of the time, Leonard of Chios, measured a ball from one of the siege guns with his own hands. “Eleven of my palms” around, he wrote (our translation). So how big Orban's gun really was, we do not know. If you are enjoying this type of content, please don't forget to subscribe. Did the guns take the city? Kritovoulos says they shook the walls to pieces, and the sultan gave up on mining, because the guns did everything. But he also tells how the defenders hung bales of wool over the walls, to soften the blows. And how they built a rampart of timber and earth in the gaps. The stone balls buried themselves in it. The guns opened the walls. Men still had to climb through. Later, giant guns like this one guarded the Dardanelles, the strait on the way to Constantinople. Around 1740, the traveller Richard Pococke saw a brass gun there, “twenty feet long, but in two parts”. A man, he wrote, may very well sit in it. Lefroy had no doubt it was this gun. The guns of its castle answered a ship's salute with real stone balls. Where they fell, Pococke says, the lands opposite commonly paid no rent. In his memoirs, Baron de Tott claims he fired a giant gun made in two screwed pieces, with 330 pounds of powder. Lefroy was sure it was this gun. But the chamber, he wrote, does not hold half that. March 1807. Sir John Duckworth's British squadron forces the strait. The forts answer with stone balls. One nearly cuts the mainmast of the Windsor Castle in two. Another punches such a hole in the Active that her captain sees two of his crew put their heads through it at the same moment. The Royal Armouries' catalogue says this gun and its companions damaged six ships. Which gun fired which shot, no record we read says. But this gun, Lefroy noted, bore the mark of one shot that had hit it. How did it reach England? The talks began in 1857, says the museum, when the wrong gun was sent by mistake. Then Sultan Abdülaziz gave this one to Queen Victoria. Lefroy dates the gift to 1866; it was shipped in 1868. Lefroy counted eighteen of these old giants left, this one included, and three more just broken up. Only two dated from Mehmed's reign. One had just been broken up. This is the other. He knew of no other guns still in use after four centuries. Then Woolwich, then the Tower of London, and today Fort Nelson. What do we know? Its date, its sultan, a maker's name read four ways, its weight, its screw. What we do not know is how closely it follows the guns of 1453. No source we read says it copies them. Lefroy thought the design went back to giant bombards from Flanders, like Dulle Griet at Ghent, whose chamber, he says, also screws on. So that is the Dardanelles Gun. Not the gun that broke Constantinople, but a giant of that sultan's reign you can still stand beside. 17 tonnes of bronze, made to unscrew, and still guarding the strait 400 years later. Two questions for you. The gun names its maker, but its readers in 1868 could not agree on the name. Why would a founder sign a gun at all? And 60 oxen and hundreds of men, for one gun.
