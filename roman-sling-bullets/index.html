A lump of lead, about 4.5 centimetres long. It weighs 60 grams. On one side, a thunderbolt. On the other, raised letters: the number of a legion, the eleventh, and two words. DIVOM IVLIVM. The deified Julius. It was found at Perugia, in Italy. There, in the winter of 41 to 40 B.C., the young Octavian, the future Augustus, was starving out an army of his rivals. His side, scholars think, slung bullets like this one at the town. And this is one of the polite ones.

Glandes. Roman sling bullets. Today we're asking what these bullets were, what has been dug up, and what the texts really say. Because some of these little lumps of lead carry words. Messages, slung at the enemy.

What is a glans?

So, what is a glans? The Latin word means an acorn. The great Latin dictionary of Lewis and Short calls the missile an acorn-shaped ball of lead or clay, hurled at the enemy. Many Roman ones look more like an almond or a lemon, pointed at both ends. These, found together in England, weigh from about 67 to 123 grams. They were cast. Molten lead went into a two-part mould. Any letters were cut backwards into the mould, so they came out raised and readable on the bullet. And the army cast them itself. In Africa, in 46 B.C., the unknown author of the African War writes that Caesar's army set up smithies, cast sling bullets, and sent to Sicily for lead. The weapon that threw them was simple. Two cords and a pouch. The late Roman writer Vegetius says slings were made of flax or of hair.

The siege of Perusia

Back to Perugia. Ancient Perusia, a town on a hill in Etruria. In 41 B.C., Mark Antony's brother Lucius, who was consul that year, and Antony's wife Fulvia took up arms against Octavian. Lucius ended up shut inside Perusia. The historian Appian, writing nearly two centuries later, says Octavian ringed the hill with a ditch 30 feet deep and 30 wide, and a wall with 1,500 wooden towers. Inside, the food ran out. Appian says the slaves were refused rations and ate grass, and the dead were buried in long trenches, so the enemy would not see how many there were. When the besieged tried to break out, he says, they fought from their siege towers with stones, arrows, and leaden balls.

Bullets that talk back

And the bullets from that siege talk back. In 1885, the German scholar Karl Zangemeister published the inscribed Latin sling bullets known in his day. Perusia has its own chapter, and its own photographic plates. Most, he argued, came from Octavian's side. Some carry a commander: CAESAR IMP, Caesar, general. That is Octavian, who used his adoptive father's name. Some name a legion and an officer. L XII. SCAEVA PR PIL: legion 12, Scaeva, chief centurion. And some carry a message. One reads: ESVREIS ET ME CELAS. You're starving, and you're hiding it from me. Another was scratched by hand into the finished bullet. Bald Lucius Antonius, you're done for, by Gaius Caesar's victory. Then there are the ones I can't read out. One aims at Fulvia's body. One, from Lucius's side, aims at Octavian's backside. Parts of those words are modern restorations, but the meaning is plain. And it wasn't only the soldiers. The poet Martial, more than a century later, quotes six verses that he says Augustus himself wrote about Fulvia. They are just as crude.

Did you know? One bullet from the siege of Perusia taunts the starving town: ESVREIS ET ME CELAS, “you're starving, and you're hiding it from me” (Zangemeister 1885, n. 84). Appian says the slaves inside were refused food and ate grass.

Three hundred at the altar? A dispute

Perusia gave in to hunger early in 40 B.C. Then comes the darkest story. Suetonius writes that some say three hundred prisoners were picked out and sacrificed, on the Ides of March, at an altar to the deified Julius. Cassius Dio tells it too, as a story that goes around. Appian and Velleius, who both describe the end of the siege, don't mention it. Zangemeister linked the eleventh legion's bullet, DIVOM IVLIVM, to that story. But the bullet only names the legion and the god. Whether it called for revenge, and whether the sacrifice happened at all, we don't know.

The forger of Ascoli

There's a catch with inscribed bullets. Collectors wanted them. And where there's a market, there's a forger. From about 1860, a man in Ascoli, Giuseppe Vincenzini, took genuine, blank ancient bullets and hammered them into carved wooden dies, putting new words into old lead. A French scholar published more than 600 of them as genuine. In 1877, Vincenzini confessed to Zangemeister, in front of two witnesses. Zangemeister also records a boast. Vincenzini once bought an inscribed bullet from a countryman, sure it was ancient. Later he realised the inscription was his own work. How do you tell? A real inscription was cast with the bullet. The forger's letters were struck into lead that had already corroded, with sharp edges and angular round letters. And real bullets were mostly blank. At Ascoli, besieged by Rome in the Social War, Zangemeister and a local colleague dug for three days in 1877. 238 bullets. 2 had writing.

Did you know? The forger Giuseppe Vincenzini, who stamped new inscriptions into genuine blank Roman bullets, once boasted that he had bought a bullet from a countryman as genuine and later realised the inscription was his own work (Zangemeister 1885, p. 93).

Did lead melt in flight?

So how good was a lead bullet? The Romans had an odd idea about that. Lucretius, Virgil, Ovid and Seneca all write that a lead bullet heats up in flight. Some say it melts. It doesn't. In tests for the Royal Armouries in the 1990s, Thom Richardson measured slung bullets at about 30 metres a second. At that speed, by our own calculation, the air warms the lead by less than 1 degree. Lead melts at about 327 °C. What lead did do was fly further. In the same tests, 40-gram lead bullets went about 145 metres on average. Stones, about 80 to 90. Richardson himself warns that he never learned the sling in a culture that used it. Skilled slingers may have done better. Or the old claims of range may be exaggerated.

Did you know? Lucretius, Virgil, Ovid and Seneca believed that a lead sling bullet heats up or melts in flight. At the speeds measured by Thom Richardson, about thirty metres a second, the air warms it by less than one degree (our calculation); lead melts at about 327 degrees Celsius.

When a bullet hit

And when one hit? The Roman writer Celsus, in the first century A.D., explains what to do with a lead bullet that has broken the skin and lodged inside. Open the wound wider, he says, and draw it out with forceps, the way it went in.

Burnswark Hill, Scotland

Now, a hill in Scotland. Burnswark, in Dumfriesshire, north of the line of Hadrian's Wall. A flat-topped hill, ringed by the ramparts of an Iron Age fort. And on its slopes, two Roman camps. One to the south, one to the north. In 1898, excavators found 67 lead sling bullets here. 20 lay at one gateway of the hillfort, 12 at another. By the 1970s the count stood at 133. The archaeologist George Jobey called that unique in Britain. More recently, a team led by John Reid swept the hill with metal detectors. They found nearly 700 more signals that looked like lead sling bullets, most of them along the southern rampart. Reid traces the barrage along about half a kilometre of it. In two test trenches in 2015, 17 of the 18 lead signals turned out to be Roman bullets. Some of the bullets are small, many under 20 grams, with a hole drilled about 5 millimetres wide. When the team slung copies, they buzzed in flight, Reid writes, like an agitated wasp. He thinks they were meant to frighten. That's his team's hypothesis. And Richardson notes that a good cast whirrs anyway, with any kind of missile.

Siege or firing range?

So what happened at Burnswark? The early excavators saw a siege. In 1978, George Jobey argued for a training ground. A field school, where the army practised building camps and attacking with slings, bows and artillery. A key point: the rampart was already ruined when the bullets fell. Reid's team confirmed that. Jobey had one quibble with his own theory. There was, he wrote, some laxity in the retrieval of re-usable ammunition. Reid and his colleague Andrew Nicholson read it the other way. A barrage this big looks to them like a real assault, a show of force, perhaps at the start of the Roman advance into Scotland around A.D. 140. The title of their own paper ends with a question mark. Siege or firing range? The evidence has not settled it.

Did you know? George Jobey, who argued that Burnswark was a Roman training ground, admitted one problem with his own theory: there was “some laxity in the retrieval of re-usable ammunition” (Jobey 1978).

What we really have

So, what do we really have? Thousands of real bullets, most of them blank. A small share with words: commanders, legions, jokes and threats. Texts that say the army cast them, surgeons cut them out, and poets thought they melted. What we don't have: how many Roman soldiers carried a sling, or the full story of what happened at Burnswark.

Verdict, and what comes next

So that's the Roman sling bullet. A lump of lead you could close your hand around. And now and then, a message.

Quick answers

What is a Roman sling bullet called?

A glans, plural glandes. The Latin word means an acorn, and the Latin dictionary of Lewis and Short calls the missile an acorn-shaped ball of lead or clay, hurled at the enemy. Many Roman ones look more like an almond or a lemon, pointed at both ends.

What do the inscriptions on Roman sling bullets say?

Bullets from Octavian's siege of Perusia in 41 to 40 B.C., published by Karl Zangemeister in 1885, name commanders such as Caesar, general, and legions and officers such as legion twelve and Scaeva, chief centurion. Some carry messages, like You're starving, and you're hiding it from me, and some are obscene insults aimed at Fulvia or Octavian.

How were lead sling bullets made?

They were cast. Molten lead went into a two-part mould, and any letters were cut backwards into the mould so they came out raised and readable on the bullet. The anonymous author of the African War says Caesar's army set up smithies, cast sling bullets and sent to Sicily for lead in 46 B.C.

Did Roman lead sling bullets melt in flight?

No. Lucretius, Virgil, Ovid and Seneca wrote that a lead bullet heats up in flight, and some said it melts. Thom Richardson measured slung bullets at about thirty metres a second for the Royal Armouries; by our own calculation the air warms the lead by less than one degree at that speed, and lead melts at about 327 degrees Celsius.

What happened at Burnswark Hill?

By the 1970s excavators had found 133 lead sling bullets there, and a later metal-detector survey led by John Reid found nearly 700 more signals. George Jobey argued in 1978 for a Roman training ground; Reid and Andrew Nicholson read it as a real assault, perhaps around A.D. 140. The evidence has not settled it.

Where it happened

  • Perugia (ancient Perusia), Italy (41 B.C.): Octavian besieged Lucius Antonius here in the winter of 41 to 40 B.C.; inscribed lead sling bullets from the siege were published by Zangemeister in 1885.
  • Burnswark Hill, Dumfriesshire, Scotland (A.D. 140): An Iron Age hillfort between two Roman camps; 133 lead sling bullets known by the 1970s and nearly 700 more detected since. Siege or training ground is debated; the date is a proposal.
  • Ascoli Piceno (ancient Asculum), Italy (89 B.C.): Besieged by Rome in the Social War, 90 to 89 B.C.; Zangemeister dug up 238 bullets here in 1877, two with writing. Home of the forger Giuseppe Vincenzini.
  • Ruspina (near Monastir), Tunisia (46 B.C.): Caesar's camp in 46 B.C., where his army cast sling bullets and asked Sicily for lead (Bellum Africum 20).

The great Latin dictionary of Lewis and Short calls the missile an acorn-shaped ball of lead or clay, hurled at the enemy. Many Roman ones look more like an almond or a lemon, pointed at both ends. These, found together in England, weigh from about 67 to 123 grams. They were cast. Molten lead went into a two-part mould. Any letters were cut backwards into the mould, so they came out raised and readable on the bullet. And the army cast them itself. In Africa, in 46 B.C., the unknown author of the African War writes that Caesar's army set up smithies, cast sling bullets, and sent to Sicily for lead. The weapon that threw them was simple. Two cords and a pouch. The late Roman writer Vegetius says slings were made of flax or of hair. Back to Perugia. Ancient Perusia, a town on a hill in Etruria. In 41 B.C., Mark Antony's brother Lucius, who was consul that year, and Antony's wife Fulvia took up arms against Octavian. Lucius ended up shut inside Perusia. The historian Appian, writing nearly two centuries later, says Octavian ringed the hill with a ditch 30 feet deep and 30 wide, and a wall with 1,500 wooden towers. Inside, the food ran out. Appian says the slaves were refused rations and ate grass, and the dead were buried in long trenches, so the enemy would not see how many there were. When the besieged tried to break out, he says, they fought from their siege towers with stones, arrows, and leaden balls. And the bullets from that siege talk back. In 1885, the German scholar Karl Zangemeister published the inscribed Latin sling bullets known in his day. Perusia has its own chapter, and its own photographic plates. Most, he argued, came from Octavian's side. Some carry a commander: CAESAR IMP, Caesar, general. That is Octavian, who used his adoptive father's name. Some name a legion and an officer. L XII. SCAEVA PR PIL: legion 12, Scaeva, chief centurion. And some carry a message. One reads: ESVREIS ET ME CELAS. You're starving, and you're hiding it from me. Another was scratched by hand into the finished bullet. Bald Lucius Antonius, you're done for, by Gaius Caesar's victory. Then there are the ones I can't read out. One aims at Fulvia's body. One, from Lucius's side, aims at Octavian's backside. Parts of those words are modern restorations, but the meaning is plain. And it wasn't only the soldiers. The poet Martial, more than a century later, quotes six verses that he says Augustus himself wrote about Fulvia. They are just as crude. Suetonius writes that some say three hundred prisoners were picked out and sacrificed, on the Ides of March, at an altar to the deified Julius. Cassius Dio tells it too, as a story that goes around. Appian and Velleius, who both describe the end of the siege, don't mention it. Zangemeister linked the eleventh legion's bullet, DIVOM IVLIVM, to that story. But the bullet only names the legion and the god. Whether it called for revenge, and whether the sacrifice happened at all, we don't know. There's a catch with inscribed bullets. Collectors wanted them. And where there's a market, there's a forger. From about 1860, a man in Ascoli, Giuseppe Vincenzini, took genuine, blank ancient bullets and hammered them into carved wooden dies, putting new words into old lead. A French scholar published more than 600 of them as genuine. In 1877, Vincenzini confessed to Zangemeister, in front of two witnesses. Zangemeister also records a boast. Vincenzini once bought an inscribed bullet from a countryman, sure it was ancient. Later he realised the inscription was his own work. How do you tell? A real inscription was cast with the bullet. The forger's letters were struck into lead that had already corroded, with sharp edges and angular round letters. And real bullets were mostly blank. At Ascoli, besieged by Rome in the Social War, Zangemeister and a local colleague dug for three days in 1877. 238 bullets. 2 had writing. So how good was a lead bullet? The Romans had an odd idea about that. Lucretius, Virgil, Ovid and Seneca all write that a lead bullet heats up in flight. Some say it melts. It doesn't. In tests for the Royal Armouries in the 1990s, Thom Richardson measured slung bullets at about 30 metres a second. At that speed, by our own calculation, the air warms the lead by less than 1 degree. Lead melts at about 327 °C. What lead did do was fly further. In the same tests, 40-gram lead bullets went about 145 metres on average. Stones, about 80 to 90. Richardson himself warns that he never learned the sling in a culture that used it. Skilled slingers may have done better. Or the old claims of range may be exaggerated. And when one hit? The Roman writer Celsus, in the first century A.D., explains what to do with a lead bullet that has broken the skin and lodged inside. Open the wound wider, he says, and draw it out with forceps, the way it went in. Now, a hill in Scotland. Burnswark, in Dumfriesshire, north of the line of Hadrian's Wall. A flat-topped hill, ringed by the ramparts of an Iron Age fort. And on its slopes, two Roman camps. One to the south, one to the north. In 1898, excavators found 67 lead sling bullets here. 20 lay at one gateway of the hillfort, 12 at another. By the 1970s the count stood at 133. The archaeologist George Jobey called that unique in Britain. More recently, a team led by John Reid swept the hill with metal detectors. They found nearly 700 more signals that looked like lead sling bullets, most of them along the southern rampart. Reid traces the barrage along about half a kilometre of it. In two test trenches in 2015, 17 of the 18 lead signals turned out to be Roman bullets. Some of the bullets are small, many under 20 grams, with a hole drilled about 5 millimetres wide. When the team slung copies, they buzzed in flight, Reid writes, like an agitated wasp. He thinks they were meant to frighten. That's his team's hypothesis. And Richardson notes that a good cast whirrs anyway, with any kind of missile. So what happened at Burnswark? The early excavators saw a siege. In 1978, George Jobey argued for a training ground. A field school, where the army practised building camps and attacking with slings, bows and artillery. A key point: the rampart was already ruined when the bullets fell. Reid's team confirmed that. Jobey had one quibble with his own theory. There was, he wrote, some laxity in the retrieval of re-usable ammunition. Reid and his colleague Andrew Nicholson read it the other way. A barrage this big looks to them like a real assault, a show of force, perhaps at the start of the Roman advance into Scotland around A.D. 140. The title of their own paper ends with a question mark. Siege or firing range? The evidence has not settled it. So, what do we really have? Thousands of real bullets, most of them blank. A small share with words: commanders, legions, jokes and threats. Texts that say the army cast them, surgeons cut them out, and poets thought they melted. What we don't have: how many Roman soldiers carried a sling, or the full story of what happened at Burnswark. So that's the Roman sling bullet. A lump of lead you could close your hand around. And now and then, a message. Three questions for you. Burnswark: a real assault, or a firing range with sloppy clean-up? The drilled bullets: made to frighten, or is the buzz just a side effect? And what should I take apart next?
